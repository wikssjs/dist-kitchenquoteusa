Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, the rules aren’t just paperwork—they affect scheduling, inspections, and who can legally touch certain parts of your kitchen. For work over $500, contractors performing home improvement must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (see hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For kitchen electrical work, any circuit installation or modification must be completed by, or under the supervision of, a licensed PA electrician (verify at dli.pa.gov). For plumbing, sink hookups, dishwasher connections, and any relocation of plumbing lines must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required in most PA municipalities when your project involves plumbing rerouting, adding new electrical circuits, or removing/moving walls. Cosmetic work—like painting, hardware swaps, or cabinet refacing that doesn’t disturb wall systems—typically doesn’t trigger the same permit pathway. If you’re in a pre-1980 New Brighton home, plan for lead paint considerations: pre-1978 homes may have lead-based paint on kitchen surfaces. If contractors disturb lead paint, they should use EPA Lead-Safe RRP practices, and relevant certification should be verified (check at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov) before any gut remodel where coatings may be impacted.

Step-by-step, here’s how homeowners in New Brighton should verify credentials: (1) ask for the contractor’s HIC registration details and confirm them at hic.attorneygeneral.gov; (2) request proof of licensed electrical/plumbing subcontractors and verify credentials at dli.pa.gov; (3) request a certificate of insurance (general liability) and workers’ compensation proof; (4) ensure the contract includes itemized work descriptions. Under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, contracts over $500 must be in writing and include a 3-day right of cancellation—request that cancellation notice and keep it as part of your signed paperwork.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in New Brighton?

In New Brighton, the choice between cabinet refacing and replacement is especially important because 93% of local housing stock was built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). Many Beaver County kitchens have dated face frames and hardware, but the underlying cabinet boxes can still be square and structurally sound—assuming the interior isn’t water-damaged and the face is firmly attached. That’s where refacing often fits: cabinet refacing replaces only the door and drawer fronts plus veneer, while keeping existing boxes. Typically, refacing costs about 40–60% less than full replacement when the box condition is good.

Refacing may not be the best move when you’re dealing with swelling from past leaks, cabinets that are out of level, or cases where prior DIY work left gaps that can’t be corrected cleanly without full replacement. It’s also not ideal if your layout is “almost right” but not functional—like needing different drawer configurations, wider pantries, or changing where storage goes. In those scenarios, replacement (stock, semi-custom, or custom) gives you the option to redesign for how you actually use the kitchen.

Here’s a concrete budget example from the New Brighton market: if your current boxes are in good shape, refacing might land in the $2,500–$6,000 range. If the boxes are compromised and start pulling away from the wall, doing refacing first can cost more overall because you end up replacing anyway—often pushing you toward replacement budgets like the $6,000–$30,000 band for stock or semi-custom replacement. In short: refacing is the efficient option when boxes are truly salvageable; replacement is the safer bet when the box system isn’t.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in New Brighton

Choosing the right contractor in New Brighton means verifying licensing and credentials up front—before you sign, before you pay, and before materials are ordered. Start by confirming Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for the contractor responsible for the job (hic.attorneygeneral.gov for the HIC registration). For trades, verify electrical work credentials through PA’s Department of Labor & Industry resources (dli.pa.gov) and confirm that plumbing is handled by or under a PA-licensed plumber. If a contractor can’t clearly identify who is licensed for each scope (electrical vs plumbing vs general construction), that’s a warning sign.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes. You want a breakdown by labor and materials—cabinet line items, countertop fabrication and install, flooring prep, electrical/plumbing allowances, and appliance hookup—not a single lump sum. Review what’s excluded: demolition disposal, drywall repair, subfloor replacement, electrical service upgrades, permitting, and any lead-paint preparation steps for older kitchens.

Ask about warranties: workmanship warranty length (and what “workmanship” covers), plus material/equipment warranties for cabinets, countertops, and appliances. Then discuss payment schedule terms: in general, never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and use a holdback until punch list completion. Finally, get the schedule in writing—arrival estimate, cabinet/countertop lead-time assumptions, and a completion window.
