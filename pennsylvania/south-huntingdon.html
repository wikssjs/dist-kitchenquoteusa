Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, your kitchen renovation triggers licensing and permitting rules based on what work is being performed—not just how much it costs. For work over $500, Pennsylvania requires contractors to be properly registered under the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) rules (check hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For kitchen electrical changes—like adding a circuit, moving outlets, or modifying the panel—a licensed PA electrician must install or modify circuits; verify status through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing work (sink and dishwasher hookups, or relocating plumbing), it must be done by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required when you reroute plumbing, add new electrical circuits, or remove/move walls. Cosmetic tasks—such as paint, hardware changes, and cabinet refacing that does not disturb electrical/plumbing or structural components—typically do not require permits.

Lead paint is also critical in older South Huntingdon homes. If the home was built before 1978, lead-based paint may be present on kitchen surfaces. Contractors disturbing lead paint surfaces are expected to use EPA Lead-Safe RRP practices; confirm certification using the EPA’s certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring for a gut remodel in an older home.

Finally, the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract for home improvement work over $500 and includes a 3-day right of cancellation. For hiring safety, verify credentials step-by-step: (1) confirm the contractor’s HIC registration online, (2) request and review the electrician/plumber licenses (where applicable), (3) ask for a Certificate of Insurance showing general liability, and (4) obtain proof of workers’ compensation coverage. Any contractor who can’t provide those documents before starting work should be treated as a “stop and verify” situation.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in South Huntingdon?

With 73.1% of South Huntingdon’s housing stock built before 1980, many Westmoreland County homeowners are working with kitchens that are 30–50 years old. In a lot of those homes, the cabinet boxes are still solid, but the doors, finish, and dated hardware no longer match today’s look. Cabinet refacing—replacing door and drawer fronts and applying veneer while keeping the existing cabinet boxes—often costs 40–60% less than full replacement and keeps disruption lower than a full gut.

Refacing only works when the existing boxes are square, stable, and in acceptable condition. If the boxes are sagging, have damaged bottoms from prior leaks, or are out of level due to years of settling, replacement is usually the more durable and cost-effective path. Also, if you want to change pull-out storage, change door styles across corners, or fix non-standard measurements by doing a re-layout, refacing can’t solve those underlying footprint issues.

Here’s a practical example from South Huntingdon: if you spend around $2,500–$8,000 on refacing and your boxes are still true, you can often update the look with minimal demolition. But if your cabinets require filler-heavy modifications because the wall openings are not straight, you may end up paying for patches and adjustments anyway—at that point, it’s sometimes better to move into replacement territory (commonly $8,000–$35,000 for stock/semi-custom) to get consistent door reveals and proper support for countertops and new hardware.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in South Huntingdon

Start by verifying credentials. For Pennsylvania, confirm the contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov) for work over $500. For specialty trades, require proof of PA licensing: electrician status through dli.pa.gov and plumber licensing through the appropriate state resources (ask for license numbers). If the project will disturb pre-1978 surfaces, confirm whether they hold EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification for the work (verify via certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov).

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes. Avoid a single lump-sum number. You want a breakdown that separates labor vs. materials, shows allowances for cabinets and countertops, and lists scope for demolition, electrical, plumbing, and flooring. Read the exclusions: what happens if they find outdated wiring, undersized venting, or mixed/galvanized plumbing behind the walls? A good contractor spells out how change orders are handled and what approvals are required.

Then confirm warranty coverage. Ask for (1) the workmanship warranty length, (2) which manufacturer warranties apply to cabinets, countertops, and appliances, and (3) how service calls are scheduled after completion. Payment schedule matters: never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and hold a portion back until key punch-list items are complete. Request a realistic timeline in writing, including arrival date estimate and a completion window.
