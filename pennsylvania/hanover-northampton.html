Cabinets: reface, stock, semi-custom or custom

Cabinets are usually the biggest decision—and the most expensive line—in any Hanover kitchen renovation. Refacing means keeping your current cabinet boxes and replacing only the doors, drawer fronts, and visible surfaces. This saves money and keeps your kitchen functional while updating its look, but only works if your cabinet boxes are solid, square, and you’re happy with the existing layout. Refacing will not add storage or solve layout issues.

Replacing cabinets offers three classes: stock, semi-custom, and custom. Stock cabinets are quickest and cheapest, but only come in standard sizes, so expect visible filler strips if your kitchen has corners or non-standard runs—very likely in Northampton County’s older housing. Semi-custom cabinets allow some flexibility in dimensions and finishes, working well for the irregular room sizes common in pre-1980 homes across Hanover, Bethlehem, and Easton. Custom cabinets fit any space and finish, with costs and lead times to match.

Regardless of option, pay attention to construction quality: plywood boxes outlast particleboard, dovetail and full-extension drawers survive heavy use, and soft-close hardware is worth the small upgrade. Frameless styles offer more access, but require perfectly plumb walls—a challenge in older homes. The reality is that a large percentage of Hanover homes have quirks that push many renovations toward at least semi-custom.

Lead times, sequencing and why it takes longer

Homeowners in Hanover often underestimate the real-world duration of even a straightforward kitchen renovation. The sequence is more than demolition and rebuilding—every step has dependencies, especially cabinets and countertops, which form the critical path on nearly every job in Northampton County.

First comes design and measurement, followed immediately by ordering cabinets. Never wait until after demolition to order; most semi-custom cabinets here come with 6–12-week lead times, and stock can still be delayed in a tight Lehigh Valley labor market. Once ordered, demolition and rough mechanical work proceed, with any permit-triggered work inspected by the local code office. Cabinets are installed after walls are closed; only then can the countertop fabricator template for a perfect fit. This means an unavoidable dead period after cabinet install—usually 7–14 days—while counters are measured, cut, and finished. Nobody tells homeowners about this lost week, but it is universal.

Floors, backsplash, lighting and painting are sequenced last. The condensed labor pool in Northampton County sometimes increases finish times another week or two. Planning with this lag in mind, and insisting on early cabinet orders, is the single biggest way to keep your project on track.

Why the electrical line grew

One of the fastest ways for a Hanover kitchen budget to balloon is unanticipated electrical work—especially in older homes. Current code requires several dedicated small-appliance circuits, GFCI protection for any outlet near a countertop, strict outlet spacing (so no spot is more than 24 inches from a receptacle), and separate circuits for dishwasher, garbage disposal, and microwave. An electric range usually demands a 240-volt line and often its own circuit. Most kitchens in Bethlehem and Easton dating to before 1980 were built with only one or two circuits, sometimes ungrounded or with aluminum wiring in stretches from the 1960s and 70s.

Once walls are opened in a major remodel, updated wiring to code is not optional—inspections will require it, and it is unsafe to close up walls otherwise. Many homeowners begin projects expecting a simple rewire, but find they need a new subpanel or a service upgrade as well, especially where kitchen loads are higher from double ovens or pantries wired for extra lighting. This upgrade typically runs $6,000–$15,000 on top of the base renovation for full rewiring, new circuits, breakers, and GFCI/AFCI devices. For homes built after 1990, budget on the lower side, but almost no kitchen gets by without some new circuits in Hanover’s stock.

Defining your scope before you get quotes

Few things make more difference in kitchen renovation costs than scoping your project before asking for bids. In Hanover, the word “remodel” could mean a cosmetic update or it could mean gutting to the studs and moving structure—a fivefold jump in price and timeline. Here are the four most-used scope tiers:

  • Cosmetic refresh: Paint, replace hardware, install a new backsplash or lighting—nothing that opens walls or changes major components.
  • Pull and replace: Remove and reinstall cabinets and counters in the current configuration. Plumbing and electrical stay put, but all visible finishes are new.
  • Gut, same layout: Everything out to studs, with all new plumbing, electrical, insulation, and finishes—but the existing footprint remains.
  • Layout change: Walls, windows, doors, plumbing or wiring are moved; often needs engineering, permits, and more coordination.

The steepest cost cliff is between a gut remodel that keeps the same layout and one where features are moved. Once a sink or range moves, you cross over into permit, plan review, and more specialized subs—not to mention longer downtime and more dust. Decide your tier before you call a contractor. The most honest quote cannot be given until the scope is clear, and fishing for bids without it only leads to frustration all around.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

Permit requirements in Hanover are specific to scope. Simple updates—painting, replacing cabinet hardware, refacing, or swapping in a countertop with the plumbing left undisturbed—can typically be done permit-free. Any renovation adding new or moved electrical circuits, altering plumbing lines, moving a wall, or making structural changes does trigger a permit request—especially in municipalities like Bethlehem, where plan review is required for wall or major service changes. Bringing in a new range hood or converting to a gas cooktop might also push your project over the permit line, depending on mechanical and venting needs.

In Bethlehem, municipal code inspectors perform electrical inspections—not a third-party agency—so expect coordination with the Bureau of Code Enforcement. For renovations over $500, Pennsylvania law requires your contractor to be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor through the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov), with mandatory workers’ compensation coverage (verify at WCAIS). Skipping proper permits and registration can create major resale headaches, with unpermitted work delaying closings or requiring costly corrections—especially as buyers’ lawyers are thorough in Northampton County transactions.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Hanover

Should I choose plywood or particleboard cabinet boxes? Plywood cabinet boxes are widely considered the better investment for most Hanover kitchens. Plywood is stiffer, resists moisture better, and holds screws longer—big advantages if your renovations touch pre-1980 walls that may be out of square or subject to minor movement (common across Northampton County's older housing stock). Particleboard is less expensive and works fine for a cosmetic upgrade, but it can chip and deform if water sits in a leak or spill. Many reputable semi-custom cabinet lines let you pick either construction, with costs from $21,000–$31,500 installed for a typical kitchen. If you are staying long-term, spend the extra for plywood. If you are selling soon or your kitchen is dry and light-use, particleboard can work, but durability in Hanover's climate justifies plywood for most owners.
What adds the most resale value in a kitchen? In Hanover, where most buyers expect a functional, bright, and up-to-code kitchen, the highest returns come from well-designed cabinetry and durable countertops—especially if you update the lighting and appliance layout to current standards. Potential buyers here are often families seeking reliable systems over flashy gadgets, thanks to the high homeownership rate and typical income profile. Spending on semi-custom cabinets and a quality stone or composite top—together in the $21,000–$31,500 range—outpaces high-end appliances or exotic finishes in resale value. Avoid luxury appliances paired with basic cabinets, as that often signals a shortcut elsewhere; updated electrical, good layout, and a bright finish appeal more to Hanover buyers than any single designer fixture.
Should I reface or replace my kitchen cabinets? In Hanover, cabinet refacing is a solid choice if your existing cabinet boxes are sturdy, square, and the kitchen layout works for you. Refacing saves significant money—falling in the $7,400–$21,000 range—versus the cost and disruption of full replacement. It updates the exterior: new doors, drawer fronts, and often soft-close hardware. However, if you want to change the storage configuration, solve layout limitations (which are common in the many pre-1980 homes across Northampton County), or your boxes show water damage or structural issues, replacement is definitely worth considering. If your budget is tight and you’re satisfied with how things are laid out, refacing is hard to beat. Otherwise, don’t be afraid to invest in new cabinets.
How should renovation payments be scheduled? Pennsylvania law (specifically under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act) requires a written contract for kitchen renovations over $500, and it is standard in Hanover to break payments into deposit, milestone, and final phases. Most contractors will ask for a deposit—often capped at one-third of the total job, or enough to cover cabinet and material orders for projects in the $31,500–$126,500 band. Milestone payments typically cover rough-in completion or cabinet delivery. The final payment is due once all work passes inspection and the punch list is complete. Never pay in full upfront. All payments should be tied to documented progress and receipts for materials, and you always have a 3-day right of cancellation in Pennsylvania. Ask your contractor for exact milestone triggers in writing.
How much do new kitchen cabinets cost in Hanover? For a Hanover kitchen, replacing all cabinets with new stock or semi-custom units typically runs between $7,400–$31,500. That figure includes removal of the old cabinets, installation of the new ones, hardware, and reasonable patching. If your kitchen has non-standard dimensions, as is common in the pre-1940 housing near Bethlehem and Easton, you may find yourself at the higher end of this range or nudged into custom cabinetry. Refacing—just changing doors, fronts, and veneer—runs $7,400–$21,000. Quality, features like soft-close and full extension drawers, and chosen finish all shift the price. Avoid spending at the bottom of the band unless you plan a short-term sale.
What is a realistic contingency for a Hanover kitchen? In Hanover, contingency should not be treated as optional—especially since 34.3% of the area’s homes were built before 1980. A realistic figure is 10–15% of your total kitchen renovation budget. On a typical full gut remodel (let’s call it $68,500), that means setting aside roughly $7,000 to $10,000 for surprises discovered once walls are open. Common setbacks include outdated electrical, hidden plumbing issues, old framing that complicates cabinet install, or local code triggers that require more upgrades than planned. Skimping on this line risks costly delays and rushed decisions, but most Hanover projects spend at least half of this amount—better to keep it on hand and not need it than be caught short midstream.
How does Hanover's housing stock affect cabinet fit? Hanover’s homes—like much of Northampton County—include a high percentage of pre-war and mid-century houses with walls that aren’t square, floors that sometimes slope, and layouts different from today’s builder-grade kitchens. That means stock cabinets (which come only in fixed widths and heights) may not line up well, resulting in awkward filler strips or wasted space. In these kitchens, semi-custom cabinets—costing between $21,000–$31,500 installed—often become necessary, even for those who started with a “stock” budget. They let you adjust size and configuration to the true room dimensions, maximize available storage, and avoid the visible band-aids that hurt resale. If you live in a 1990s or newer build, stock may fit fine; but older Hanover kitchens often pay for custom sizing whether the owner intended it or not.
How long does a kitchen renovation take in Hanover? The real timeline for a kitchen renovation in Hanover is longer than most national averages suggest. For a gut remodel that keeps the layout the same, expect 6–10 weeks from start to final inspection. Adding layout changes—moving plumbing, walls, or major appliances—pushes timelines to 10–16 weeks, especially due to Northampton County’s tighter remodeling labor market and required plan reviews in places like Bethlehem. Critical-path delays most often come from semi-custom cabinet lead times (6–12 weeks) and the dead week after cabinet install while countertops are fabricated (seen even in projects at the $31,500–$126,500 range). Quick jobs, like countertop-only or refacing, can take less than two weeks, but once you open walls, add time for inspections, coordination, and the wider supply chain. Plan for a minimum of eight weeks if walls or services move.
What happens if previous work was unpermitted? Unpermitted work in Hanover can cause major obstacles at resale or future remodels. When you sell, buyers’ inspectors in Northampton County will note non-compliant kitchens—especially missing GFCIs, improper circuit counts, or altered walls. Municipalities like Bethlehem require permits and late-stage inspections for most electrical or significant layout changes. If work was done without the right paperwork, homeowners may need to prove code compliance by opening up walls or paying for “as-built” permits, which can exceed $4,200–$17,000 depending on what must be redone. Insurance claims can be denied for damages from unpermitted work, and bringing everything up to current code is required, not optional. Always verify registration and permits on new projects to avoid inheriting someone else’s liability.
