Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, most kitchen renovation contractors who do work over $500 must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work, any installation or modification to circuits must be done by a licensed PA electrician; you can verify credentials through dli.pa.gov. Plumbing work—such as sink and dishwasher hookups, garbage disposal connections, or relocating supply/drain lines—must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits depend on scope and municipality. In most PA municipalities, renovations that involve plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal typically require building permits and inspections. Cosmetic work such as cabinet refacing, painting, replacing hardware, and swapping out like-for-like fixtures usually does not trigger permits. However, even “minor” changes (like moving an outlet behind a new cabinet layout) can require electrical review if a circuit alteration is involved.

Lead paint is another key Exeter concern. Pre-1978 homes may contain lead-based paint on kitchen surfaces. If your project disturbs painted surfaces, contractors generally need EPA Lead-Safe RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) certification. Before hiring, verify certification at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Finally, under the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, you should receive a written contract for kitchen work over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation. When verifying a contractor, check: (1) HIC registration online, (2) PA electrician/plumber licensing where applicable, (3) Certificate of Insurance for general liability and (4) proof of workers’ compensation coverage.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Exeter?

With 43.8% of homes in Exeter built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), a lot of kitchens still have cabinet boxes that are structurally fine but visually tired—45-minute-scratchy hinges, sun-faded finishes, and outdated door styles. That’s why cabinet refacing is so common in Berks County: you keep the existing cabinet boxes and replace only door and drawer fronts (plus veneer and hardware). In many homes, refacing costs 40–60% less than full replacement because you’re reducing demolition, disposal, and the time it takes to install fully new frames.

Refacing is cost-effective only when the existing boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. If you have water-damaged bottoms, significant sagging, major gaps, or cabinets that don’t sit level, the “savings” can disappear quickly—because repairs, filler corrections, and rework become more likely. In Exeter’s older housing stock, it’s not unusual to discover the wall isn’t flat after the countertop comes off, and that can affect how doors align and close. In those cases, replacement may be the better long-term fix even if it’s more expensive upfront.

Here’s a practical example: if your current boxes are sound, refacing might land around $1,600–$8,000, and then you can spend the remaining budget on quartz and better lighting. But if your cabinets have been patched multiple times or are out of square, moving up to stock replacement (often $8,000–$45,000) can justify the higher spend because it reduces hidden labor—like rebuilding inconsistent mounting surfaces and correcting repeated alignment problems.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Exeter

Choosing the right contractor in Exeter starts with credentials and proof—because kitchen renovations mix carpentry, finish work, and often electrical and plumbing changes. For PA licensing, verify the right professional for the right scope: check your contractor’s HIC registration if the job is over $500 (hic.attorneygeneral.gov), and confirm electrical work is performed by a licensed PA electrician using dli.pa.gov. If plumbing is involved, ensure the plumber is licensed and either working directly or under supervision for the portions that require it.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes—not a single lump sum. The best quotes break out labor and materials by line item (demo, carpentry, cabinet install, countertop fabrication and setting, flooring prep, electrical/plumbing coordination, and trim/paint). Read the scope closely for inclusions and exclusions: who handles site protection, disposal, floor leveling, underlayment, and any patching? Also ask whether insurance claim documentation is handled if you’re doing work due to a water event or hidden damage.

Warranties and payment schedules matter. A reputable kitchen team provides a workmanship warranty (and separately confirms equipment/material warranties for appliances, countertops, cabinets, and flooring). Avoid large upfront payments; a good rule is never more than 10–15% upfront, and use a holdback until key milestones are complete. Finally, get a written arrival estimate and completion window, including countertop fabrication lead times—especially for quartz and semi-custom cabinetry, which can extend schedules in 2026.
