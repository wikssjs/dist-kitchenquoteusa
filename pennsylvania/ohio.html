What a kitchen remodel costs in Ohio

Kitchen remodel budgets in Ohio range more widely than most homeowners expect, with costs shaped by both home value and the prevalence of single-family ownership. With a homeownership rate of 86.3% and a median household income of $160,365 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), most owners in Ohio are making renovation plans for homes they will keep, not just flip. That matters: when you’re setting the budget, long-term durability and layout matter more than short-term sticker shock.

The age of your house drives both need and cost. Many houses in the region trace their layouts to 1940 or earlier, when kitchens were smaller and closed off. Updating one of these usually means gutting, moving walls, and rewiring. Newer, post-1980 homes typically have layouts that can handle finish upgrades, so gutting is less common unless storage or flow is poor.

The biggest risk to your budget comes from “scope creep”—comparing prices for a finish-only cabinet swap and then wanting a full gut once you see what’s possible. Always align the price band to the scope. Below is what you can expect for a typical Ohio kitchen, keeping in mind that quality of materials and any change to the layout are bigger cost drivers than square footage.

Quartz, granite, laminate or butcher block

Countertop decisions in Ohio kitchens come down to use, not just looks. Quartz is engineered, non-porous, and stands up to stains and casual cleaning, but it’s less tolerant of hot pans — a misplaced roaster can scorch it. Granite is the standard for natural stone, uniquely patterned and heat-tough, though it needs sealing every few years to keep oil and wine from leaving marks. Granite seams may be more noticeable, especially with bold patterns.

Laminate has improved enormously in the last decade. It resists everyday spills, hides seams better than most stone, and is easy to repair or replace. For rental units or a modest kitchen, laminate is honest value, not a compromise. Butcher block has warmth nothing else matches but needs regular oiling and careful cleaning to prevent stains. Over time, cuts and nicks add character or, depending on your taste, become a nuisance.

Solid surface options (like Corian) are repairable — a gouge or crack can be sanded out, and seams are less obvious than with quartz or granite. They’re slightly more sensitive to heat than stone. If you’re after a consistent, easy-to-clean finish and don’t set hot pans directly on the counter, solid surface is a solid middle ground.

On the fabrication side, prices jump most with edge profile, sink cutouts, and the number or position of seams. A template visit (digital or physical) produces a better fit than taking measurements only — expect fabricators to template for most solid-surface or stone jobs but not always for laminate. The more complex the cutouts — for undermount sinks, cooktops, or pop-up outlets — the steeper the quote. Always ask if the quoted price covers final field measurements, so there are no surprises.

Allowances, exclusions and change orders

Comparing kitchen quotes in Ohio is harder than it should be, largely because of allowances. An allowance is a placeholder amount for something you haven’t picked yet — think backsplash tile, lighting, or appliances. Two bids can come in $5,000 apart with the same listed total, just because one builder’s tile allowance is a realistic $2,000 and the other’s, an impossible $500. Always check allowance lines before comparing bottom lines.

Insist that your quote names the actual cabinet brand and line, not just “quality cabinets.” Countertop lines should specify the exact material, thickness, edge details, and number of seams. For electrical, the quote should break out which circuits are being added or upgraded, not just say “as needed.”

All real contracts will lay out what happens if the crew finds unexpected trouble behind the walls — how extra work is priced and how you approve it. Change orders should always need your written sign-off before the contractor proceeds. A proper payment schedule ties payments to milestone completions (for example, after cabinets are set), not just calendar dates. Lien waivers from subcontractors at each payment keep your title clear. In Pennsylvania, contracts over $500 require a written agreement and you have a 3 business-day right of cancellation.

Cabinets: reface, stock, semi-custom or custom

Cabinet selection drives your kitchen budget more than any other decision. Refacing keeps your old cabinet boxes but gives you new doors, fronts, and a veneer skin — this only works if the boxes are square, solid, and laid out how you want. Refacing costs far less, but you can’t add storage or change the shape of your kitchen. If the floorplan isn’t what you want, or if the boxes are particleboard and sagging, you’re wasting money by trying to reface.

Stock cabinets are the fastest and often the cheapest route, but they’re built in fixed sizes. If your home has standard, post-1980 walls, they’ll generally fit. In older Ohio homes — especially those resembling pre-1940 Pittsburgh kitchens — walls are rarely plumb or sized to catalog dimensions, resulting in wasted inches or lots of filler strips. Semi-custom cabinets let you adjust sizes, finishes, and features for a middle ground. Full custom handles any angle or challenge, but ramps up both price and lead time.

Cabinet quality isn’t dictated by price tier. Look for plywood box construction if you can swing it; particleboard is less expensive, but won’t hold up as long in a busy kitchen. Dovetail drawer boxes and full-extension soft-close hardware are worth the extra up front for durability. Frameless (European-style) cabinets offer more interior space, while face-frame cabinets look traditional and are more forgiving of wall issues in older homes.

Surprises in an older Ohio home

Older homes in Ohio, especially those built before 1980, surprise even seasoned contractors once demolition starts. The most common headache is out-of-plumb walls and uneven floors — expect extra labor and shimming to get cabinets and counters level. It may look straight, but nothing in a pre-war house truly is.

Wiring is another culprit. Kitchens from earlier eras often have undersized or ungrounded wiring, sometimes with a single circuit shared by lights and outlets. Upgrading to today’s kitchen code (dedicated appliance circuits, GFCI protection) isn’t optional when the walls are open, and can add $3,500–$11,000. Plumbing snags include galvanized water lines, which clog and leak, or cast iron drains that are slow or corroded. Fixing can mean cutback and replacement at $2,000–$7,000, depending on how far it runs.

Insulation is often missing on exterior kitchen walls in pre-1980 stock. If you find it empty behind drywall, it pays to add new insulation while the wall is open. Previous owners’ unpermitted work is common and may not meet code, creating cost or inspection headaches. In homes built before 1978, any disruption of painted surfaces is subject to the federal EPA Lead RRP Rule — firms must be EPA-certified and may test painted surfaces before disturbing them.

In more recent built homes, expect aging laminate counters, wear at particleboard cabinet bases, or minimal circuit counts that fall short of modern appliance needs. Surprises still come up, but tend to be less about the unknowns behind the walls.

Circuits, GFCI and code compliance

If your Ohio kitchen is in an older house, nothing bursts the budget like the electrical. Current code calls for at least two dedicated 20-amp small appliance circuits, GFCI protection for all countertop outlets, dedicated circuits for the dishwasher, disposal, and microwave, and 240-volt service for an electric range. Countertop receptacles are supposed to be spaced so no point is more than 24 inches from a plug.

Older homes — especially those with a pre-1940 kitchen now being gutted — rarely meet this standard. You’re likely to find ungrounded, two-wire circuits, sometimes aluminum branch wiring, and one circuit serving lights, fridge, and outlets. The main panel often lacks spare slots. Once walls are opened, any new wiring must meet today’s code, which isn’t negotiable and quickly becomes a cost item not planned for at the start.

Even late-1990s kitchens, though seemingly modern, often lack enough circuits for today’s appliances, especially with the addition of an island or more countertop space.

Bringing electrical up to code in these conditions — new panel circuits, rewiring, and GFCI — typically runs $3,500–$11,000 on top of your main budget. Don’t skip this: it is both a safety issue and a resale red flag if ignored.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

In most of Ohio, you don’t need a permit for purely cosmetic changes — painting, updating hardware, refacing cabinets, or swapping a countertop in the same footprint. What changes things is touching electrical, plumbing, walls, or structure. In Pittsburgh (Allegheny County seat), a permit is typically required for any wall layout change, or relocation of plumbing or electrical lines during a kitchen job. Full remodels with layout changes usually go through a plan review at the Department of Permits, Licenses, and Inspections.

Cabinet replacement, with no change in layout, usually doesn’t require a building permit in most Allegheny County boroughs. But add or move outlets, switches, or plumbing — or touch structure — and you’re into permit territory. Mechanical work like a new hood may mean a permit as well.

Electrical inspections in Pittsburgh are performed under city authority, but in nearby suburbs, approved third-party inspection agencies may be the ones on site. This catches many owners by surprise, especially when selling a home later on — unpermitted work gets flagged and can delay or derail a sale.

Your contractor should be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov), and every contractor with employees must carry workers’ compensation insurance (check at WCAIS). Protect yourself at resale — always insist on permitted work for anything behind the walls.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Ohio

Why are kitchen quotes in Ohio so far apart? Bids for kitchen renovation in Ohio can be thousands apart for the same-sized kitchen, mostly due to differences in what's actually included. Some contractors use lowball “allowances” — placeholder amounts for things like tile or lighting that won’t cover what you’d actually pick. Others quote realistic figures up front. For example, one company’s bid may include a tile allowance of $500 while another allows $2,000 for the same line item, making direct comparison almost impossible until those details are clear. Labor costs, cabinet construction, finish level, and whether any hidden electrical or plumbing updates are included all move the number as well. Always go line by line and ask to see specific brands for cabinets, exact scope for electrical, and how unforeseen conditions are handled. Don’t pick your contractor by lowest price — the difference often shows up later as extra charges or change orders mid-project.
What does a full gut kitchen remodel cost in Ohio? For a full gut remodel in Ohio (removing everything down to the drywall and replacing cabinets, floors, appliances, and fixtures), expect a price band of $33,000–$132,000. A typical Ohio kitchen falls around $71,500, depending more on finish choices and appliance grade than square footage. If you’re planning to change the layout — moving walls or shifting plumbing and electrical — the cost will ride the top end. In older homes, budget extra for things you can’t see at the start, such as wiring that doesn’t meet current code, unlevel floors, or older plumbing. Owners in Ohio tend to invest more than landlords, reflecting the state’s high homeownership rate (86.3%) and median household income. Cutting corners on behind-the-wall systems is usually a false savings, especially if you intend to stay put.
Should I hire a general contractor or a kitchen firm? In Ohio and the greater Allegheny County region, both options exist, but the right choice depends on project complexity. For a straightforward cabinet swap or countertop replacement — something in the $4,400–$33,000 range — a specialized kitchen firm might move faster and know the products intimately. For any project involving walls moved, electrical upgrades, or permitting, a general contractor with experience handling multiple trades and pulling permits is the safer bet. Remember, in Pennsylvania a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration is required for jobs over $500. Vetting your GC’s paperwork and insurance is easier while you still have options — don’t let urgency rush you. In both cases, clear contracts and line-item pricing are more important than what the sign on the truck says.
How do I verify a kitchen contractor in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, every contractor bidding work over $500 must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) with the Office of Attorney General. To check your contractor, visit hic.attorneygeneral.gov — you can look up company and owner names as well as verify standing. For jobs involving employees beyond the owner, Pennsylvania law requires workers’ compensation coverage. You can confirm coverage at WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov), which lists all active policies. Before you sign, insist on written proof of coverage and check references within your city or borough. Registration alone doesn’t guarantee quality, but an unregistered or uninsured contractor in Ohio or Pennsylvania is a gamble. Make sure you get a written contract for jobs above $500 — it’s the law and your best protection against disputes.
Should I renovate before selling or let the buyer do it? In the Ohio and Allegheny County market, this comes down to your home’s age and condition. If your kitchen is functional but outdated, you may not recoup a full-gut investment ($33,000–$132,000) before selling, especially if buyers in your area expect to do some work themselves. Modest cosmetic upgrades or even just a cabinet refacing ($7,700–$22,000) can help attract offers without over-investing. In neighborhoods with strong homeownership and high median income, buyers are more likely to want a say in design — doing too much can work against you. However, homes with obvious layout or safety issues (like missing GFCI outlets or failing cabinets) often stall or lose value at inspection. Always factor in your local buyer pool and talk to a real estate agent before making a big spend.
Why is there a gap after cabinets are installed? In both older and new Ohio homes, it’s common to see small gaps between new cabinets and uneven walls or ceilings, especially in homes built before drywall was the norm. Original plaster, settling over decades, and shifts in the framing all contribute. Installers will often use molding or scribe strips to close these gaps after the main cabinet boxes are installed. With stock cabinets ($7,700–$33,000), small filler strips are almost always needed to make up for non-standard wall dimensions. In high-end or custom jobs, woodworkers may scribe-to-fit, but even then, nothing makes century-old framing perfectly straight. The important thing is to understand that these gaps are a normal part of older-home renovations — you’re not getting a “bad install” just because the walls are wavy.
Should I reface or replace my kitchen cabinets? Refacing saves considerable money and time, coming in between $7,700–$22,000, compared to replacing cabinets outright at $7,700–$33,000 or more for semi-custom. Refacing works only if your existing cabinet boxes are solid, square, and in the layout you want — it does not allow for new storage, moving cabinets, or changing a dated footprint. If your home’s boxes are particleboard, water-damaged, or don’t offer the flow modern kitchens demand (common in older Ohio homes), replacing is the right answer. On the other hand, for more recent homes where the layout is satisfactory and the basic cabinet frame is in good shape, refacing is a legitimate solution — not just a shortcut. Think about how long you intend to stay: if it’s a long-term play and you want a better layout, full replacement pays off.
What countertop material is best for a family kitchen? In Ohio homes with active families, quartz tends to be the workhorse — it offers a non-porous finish, resists stains, and cleans easily. The main caveat is heat; set a hot pan down and it can scorch, so always use trivets. Granite gives more heat resistance but needs periodic resealing — not a big chore, but something to keep on the list. Laminate is durable and easy to replace if it ever gets damaged, and shouldn’t be dismissed for busy homes — a new install typically runs $4,400–$17,500. Butcher block is loved for warmth but is fussy on maintenance (regular oiling, no standing water). For most, the decision comes down to balancing price, maintenance willingness, and the realities of a household with children, pets, and heavy traffic. No option is right for everyone — think years out, not just this season.
Is lead paint a concern in Ohio kitchen renovations? In Ohio, as in Pennsylvania, lead paint is a concern for any home built before 1978. Federal rules (EPA Lead RRP Rule) apply — before disturbing painted surfaces in these older homes, a certified renovation firm should test and, if needed, contain all dust and debris. This adds some cost to demolition, especially for full gut jobs ($33,000–$132,000). The law isn’t just a formality; lead dust is a real hazard, especially if you have children or work done in the colder months with windows closed. Clarify your contractor’s lead-safe process and confirm their EPA certification. If your home was built after 1978, this isn’t an issue, but in established neighborhoods—particularly those with a lot of mid-century or pre-war homes—it’s something you shouldn’t skip or ignore. Always ask early, as this determines who you can hire and how the job will be run.
