Cabinets are the critical path

In Lewisburg and the rest of Union County, cabinets are the part of a kitchen renovation that set the pace for everything else. Here’s what the sequence actually looks like: First, you work out your kitchen layout and make final measurements. Only then can you order cabinets. Do not rip out your kitchen until your cabinet order has a confirmed ship date—lead times have stretched, and travel surcharges from the bigger metro areas mean fewer choices for rush delivery.

After cabinets are ordered, demolition begins. Walls and floors come out, any structural or rough-in changes for plumbing and electrical are done, and the site is cleaned up for inspection. Once framing, wiring, and rough plumbing pass muster, the new cabinets go in. At this point, the job often slows down and homeowners get frustrated, because countertops can’t be templated from a plan. Stone or other tops need to be measured in person, after cabinets are fully installed and secured. There’s always an unavoidable gap—usually a week or more—between when the cabinets go in and when the countertop can even be measured, let alone fabricated and installed. Expect a real dead period here; nobody tells you this up front, but it’s baked into the process regardless of contractor.

In our market, with a thinner trade labor pool and longer lead times, this waiting period can stretch out more than homeowners want. Order cabinets before demolition, not after, or risk stretching your project well past your target.

Circuits, GFCI and code compliance

Electrical code upgrades catch more Lewisburg homeowners by surprise than anything else. Current Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC) expects your kitchen to have GFCI-protected countertop outlets, dedicated small-appliance circuits, a 240V line for an electric range, and individual circuits for the dishwasher, disposal, and microwave. Receptacle spacing and count is rigidly enforced, and once you’ve opened the walls, any new or altered wiring has to be brought up to today’s code—no grandfathering for “just replacing cabinets.”

The local housing stock is mostly from 1950–1980, so it’s normal to find a kitchen with only one or two ungrounded circuits, a fuse box or a panel with no room to expand, and sometimes even aluminum branch wiring in late 1960s or early 1970s homes. Bringing these up to code almost always means adding circuits—sometimes a new subpanel or a full service upgrade. Inspectors in Union County enforce these requirements strictly, especially if electrical work is in the remodel scope. Even if your kitchen is a bit newer, late-1990s builds often lack dedicated microwave or disposal circuits and fall short on GFCI protection.

For most older Lewisburg homes, a full electrical upgrade during a kitchen renovation will fall between $4,600–$18,500, depending on how extensive the rewiring and panel upgrades need to be. It’s rarely money you want to spend, but it’s required if you touch the bones.

Renovating to live in versus renovating to sell

No matter what anyone else tells you, a full kitchen remodel in Lewisburg makes your house easier to sell—but rarely returns every dollar you spend. What actually works in Union County is matching the project to the reason you’re renovating. If you plan to sell soon, focus on neutral finishes, spend at the mid-range, and avoid moving walls or utilities. That’s how you present a move-in ready kitchen without sinking more than you’ll get back. A typical cabinet replacement or modest full remodel—somewhere in the $27,500–$59,500 range—matches most of the Lewisburg housing stock, and that is what buyers expect to see.

If you’re renovating to actually live in your home, your spend should be about function and daily use, not some unknown future buyer. This is where personal taste and upgraded materials can make sense, but know that kitchens priced much above the neighborhood set the recovery ceiling. With a median household income of $42,302 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), the odds of recouping a high-end renovation are low. Lewisburg isn’t a market where $100,000 kitchen investments regularly trade hands. Homeowners here are practical—don’t over-improve for resale, but do invest for your own use if you plan to stay for a decade or more.

Permits and inspections for a Lewisburg kitchen

For most kitchen projects in Lewisburg, local permit requirements depend on what you touch. Cosmetic upgrades—painting cabinets, replacing hardware, even putting down new counters on old boxes—do not typically require a permit. Once you move or add electrical circuits, relocate plumbing, or plan to open a wall (especially a load-bearing one), a building permit from the Lewisburg Borough Codes Office is generally required. Structural changes always trigger a formal plan review. Replacing cabinets only (no utility moves) is commonly done without a permit, both within Lewisburg and East Buffalo Township.

Inspections are part of the permit process. In the borough, the codes office manages these, but in surrounding Union County townships, third-party agencies—such as Middle Department Inspection Agency—handle electrical inspections. This sometimes surprises homeowners expecting a municipal inspector only. When a permit is required, you need a written contract if your project exceeds $500, under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. Your contractor must be registered with the Office of Attorney General—verify their number at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. When employees are working, Pennsylvania workers’ compensation coverage is mandatory, and you can check status through the WCAIS system.

Skipping permits is a risk. Unpermitted work often gets flagged in future home sales, potentially blocking a closing or lowering your sale price. Get it done right and documented from the start.

What's behind the walls in a Lewisburg kitchen

Almost nine out of ten homes in Lewisburg were built before 1980. Once you start demolition, a few surprises are common. Out-of-plumb walls and out-of-level floors show up often—a carpenter can shim cabinets, but it’s extra labor, adding $1,000–$3,700 or more depending on the fixes. You may find old wiring—two-wire, sometimes ungrounded, or aluminum branch circuits. Upgrading wiring and adding new circuits to code can cost $4,600–$18,500 in a typical project. Galvanized plumbing and cast iron drains are standard pre-1970s; replacing aging drains and supply lines ranges from a patch job to a full re-pipe bill.

No insulation on exterior walls is routine in older kitchens, especially around the window bays—expect to add batt insulation during an open-wall phase. Evidence of prior unpermitted work, like ad hoc venting or nonstandard framing, is another local reality. In homes built before 1978, the federal EPA Lead RRP rule applies. If you’re disturbing painted surfaces, your contractor must be certified, and lead-safe practices and possible testing are required before work starts. Asbestos-containing flooring or mastic is sometimes uncovered in mid-century homes, so don’t pull vinyl yourself until it’s evaluated.

None of these are surprises unique to Lewisburg, but knowing to budget for them—and asking about these up front—helps avoid half the project stress.

Living through a kitchen renovation

Living without a kitchen, even for a few weeks, is harder than most Lewisburg homeowners expect. Setting up a temporary space is essential: move your fridge to the hallway or dining room, set up a microwave and a kettle on a folding table, and use a bathroom or laundry sink for the basics—pots won’t fit, but it’s better than nothing. Switching to paper plates and single-use cutlery keeps the pileup down. Be honest about the food budget: eating out or ordering in for six to eight weeks can easily surpass your flooring allowance, landing in the $4,600–$9,200 range if you’re not careful.

Dust containment needs to be planned. Contractors should install plastic sheeting and zip walls to keep debris out of adjoining rooms, especially if you’re living in. The dumpster and materials will need driveway or yard space; plan for the disruption, and make sure pets are out of reach—doors may be open for long stretches. For the demolition phase, which is the loudest and dustiest week, moving out temporarily is smart if you have young kids or asthma. Communicate with your contractor about daily routines so staging and cleanup make less of a mess on your side of the plastic.

Budget for inconvenience as seriously as you do dollars. In a Union County winter, even a few days eating out adds up quick.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Lewisburg

Is lead paint a concern in Lewisburg kitchen renovations?

Yes—if your home in Lewisburg was built before 1978, there’s a real chance that existing paint contains lead. The federal EPA Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule (RRP) applies to all pre-1978 homes in Lewisburg and anywhere else in the U.S. Contractors working on these kitchens must be certified in lead-safe practices, and will typically do a test before disturbing any painted surface. Most homes here—about 88.1% per census data—fall into this risk window. Lead-safe work means extra dust control, sealing off work zones, and possibly extra cleanup costs. This doesn’t usually add a huge amount to your project price, but you do want to verify that your contractor is qualified. For context, minor lead-safe setup may add a few hundred dollars to a job, while more involved abatement can go higher. If in doubt, ask for credentials and references before signing on. You don’t want to cut corners on safety—especially if kids or pregnant women will be present during the renovation.

What is the cheapest way to update a Lewisburg kitchen?

If you’re watching costs closely, the least expensive way to update most Lewisburg kitchens is cabinet refacing or painting, paired with hardware and a lighting refresh. You can often keep your existing layout and boxes intact and spend between $6,400–$18,500 on refacing, which gives you new doors and drawer fronts with a fraction of the mess and downtime. Painting cabinets and changing the pulls is even less—often a DIY job if you’re handy and careful about prep. If your counters are in rough shape, a basic laminate or entry-level stone replacement can make a huge visual difference for $3,700–$8,300. Focus on visible surfaces and skip any layout or plumbing moves, which drive up costs fast. Most importantly, keep your spending in line with typical home values in Lewisburg so you don’t over-improve for the area.

Is laminate countertop a bad choice?

Laminate countertops can still be a solid choice for many Lewisburg homes, especially older houses that aren’t likely to justify premium materials at resale. Modern laminates are much better than the ones from the 1970s and 80s—more colors, better edge details, and easier care. If your budget is tight, laminate allows you to put your dollars into other upgrades, such as better lighting or appliances. A typical laminate top costs at the low end of the local band, around $3,700–$6,400 installed. It’s not the “lifetime” material that quartz or stone is, but if you’re updating a rental or prepping a mid-range house for sale, it does the job. If you’re worried about resale, most buyers expect stone—or at least solid-surface—in newer kitchens, but in an older Lewisburg house, laminate is still practical and not out of place.

Do I need a permit for a kitchen renovation in Lewisburg?

In Lewisburg, whether you need a permit depends on the type of work planned. If you’re just swapping out cabinet doors, repainting, or replacing a countertop in the same footprint, no permit is typically required. But as soon as you move plumbing, change electrical circuits, or touch structural walls, you’ll need a permit from the Lewisburg Borough Codes Office. This includes relocating appliances that require running new lines. For structural work, expect a formal plan review. In surrounding Union County townships, rules are similar, but check with your township office to confirm. Electrical inspection is sometimes done by third-party agencies. For any contract over $500, state law requires a written agreement, and your contractor has to be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Skipping the permit process is likely to cause headaches at resale—unpermitted work can stall or sink a closing, so it’s not worth the risk.

What is a change order and how should it work?

A change order is a written amendment to your remodeling contract when you or your contractor want to add, remove, or adjust scope after work starts. In a Lewisburg kitchen, changes come up often—opening a wall might reveal old wiring that needs full replacement, or you might decide mid-project to add undercabinet lighting. Under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, change orders must be documented in writing, with a clear price or price range ($1,000–$18,500 is a common range for “hidden” corrections like electrical). You should get a written description, the new cost, and your signature before the extra work starts—not after. This protects both you and the contractor. Never rely on “handshake” agreements or verbal changes for anything in this state—get it in writing, and make sure both parties have a copy. It’s the best way to avoid disputes or surprise billing at the end of the job.

What adds the most resale value in a kitchen?

In Lewisburg, mid-range kitchen upgrades generally add more resale value dollar-for-dollar than high-end renovations. Buyers in Lewisburg want clean, functional kitchens with neutral finishes, good lighting, and modern appliances. Replacing cabinets with semi-custom or stock lines and upgrading to solid-surface or stone countertops often brings the house up to market standard—the typical combined spend in our market is between $8,300–$27,500. Changing layouts, removing walls, and installing high-end finishes rarely pay back the full investment locally. Stick to neutral colors, keep the layout familiar, add energy-efficient lighting, and replace aging appliances with reliable mid-tier brands for the best value recovery. Remember, Lewisburg’s typical home buyer is budget-conscious, and over-improving above the local median doesn’t yield a bigger return at resale.

How far ahead should I book a contractor in Lewisburg?

Trade labor in Union County, especially in finish trades like kitchen cabinetry and flooring, is tighter than in Harrisburg or Williamsport. Expect to wait—most reputable contractors are booked three to six months out for larger renovations. If you want a summer project, start planning in winter or early spring. Some contractor schedules stretch further because travel surcharges result in more jobs lined up per trip. For simple scope—cabinet refacing or countertop replacement in the $3,700–$14,500 range—you might find a slot sooner, but full kitchen remodels take longer to place. Book early, get on the calendar, and finalize your design decisions so you’re ready to order cabinets and materials as soon as your turn comes up. Last-minute or emergency jobs rarely get priority and could cost more just to schedule.

What is a realistic contingency for a Lewisburg kitchen?

Old houses bring surprises, and in Lewisburg—with 88.1% of homes built before 1980—you need to budget for the unexpected. Most contractors recommend a 10–20% contingency on top of quoted project costs. For a mid-range remodel at $27,500–$59,500, plan on holding aside at least $2,700–$11,000 extra to cover hidden problems like electrical upgrades, uneven floors, plumbing fixes, or asbestos flooring. Nearly every project on a pre-1980 house here reveals something once the walls are open. If you come in under budget, great; if not, you’ll have the funds on hand to make good decisions without stress. Avoid spending up to your absolute ceiling at the start—give yourself room to adapt to what you find.

How much more does moving the sink and range cost?

Moving the sink, range, or any appliance that needs new plumbing or electrical runs usually pushes your kitchen renovation into a higher price and permit bracket. In Lewisburg, simply shifting a sink or range a few feet can raise project costs by several thousand dollars—running new supply and drain lines, new circuits, patching floors and walls, and permitting all add up. For a full kitchen remodel with layout changes, plan on a total project cost of $59,500–$110,500, compared to keeping the same layout at the lower end of the full gut range. Always get an itemized quote and check that any contractor pulling new lines is registered and that permits are logged—especially if you may sell later. It’s almost never worth moving utilities just for a look; do it for daily function, not for resale.
