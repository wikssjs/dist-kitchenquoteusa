Countertop materials compared

When choosing countertops for your Jeannette kitchen, think about how you actually use the space, not just what looks best under showroom lights. Quartz is engineered and non-porous, so it shrugs off coffee and wine stains, but it can be damaged by direct heat—placing a hot pan too soon can cause marks. Granite is natural stone and can stand up to high temperatures, but it needs periodic sealing to avoid staining and can chip on corners. Seams in both quartz and granite are visible, though a skilled fabricator can usually minimize their appearance.

Laminate countertops are much better than their reputation, especially for modest kitchens or rentals. They resist most stains and scratches and are easy to clean. Newer laminates mimic stone well and are affordable. If the budget tops out near the lower band—around $3,600–$4,500—laminate is often the right call and not a compromise. Butcher block is warm and adds charm, but it requires frequent oiling and is vulnerable to stains and cuts. Solid surface (like Corian) is completely repairable; scratches and burns can be sanded out, making it practical for families.

Fabrication factors into price: a premium edge profile, extra cutouts, and keeping seams out of sight raise the cost. The template visit is critical—a physical template allows a precise fit, while working off rough measurements leads to mistakes. For all stone and solid surface tops, fabricators won’t cut until the base cabinets are installed and level.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

For kitchen work in Jeannette and the rest of Westmoreland County, permits center around health and safety, not finishes. Cosmetic changes—painting, new cabinet hardware, replacing cabinet doors, countertop swaps where the sink and appliances stay put—don’t require permits. The line is crossed when you move walls, relocate plumbing, add new electrical circuits, or do work that could affect structural or fire safety. In Greensburg, for instance, removing or altering a structural wall or moving plumbing lines will trigger a building permit, often with a plan review if the work changes layout or system capacity.

In nearby Hempfield Township, replacing cabinets alone usually doesn’t need a permit if appliance locations stay unchanged. However, upgrading electrical—old 1960s–70s kitchens rarely have enough circuits for modern needs—means an inspection, which is often done by certified third-party agencies like Code.sys rather than the city office.

Any contract over $500 requires the contractor to be registered with Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Contractor system (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov), and all employers must have workers’ compensation coverage (check via WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov). Unpermitted work may cause problems at resale or with insurance—if what you’ve done isn’t legal, you may be forced to undo or redo it.

Cabinets: reface, stock, semi-custom or custom

The cabinet decision is where most budgets are set—and blown. Refacing keeps your current boxes but gives you a new look with fresh doors, drawer fronts, and veneer. It makes sense if the cabinets are structurally sound, laid out the way you want, and you’re not aiming for a big change. It does not create new storage or change the basic layout, but when suitable, refacing is a solid choice in Jeannette’s many well-built, older homes.

Stock cabinets come in set widths and heights, so a wall that is even slightly off will need filler strips and won’t look “fitted.” Semi-custom gives you more options for both sizing and finishes, letting you work around the non-standard wall dimensions common in Westmoreland County’s 1960s–1980s homes, which, as local contractors know, rarely match modern measurements. Custom cabinets are built for your space and offer any feature or finish but command the highest price and longest timelines.

Construction quality matters as much as the label: plywood boxes are stronger than particleboard, dovetail joints outlast staples, and full-extension soft-close hardware should be standard above the entry level. Frameless (Euro-style) cabinets offer more storage, while framed give a more traditional look.

Lead times, sequencing and why it takes longer

Kitchen remodels in Jeannette are all about sequencing—and most delays come from missing how things depend on each other. Up front you have the design and measurement phase, critical if non-standard walls are in play (which is common in Westmoreland County’s older houses). Next, cabinets are ordered—lead times for semi-custom or custom are usually 6–12 weeks. Always order cabinets before you start demolition; waiting until demo is done risks adding months to the project while you live in a construction zone.

After demolition, rough mechanicals (electrical and plumbing) are done and inspected. Only then are base cabinets installed. The next pinch point nobody ever warns about: countertops can’t even be templated until the cabinets are set and level. Fabricators won’t cut stone or solid surface from a drawing—there is always an unavoidable gap of a week or more between cabinet install and countertop template, then another 2–4 weeks for fabrication and installation.

Finish-trade labor in Westmoreland County is in shorter supply compared to Allegheny County, so allow for longer stretches between trades. Once tops are in, then flooring, tile, and trim go in. Missing any step or misreading timing piles on extra days.

Three ways to organise the project

How you structure your kitchen renovation in Jeannette affects cost, stress, and timelines more than almost any other decision. The most hands-off path is hiring a design-build kitchen firm—they handle everything from design through installation under one contract. This means the highest cost but the least homeowner involvement, and a single point of accountability if anything goes sideways.

A general contractor sits in the middle. They coordinate all the trades—electrician, plumber, cabinet installer, countertop fabricator—and run the schedule, but the homeowner or designer still chooses products and oversees design. This costs less than a full design-build but still shifts risk and headaches off your plate.

Owner-managed projects are cheapest but demand serious management of timing, trades, and materials. If an electrician or a countertop crew can’t hit your window, delays stack up. In Westmoreland County’s current labor market, where finish trades are in short supply, this adds stress. If you have a day job and your project requires permits, skip the self-managed route unless you are prepared for full-time project management.

Renovating to live in versus renovating to sell

A new kitchen absolutely helps a house in Jeannette sell faster—but don’t expect to get every dollar back out. Most renovations recover less than their full cost, and mid-range remodels tend to return a higher percentage than upscale ones. For homeowners staying put, it makes sense to spend where you’ll use it every day and ignore resale. For sellers, neutral finishes, a mid-range spend—typically around $27,000–$58,500 for a full gut remodel—and skipping major layout changes are often the smart call.

Jeannette’s median household income sits at $47,801 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), and buyers in the area rarely pay a premium for a gourmet kitchen above that market’s range. Over-improving—putting in a $100,000 kitchen for a $48,000 neighborhood—doesn’t pay off and may even make the home harder to sell. Spend carefully: matching your kitchen’s scope to local expectations avoids disappointment and wasted money.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Jeannette

How much do new kitchen cabinets cost in Jeannette? For most Jeannette kitchens, new cabinets can range widely in price. Stock cabinets start around $6,300–$27,000, covering both basic lines and semi-custom options that work well for typical Westmoreland County homes built in the 1960s–1980s. Full custom cabinets, though rare in this area, also fall within $18,000–$27,000 unless you opt for exotic materials or specialty finishes. Replacing cabinets is usually the single largest expense after moving walls or plumbing, so be sure the layout is set before you place the order. The final price will depend on whether your walls match modern cabinet sizes, the material you choose for cabinet boxes, and the quality of the hardware. Labor and materials together make up the full cabinet cost, and in this market, lead times are typically 6–12 weeks for anything above the basic lines.
How much more does moving the sink and range cost? Shifting the location of your sink or range is one of the biggest drivers of cost creep in a Jeannette kitchen remodel. Keeping the same layout puts you near the low end of a full gut remodel, but moving plumbing or electrical means upgraded service runs, patching, inspections, and possibly even a layout permit. This moves your scope into the “gut plus layout change” band, starting around $58,500–$108,000, with the longer duration (10–16 weeks is common). The labor market here can also add lead time for rough-ins. Unless your existing kitchen is truly dysfunctional, it often pays to work with what you have, especially since Westmoreland’s many 1960s–1980s homes may already have more modern layouts than older city kitchens.
What should I expect in a pre-1980 Jeannette kitchen? Most homes in Jeannette were built before 1980, and that brings both opportunities and challenges. Expect to deal with cabinets or walls that may not match current measurements, which makes stock cabinets harder to fit. Electrical systems often need upgrading—older kitchens were built for fewer appliances and can’t handle today’s load safely. If your home predates 1978, the federal EPA Lead RRP Rule applies; any contractor disturbing painted surfaces must be lead-safe certified. Cabinet refacing is sometimes possible, but if the boxes are not solid or the layout is poor, replacement in the $6,300–$27,000 range is usually smarter. Be ready for surprises behind the walls—plumbing and wiring upgrades are common hidden costs in Jeannette’s older homes.
Is induction worth it over gas? Induction cooking has real advantages—speed, precise control, and easy cleaning. In Jeannette, where many kitchens are getting upgraded electrical during a remodel, it can be a smart option. Induction ranges require a dedicated 240V circuit, which is typically feasible during a full gut renovation in the $27,000–$108,000 range. If you’re already tearing out walls and touching electrical, adding the right wiring doesn’t add much. Some cooks still prefer gas, but induction is as fast or faster, safer with kids, and sometimes more energy-efficient. However, if you’re not moving the range or upgrading the service panel, sticking with current fuel is usually cheaper. If you want induction for the long-term, do it when the trades are already in for a big remodel.
How should renovation payments be scheduled? In Pennsylvania, including Jeannette, kitchen renovation contracts over $500 are regulated by the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. A deposit is common and should never exceed one-third of the contract price upfront. Subsequent payments are typically tied to milestones: cabinets ordered, demolition complete, cabinets installed, countertops templated, and final punch list. For example, a gut remodel in the $58,500 range might be split as a 30% deposit, 40% at midway (like cabinets installed), and the remaining 30% upon completion. Never pay in full until all work is finished and passes inspection, and keep everything in writing. If anyone asks for the entire amount upfront, that’s a red flag in the Pennsylvania market.
Should I order cabinets before demolition? Yes, always order your cabinets before you begin demolition. In Jeannette and throughout Westmoreland County, semi-custom or custom cabinets typically take 6–12 weeks to arrive. If you wait until your old kitchen is torn out, you could spend weeks living in a construction site just waiting for the cabinets to show up. For a standard replacement, this keeps your total project duration—the time you’re without a working kitchen—closer to 6–10 weeks instead of dragging on. In a tight labor market, it’s even more important to schedule trades in advance. However, be sure your measurements are solid—sometimes, minor demo is needed to check wall conditions before final cabinet orders for older homes.
Can I cancel a renovation contract in Pennsylvania? Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act guarantees that homeowners in Jeannette have a 3 business-day right of cancellation for any home improvement contract over $500. This “cooling-off” period starts once you sign, giving you time to reconsider or seek another bid. The contractor must include clear language about this right in the written contract—if it’s missing, the contract does not comply with state law. If you do cancel in time, all deposits must be returned. This rule covers all kitchen renovations priced above $6,300 (which includes almost any new cabinet project), so you are protected as long as you act promptly and communicate in writing. Never let a contractor tell you otherwise.
Can I over-improve for my Jeannette neighbourhood? Yes, and it’s easy to do if you go all-in on high-end materials or layout changes. In Jeannette, where the median household income is $47,801, the market simply doesn’t support luxury kitchen prices. If you put a $108,000 kitchen in a neighborhood where most homes are worth less than that, you won’t get the extra money back on resale. Focus on mid-range materials and a good, functional layout. Resale buyers want something fresh and usable, not gourmet extras. If you’re renovating for yourself and plan to stay long-term, then investing in what brings daily value is fine. But for resale, keep the kitchen in line with local values.
Should I reface or replace my kitchen cabinets? In Jeannette’s older homes, refacing is a solid option if your cabinet boxes are sturdy, square, and laid out the way you want. Refacing runs $6,300–$18,000 and gives you a new look with minimal disruption, but it cannot add storage or correct poor layouts. If the boxes are damaged or you want to move things around, replacement is better—figure on $6,300–$27,000 for stock or semi-custom cabinets. Given the many 1960s–1980s houses here, a lot of kitchens need more than just new doors. Before you commit, have a contractor check your cabinet frames—if they’re solid, refacing saves both time and money; otherwise, a full replacement is the best investment.
