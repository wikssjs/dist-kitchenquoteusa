Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovations often trigger licensing, permits, and inspections—especially when plumbing or electrical is modified. For work over $500, Pennsylvania requires renovation contractors to hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). When you’re changing or adding kitchen circuits, a licensed PA electrician must perform the electrical work; you can verify credentials at dli.pa.gov. Plumbing changes—such as relocating a sink, adding a dishwasher hookup, or modifying supply/pressure lines—must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are municipality-specific, but as a rule in many Pennsylvania communities, kitchen renovations involving plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal require building permits and inspections. Cosmetic work—like repainting, new hardware, or cabinet refacing without disturbing wiring/plumbing—typically does not require a permit. Because Washington’s older housing stock is common (many homes were built before 1980), lead paint is a real planning factor: pre-1978 homes in Washington may contain lead-based paint on kitchen surfaces. If a contractor will disturb paint, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required; you can check a firm at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring for gut remodels in older homes.

Finally, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract for home improvement work over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation. Ask for that paperwork before work begins.

To verify credentials as a homeowner in Washington, PA: (1) confirm HIC registration online, (2) request a certificate of insurance and proof of workers’ compensation coverage, (3) verify electrician/plumber licenses through the state lookup tools, and (4) make sure any permits are pulled before demolition starts.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Washington?

With roughly 41.4% of Washington’s housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many Franklin County kitchens are “dated but still solid.” In these homes, the cabinet boxes are often structurally sound, while door styles, drawer fronts, and finish color look tired. Cabinet refacing—replacing only the door/drawer fronts and adding new veneer while keeping the existing boxes—is a middle-ground solution. In most Washington projects, refacing can cost 40–60% less than full replacement, and it’s usually faster because it avoids removing the entire cabinet run.

That said, refacing is cost-effective only when the existing boxes are square, firmly attached, and in acceptable interior condition. In older Washington kitchens, I’ll often see issues that can reduce refacing value: sagging uppers, water damage behind the fridge, cracked particleboard near sinks, or boxes that aren’t level due to earlier wall repairs. If those conditions exist, replacing the cabinets may actually be the more economical choice once you factor in filler work and the time spent correcting bad foundations.

Here’s a concrete example from typical pricing bands. If your current layout is functional and your boxes are sound, refacing commonly lands around $2,500–$8,000. If you compare that to full cabinet replacement at $8,000–$40,000, refacing makes sense when the frame is truly reusable and you only need a style refresh. But if you’re seeing moisture damage near the dishwasher and older plumbing has caused swelling, that’s where spending on replacement becomes justified—because refacing can’t fix the underlying box deterioration.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Washington

When you hire a contractor for a Washington, Pennsylvania kitchen, verify credentials first—before design, demolition, or any money changes hands. Start by checking Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for the firm and make sure the specialists are licensed for what they’ll do. For insurance and licensing, confirm online via Pennsylvania and, when applicable, specialty registries. Also ask about IICRC certification if the contractor markets it for restoration/water-related mitigation; for any claims around restoration work, verify directly through the certifying body’s online listing.

Next, require 2–3 itemized written quotes. In Washington projects, an apples-to-apples comparison matters because costs can swing when you include or exclude electrical upgrades, plumbing adjustments, and permit-driven scope. Ask for a breakdown by labor and materials (demo, framing/repairs, electrical rough-in, plumbing work, cabinet install, countertop fabrication/installation, flooring, trim, and cleanup). Avoid lump-sum bids that don’t identify allowances for cabinets, backsplash, or appliance hookups.

Read the scope line-by-line. What’s excluded—like removing a tile backer wall, reworking subfloor for LVP, replacing shutoffs, or electrical GFCI/circuit updates? Ask whether the contract includes permit handling and inspection coordination. Verify warranties: get the workmanship warranty length in writing, plus separate manufacturer warranties for cabinets, countertops, and appliances. For payment, avoid large front-loaded draws; keep upfront deposits in the 10–15% range, and hold back payment until the job is complete and punch-list items are finished. Finally, insist on a written timeline with an arrival estimate, protected work windows, and a completion target (especially for projects involving countertops with fabrication lead times).
