Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen remodels are regulated more than many homeowners expect, particularly when plumbing or electrical work is involved. Contractors performing home improvement work over $500 must be covered by Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the state program at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For any kitchen electrical work—such as adding outlets, modifying circuits, or running new lines—a licensed PA electrician must install or modify the circuits; confirm credentials at dli.pa.gov. Plumbing work, including sink and dishwasher hookups or any relocation, must be performed by or supervised by a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required in most PA municipalities when renovations include plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal. Cosmetic work—like paint, hardware swaps, or cabinet refacing without disturbing plumbing/electrical—typically does not require permits. Lead paint is a serious concern in older Plumstead homes: if your property predates 1978, lead-based paint may be present on kitchen surfaces. Pennsylvania contractors disturbing lead paint surfaces should be EPA-certified; the EPA requires Lead-Safe RRP certification for covered renovations. Verify certification before hiring using certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov (and use that verification when you’re considering gut remodels).

Finally, the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires written contracts for projects over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation. Step-by-step, I advise Plumstead homeowners to: (1) confirm HIC registration, (2) request the electrician/plumber license details, (3) ask for current certificates of liability insurance and workers’ compensation, and (4) keep copies of everything with the signed contract before work starts.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Plumstead?

With about 27.6% of Plumstead’s housing stock built before 1980, many Bucks County homeowners are working around 30–50-year-old kitchen configurations. Often the cabinet boxes are still solid and square enough to support new doors, but the door style, finish, and hardware look dated—or the doors no longer match the updated countertop and flooring. Cabinet refacing is a popular middle ground because it keeps the existing boxes in place and typically costs about 40–60% less than full replacement. In many Plumstead kitchens, that also means fewer surprises behind the cabinets, since you’re not fully demolishing and reinstalling everything.

Refacing is only cost-effective when the existing boxes are in good condition—square/true, not sagging, and able to accept new hinges and closures cleanly. If the old boxes are warped, water-damaged, or out of level, refacing can lock you into problems you would have corrected with replacement. For example, if you’re aiming for a budget cabinet update in the $1,500–$8,000 range and you have good box condition, refacing can be justified quickly. But if you’re planning new layouts (like adding a pantry pullout, switching from drawers to doors in critical zones, or correcting an out-of-plumb run), replacement often becomes the smarter investment—even if the upfront number moves toward the $8,000–$35,000 band.

Local Bucks County conditions also matter: older walls can hide uneven studs or soffit remnants. Replacement gives you more opportunities to correct those issues and align everything to modern standards. Refacing can still work beautifully, but the scope must be matched to the existing cabinet condition, not just the homeowner’s aesthetic goals.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes.
