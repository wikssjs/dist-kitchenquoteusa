Why the contingency exists

With 62.9% of Plum’s homes built before 1980, surprises behind the walls are routine here. When demolition starts, contractors often discover out-of-plumb walls and sloping floors. This means added hours of shimming and carpentry just to get new cabinets to sit level—what looked square on the outside rarely is underneath. In a full gut, you may find original wiring that’s not properly grounded or undersized for today’s appliances. Expect to resolve this with circuit upgrades and, in cases of existing knob-and-tube or aluminum, a full electrical update can land in the $4,000–$10,000 range. Plumbing surprises are just as common—galvanized supply lines and cast-iron drains that have reached the end of their serviceable life are the norm, not the exception, adding another $2,000–$7,000 if they need replacing.

No insulation on exterior walls beneath old cabinets is still found in pre-1980 homes. Installing batt or rigid insulation before closing up can add a bit, but skipping it isn’t smart given Allegheny County’s winters. Older homes have sometimes seen unpermitted work—creative wiring or makeshift framing—requiring further rework to bring up to code. For homes built before 1978, the federal EPA Lead Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Rule applies; if painted surfaces or certain flooring will be disturbed, lead and asbestos testing comes first. Mitigation can start at $2,000–$5,000 per hazard.

In newer Plum subdivisions, the shock is different: particleboard cabinets, laminate counters delaminating, and older basic floors all tend to hit replacement age together, forcing a “rip the Band-Aid off” approach.

Allocating a kitchen budget

Most Plum homeowners are surprised how much of a kitchen remodel's budget goes to cabinetry—and how little is left for splurges after the essentials. For a typical gut renovation in the $67,500 range, 25–35% will be eaten by new cabinets. Labour is the next biggest piece, especially if you’re restructuring or upgrading systems in one of Plum’s older homes. Countertops, appliances, and flooring come after, with a surprising bump for electrical and plumbing once you look behind the walls.

The two lines that routinely get left out are the most critical: a contingency fund—essential for pre-1980 homes where “what else will we find?” is always a real question—and soft costs like permits, delivery, and design services. They look small on paper, but being caught without them will wreck even a careful plan. If you have an extra thousand, it buys the most in cabinetry upgrades (real plywood boxes outlast particleboard) or in adding circuits over premium lighting or swapped-in pro appliances. Spending top dollar for an appliance but putting it in basic cabinets is rarely worth it in this market; focus first on making the room solid and the workflow right.

Cabinets are the critical path

Most delays in a kitchen renovation in Plum trace back to one thing: the cabinet timeline. Here’s how the actual workflow goes. You start with a finalized design and field measurements—these hold up everything else. Cabinets must be ordered at this point, not after demolition. Why? Lead times in the Pittsburgh area, including Plum, are stretched due to steady demand and limited skilled trades. Six to twelve weeks for semi-custom is not unusual, and special orders take longer.

Only after cabinets ship do you schedule demolition and rough-in—letting demo get ahead of orders is a mistake that leaves you eating takeout with no progress on site. Once walls are opened, rough plumbing and electrical are completed and inspected, then it’s time for cabinets to be installed. Only after cabinets are set can countertops be templated. Countertop fabricators will not cut stone or solid surface from a plan—they need cabinets in place with every wall out-of-plumb detail accounted for. This creates an unavoidable pause: even the fastest fabricators need 7–14 days from template to install. That dead week surprises homeowners more than anything else.

Once tops are in, appliances, backsplash, flooring (if not pre-installed), and finishing trim follow. In the high-demand Allegheny County market, every handoff is at the mercy of the trade crunch. The critical path runs through cabinets and tops—order both well ahead, and you’ll avoid most headaches.

Comparing kitchen renovation bids in Plum

Most kitchen bids in Plum look the same at first glance—but line-by-line, they’re night and day. The biggest culprit is allowances: these are placeholder dollar amounts for items not yet picked (tile, fixtures, appliances). One bid may budget $5,000 for appliances, the next $12,000; both claim to be “complete.” Always compare allowance realism before you judge which proposal is more economical. A $15,000 difference can hide in wishful thinking on these lines alone.

Beyond allowances, a real quote specifies cabinet manufacturer and construction, countertop type and thickness, and spells out who prices and approves unforeseen conditions—especially common in older Plum homes. The change-order process should require your written approval before any extra work starts, not just an after-the-fact invoice. The payment schedule should be tied to progress milestones (not calendar dates), and you should receive lien waivers from subs as you pay, to keep your title clean.

Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract (for jobs over $500) and gives you three business days to cancel after signing. If any contractor pushes you for a handshake-only or wants you to waive these rights, walk away.

Code requirements for Plum kitchens

In Plum and throughout Allegheny County, cosmetic work alone—like painting, hardware swaps, or refacing cabinets—typically doesn’t require a permit. You also won’t need a permit to replace countertops, as long as you aren’t moving plumbing or appliances. Any change that moves or adds electrical circuits, plumbing lines, or alters structural walls likely does require a building permit. In Pittsburgh itself, wall changes and plumbing/electrical relocations will almost always trigger a permit and plan review, but most boroughs only require permits when altering systems or the structure.

Electrical inspections are handled directly by the City of Pittsburgh where applicable, while some suburbs use third-party inspection agencies. Always check with your municipality’s code office before starting: what applies in Plum is set locally, even though it’s all under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code. Don’t assume the same rules as Pittsburgh proper.

Any contractor with more than $500 in total contract value must have a Home Improvement Contractor registration with the state Office of Attorney General. You can verify that at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Employers must carry workers’ compensation by state law, which you can check at WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov).

Skipping required permits can risk fines and cause major headaches at sale or refinance—lenders and buyers want proof the work was permitted and inspected.

Living through a kitchen renovation

There’s no glossing over it: losing your kitchen in Plum for six to ten weeks is a test of patience and flexibility. It’s best to set up a temporary kitchen space in a dining room or basement—find an outlet for a microwave, keep your fridge powered, set up a folding table for meals, and expect to rely on your bathroom or laundry sink. Paper plates and plastic cutlery become standard, and you’ll see your food budget rise with more takeout, which over eight weeks often adds up to more than a new floor costs.

Dust is a bigger issue than most expect, especially in older homes where tearing out walls releases decades of debris. A conscientious crew will set up plastic sheeting with zippers to isolate the kitchen, but in the dry indoor winter air, dust migrates fast. Pets can be an issue too—expect doors open for loads, so animals should be safely separated. The dumpster and material deliveries will need driveway space, and in Plum, hilly sites can make access tricky.

Some families move out during demolition week, but most tough it out after the loudest phase. Expect a few days of true kitchen “shutdown,” but more weeks without most conveniences. Factor the food expense into your real budget—it’s not a small cost.

If you're in an older Plum house with galvanized or ungrounded lines, these upgrades are often needed anyway, and it's more efficient to include them with a layout shift. In short: moving things costs real money, but it can make a dramatic difference, especially if you're correcting a cramped or awkward old layout that just doesn't work for your family.
How does Plum's housing stock affect cabinet fit? Most homes in Plum were built before 1980, and many have walls or floors that are far from square or level. Newer subdivisions have fewer issues, but even there, you may run into builder-grade framing that's not as perfect as you'd expect. Always allow extra time in the schedule if your house is pre-1980 — it’s routine, not the exception, in Plum.

Why are kitchen quotes in Plum so far apart?
