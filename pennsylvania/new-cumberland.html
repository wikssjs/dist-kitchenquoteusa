What a kitchen remodel costs in New Cumberland

Countertop materials compared

Choosing a countertop for your New Cumberland kitchen should be about how you use your space, not just what looks good under bright lights. Quartz is a popular engineered option that stands up to stains and never needs sealing, but it’s surprisingly sensitive to direct heat — a hot pan can scorch it. Granite is natural, more heat-tolerant, but does require resealing every couple of years to avoid staining, especially given the freeze-thaw cycling our homes experience. Laminate, especially the better lines, has come a long way: it shrugs off stains and scratches more easily than most people think, and is often a sound choice in rentals or modest kitchens, not just a budget fallback.

Butcher block brings warmth, fits many homes here, and is easy to sand and refinish, but it demands regular oiling and you’ll need to accept scratches and dings as part of the look. Solid surface (such as Corian) is seamless and fully repairable if scratched or burned, though care is needed around very hot pans.

Price is affected most by edge profile complexity, number of sink or cooktop cutouts, and how visible the seams will be. Unlike some trades, countertops always require an exact template or precise measurement in your finished kitchen — not just a plan. That means you can’t shortcut the fabrication and installation process. In many cases, especially when combined with mid-range cabinets, spending on high-end stone is not necessary for an attractive, durable kitchen in New Cumberland.

Lead times, sequencing and why it takes longer

The number one frustration I see on New Cumberland kitchen projects is timeframes running longer than anyone expected. This is rarely because trades are slow — it’s down to the order and dependency of each step. First comes design and exact measurement. Only then can cabinets be ordered, and lead times for cabinets locally can run 6–10 weeks based on the cabinet line and current labor squeeze in Cumberland County compared to Harrisburg.

Once cabinets are on order, only then does demolition begin. With the kitchen open, rough-in for electrical, plumbing, and sometimes HVAC updates follows, and required inspections (especially electrical) must pass before finishes begin. Cabinets are then installed, and this is the critical path: countertop vendors will not template from plans, only from installed, leveled cabinets. This creates a real “dead zone” — even if every trade is humming, you’ll typically wait a week or more after cabinet install for your countertop template appointment, then another one to three weeks for the fabrication and install. That delay catches nearly every homeowner by surprise. Floors, tile, and final trim can finish only after.

If you want to keep your renovation moving, resist the urge to rip out the old kitchen before the new cabinets are ordered. This avoids weeks of living without a real kitchen, especially important with our local finish-trade labor running tighter. Build that “gap week” into your expectations.

Electrical work in an older New Cumberland kitchen

In older New Cumberland homes — and with 93.7% of housing here built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024) — kitchen electrical is often out of step with current code. Today, a safe and permitted kitchen needs dedicated small-appliance circuits for countertop outlets, GFCI protection at those outlets, properly spaced receptacles so you’re never more than 24 inches away, and dedicated lines for dishwasher, disposal, and likely the microwave. If you cook with electric, a 240-volt circuit is also standard.

But open up the walls in a New Cumberland kitchen, and you might find just two old ungrounded circuits, no GFCI, and a panel that’s full — nothing like what a new kitchen needs. If the inspector sees open walls (which they will on most permitted jobs), they’ll expect everything behind them to come up to code, no exception. That’s also true in post-1960 tract kitchens around Hampden and Lower Allen, which often lack the minimum circuits or GFCIs, falling short of today’s standard. Even many late-1990s homes don’t have the number of circuits code now expects.

This is the leading cause of mid-project cost blowouts: bringing the kitchen’s wiring to code, which can cost from $4,000–$10,000 depending on panel size, access, and what is discovered during demolition. You do not want to skip this step — surprise issues will always return at resale, and unsafe electrical isn’t worth the gamble.

Code requirements for New Cumberland kitchens

In New Cumberland and nearby townships, you do not typically need a permit for cosmetic kitchen work — repainting, swapping hardware, or even refacing cabinets as long as no utilities move. Replacing countertops over existing cabinets seldom triggers permits. What does: any new or relocated electrical circuit, changed plumbing layout, or moving/removing walls. Full remodels that change layout frequently require plan review in townships like Carlisle and Mechanicsburg, and sometimes mechanical permits for a vented range hood.

Electrical inspections, specifically in Carlisle, are done by third-party UCC agencies such as Commonwealth Code Inspection Service, not by the city’s own inspectors. This surprises many homeowners who count on a municipal office to do the walk-through; third-party agencies are more likely to spot and document deficiencies, especially after demolition.

Anyone contracted for more than $500 in kitchen work in Pennsylvania must carry a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Office of Attorney General. You can check this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers' compensation is legally required for any contractor with employees, verifiable through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov).

If you skip needed permits, you risk major trouble at resale — appraisers and buyers’ agents do ask, and prior unpermitted work is the biggest cause of failed home sales besides water and foundation problems.

Resale value and kitchen spending

A kitchen renovation nearly always helps a New Cumberland house sell, but it does not pay back its full cost unless you are strategic about scope and finish level. Most mid-range kitchen remodels recover more value at resale than top-end ones, simply because buyers won’t pay above the prevailing range for any one upgrade. Renovating to sell and renovating to stay are very different projects. If you’re selling in the next year, neutral colors, practical materials, and a spend in the mid bands (such as $30,000–$64,500) fit the profile here. Swapping cabinets and counters — without moving walls — is often enough.

If your plan is to live in the house for years, then invest in what improves your daily life, regardless of resale: maximize the layout, put money into cabinets you’ll enjoy opening, pick appliances you want to use. Bear in mind New Cumberland’s median income of $77,770 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). A gut remodel priced toward the top of the range may not recover the difference at resale here, even in strong markets. Over-improving drives sellers to disappointment.

Comparing kitchen renovation bids in New Cumberland

When comparing kitchen renovation bids in New Cumberland, the most misleading number on the page is the bottom-line total. The culprit: allowances. An allowance is a placeholder — for example, if tile hasn’t been selected, the contractor inserts a dollar amount into the bid. If one bid lists a $2,000 stone counter allowance and the other bids the actual granite you want at $8,000, you might think the first contractor is cheaper, when in reality you’ll pay the difference later.

For an apples-to-apples comparison, start with those allowance lines. Ask for each to be specific: name the actual cabinet brand and model, call out countertop type and thickness, and note if installation and removal is included. Details matter in electrical work (will they add required circuits? GFCI everywhere needed?), and every bid should spell out how change orders are handled and if unforeseen conditions require your written sign-off before extra work. A milestone-based payment schedule protects you from the project running ahead of the money. Lien waivers from subcontractors at each payment stage prevent legal headaches down the line.

Under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, every contract over $500 must be in writing, and you have a three business-day right to cancel. Read it and don’t be afraid to ask a contractor to clarify any line before signing.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in New Cumberland

Does a kitchen renovation increase home value in New Cumberland? A kitchen renovation almost always boosts your home’s appeal and can help it sell faster in New Cumberland. However, it’s important to be realistic about payback. Most mid-range remodels (costing $30,000–$64,500) recoup a solid portion when selling, but you typically will not recover every dollar spent, especially if you go for high-end finishes or major layout changes in a modest neighborhood. This area’s median income is $77,770 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), so buyers expect good, functional kitchens — not over-the-top luxury. If you’re planning to sell within a couple of years, neutral finishes and practical updates attract more buyers. If you plan to live in your house long-term, focus on what works for your family, and enjoy the investment even if it doesn’t all come back at resale.
How much do new kitchen cabinets cost in New Cumberland? Most homeowners in New Cumberland can expect to pay between $6,900–$30,000 for new cabinets, with the typical project around $20,000. That range covers stock and semi-custom cabinets, basic to higher-end finishes, and standard hardware. Factors that push the price up include full custom work, specialty inserts, and unique finishes. This area’s high percentage of homes built before 1980 (93.7%; U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024) means you'll often deal with odd measurements and may need some customizations even with “standard” cabinets. Don’t skip professional installation — it’s worth it for older houses with out-of-square walls and floors, common in New Cumberland. Refacing cabinets costs a bit less and keeps the existing boxes.
Does replacing cabinets require a permit? In most New Cumberland jobs, simply removing and installing new cabinets — as long as you’re not moving or adding plumbing or electrical — does not require a permit. Per local practice, cabinet refacing and straightforward cabinet swaps are completed without permits. However, if you plan to relocate outlets, switches, or lighting with your new cabinets, or move plumbing, you will cross into permit territory. Projects triggering new circuits or layout changes (gut remodels, wall moves) almost always need a permit and plan review, which is common in towns like Carlisle and Mechanicsburg. It’s smart, in every case, to document what was done. Skipping needed permits can cause real headaches at resale, and jobs over $500 require a written contract and contractor registration by Pennsylvania law.
Do older New Cumberland homes need electrical upgrades for a kitchen? Yes, nearly every older home in New Cumberland (and 93.7% were built before 1980; U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024) will need electrical work in the kitchen to meet current code. Modern kitchens require multiple dedicated small-appliance circuits, GFCI-outfitted outlets, and separate lines for major appliances like dishwashers and microwaves. In many houses, opening walls reveals just a couple of ungrounded circuits or limited panel space. Once work exposes wiring, inspectors expect the new work to meet today’s standards — no exceptions. A full upgrade during a kitchen remodel can cost from $4,000–$10,000, depending on what’s found in your walls and the panel’s capacity. Budget for this when planning, as electrical upgrades are both a safety issue and a legal requirement.
Should I choose plywood or particleboard cabinet boxes? In New Cumberland, both plywood and high-quality particleboard are used in kitchen cabinet construction. Plywood is strong, resists moisture a bit better, and tends to hold screws more securely, which can be an advantage in older homes with uneven walls. Particleboard (especially commercial-grade) is stable and often found in semi-custom cabinets well below the top tier of pricing ($6,900–$30,000). For most kitchens here, the choice often comes down to budget and expected moisture — if you don’t have water problems or heavy abuse, particleboard is a smart use of funds. Plywood is worth the splurge for sink bases or if you want cabinets to last fifty years, but it’s not always necessary. Good installation is more important than the underlying material for long-term satisfaction.
How far ahead should I book a contractor in New Cumberland? Given the slightly tighter finish-trade labor market in Cumberland County relative to Harrisburg, you’ll want to contact contractors at least two to three months before you hope to start the work. If your project is full-scope — cabinets, electrical, flooring, and finishes ($30,000–$119,000) — allow for additional lead time. Cabinet orders alone can take six to ten weeks, especially for semi-custom work. Delaying contractor selection will leave you living with a torn-up kitchen much longer. Spring and summer are busiest; planning meetings, selections, and deposits should ideally occur before your current kitchen is touched. Booking a trusted contractor ahead ensures your project stays on the calendar and doesn’t get delayed due to labor bottlenecks.
How much more does moving the sink and range cost? Moving a sink or range in a New Cumberland kitchen brings new plumbing and electrical work — and it’s where you step from a simple remodel into full gut territory. Relocating a sink involves new supply and drain lines, possible slab or subfloor access, and patching walls. The same goes for a range, especially if it requires new 240-volt wiring or a dedicated gas line. The upcharge for moving these major fixtures adds $8,000–$20,000 to your kitchen cost, in addition to the base remodel. That’s because you’ll need both skilled trades and inspections. Unless your layout is truly dysfunctional, focus your budget elsewhere; layout moves should be made for life-changing improvements, not modest convenience.
How do I set up a temporary kitchen? During a New Cumberland kitchen remodel, especially a full gut project ($30,000–$119,000), you’ll live without the main kitchen for weeks. Most people set up a temporary space in a dining room or basement with a folding table, coffee maker, microwave, and hot plate. Relocate the fridge if possible; if not, a small secondhand fridge or cooler helps. Use disposable plates or do dishes in a bath sink — but avoid pouring greasy water down the tub. Set up power strips for small appliances, but don’t overload them on older circuits, especially common in pre-1980 homes here. Meal prep ahead and freeze portions. Some families plan around a vacation or use this period to stay with relatives for the roughest phase. Having a functional, if limited, temporary space reduces stress considerably during the job.
Is a cosmetic refresh enough or do I need a gut remodel? In many New Cumberland homes, especially post-1960 builds, a targeted refresh — such as cabinet refacing ($6,900–$20,000), new counters, and fresh paint — can dramatically improve appearance and function without the mess or cost of a gut remodel. Gutting makes sense if the layout isn’t working, if electrical or plumbing needs major overhaul, or if you have moisture or structural problems. Many kitchens here just need better lighting, new surfaces, and updated hardware to look and feel new. Don’t gut your kitchen simply because you think it’s expected; weigh the return and your actual frustrations. Smaller jobs are generally faster, easier to live through, and less likely to cause headache at resale.
