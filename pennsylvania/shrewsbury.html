Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, contractor credentials and permits aren’t optional—especially once your kitchen involves plumbing or electrical changes. For home improvement work over $500, Pennsylvania requires kitchen renovation contractors to hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work, any installation or modification to circuits must be completed by a licensed PA electrician; verify credentials and licensure at dli.pa.gov. For plumbing, sink, dishwasher hookup, and any relocation of plumbing lines must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required when you alter the kitchen beyond cosmetics. In most Pennsylvania municipalities, building permits and inspections are triggered by plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal/restructuring (and often when you alter openings). Cosmetic work such as paint, hardware changes, and cabinet refacing typically does not require a permit. In older Shrewsbury homes, lead paint can also be a real factor: if your kitchen has surfaces that may contain lead (pre-1978 homes are the risk group), EPA requires Lead-Safe RRP certification when contractors disturb lead-based paint surfaces. You can confirm certification using the EPA’s certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring anyone for gut remodels.

For the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, written contracts are required for all home improvement work over $500, and homeowners get a 3-day right of cancellation—ask for it in writing. When vetting a Shrewsbury contractor, verify HIC registration online, request current certificates of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation, and confirm that subcontractors (electrician/plumber) are licensed and insured. A reputable firm will provide these documents before work begins.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Shrewsbury?

With 46.2% of Shrewsbury’s housing stock built before 1980, many York County homeowners are dealing with 30–50-year-old kitchens where the cabinet boxes are often still structurally usable, but the style—and sometimes the functionality—feels dated. Cabinet refacing is a popular middle ground because you typically replace only the door and drawer fronts and apply veneer to the visible cabinet faces, keeping the existing boxes and interior framework. In the Shrewsbury market, refacing often lands about 40–60% less than full replacement, which can be a smarter use of dollars when the layout is workable and the boxes are straight.

That said, refacing only works when the existing cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. In older plaster-and-stud walls, we sometimes see cabinets that are slightly out of square due to years of settling or moisture exposure. If the cabinet boxes are racked or have soft/failed particleboard backs and bottoms, refacing just preserves the underlying problems. In those cases, replacement—either stock (faster lead time) or semi-custom (better fit and options)—is the cleaner, longer-lasting approach.

Here’s where homeowners can justify the difference. If your current cabinets are sound, refacing around $2,000–$8,000 can be enough to refresh the kitchen dramatically, especially when you pair it with a countertop upgrade (for example, quartz or granite within the $2,000–$9,000 countertop installation band). But if you’re replacing a poorly fitting run of base cabinets or need to correct chronic clearance issues under non-standard walls, full cabinet replacement—often $8,000–$35,000 for stock-to-semi-custom—can prevent repeated filler work and future misalignment.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Shrewsbury

Choosing the right kitchen renovation contractor in Shrewsbury starts with verifying licensing and certifications the same way we would for any trade partner working in a York County older home. First, confirm Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for any work over $500 using the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s HIC site (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). Second, for electrical work, confirm the contractor’s licensed electrician details using dli.pa.gov, and for plumbing confirm the licensed plumber credentials through the appropriate Pennsylvania licensing verification. If the job may involve lead-risk work in pre-1978 areas, ask whether the firm and its crew hold EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification and verify through certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes—not just a lump sum. A proper quote breaks out labor vs. materials (cabinet install, countertop template and install, electrical rough-in/trim, plumbing modifications, flooring subfloor prep, and haul-away). Read the scope line-by-line: what’s included for demolition and dust control, who disconnects/reconnects appliances, and what’s excluded (notably drywall repairs, subfloor leveling, or electrical circuit corrections). Confirm warranties too: ask for the workmanship warranty length, plus separate equipment/material warranties (cabinets, countertops, flooring, and appliances). For payment scheduling, never pay more than 10–15% upfront and use a holdback until the job is complete and punch-list items are resolved. Finally, demand a schedule in writing: an arrival estimate, inspection/permit lead time, and a realistic completion window.
