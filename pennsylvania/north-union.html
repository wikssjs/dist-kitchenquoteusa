Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovation work is regulated to protect homeowners—especially in older-stock homes like many in North Union. If the total cost of the home improvement project is over $500, Pennsylvania contractors generally need Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For electrical work (new circuits, modifying wiring, or adding outlets tied to kitchen loads), a licensed PA electrician must install or modify any circuits—verify licensing and credentials through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing work (sink/dishwasher hookups, any relocation, and water supply/drain changes), work must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are typically required when renovations involve plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal. Cosmetic changes like painting, hardware swaps, or cabinet refacing typically do not require permits. In older homes, lead safety is critical: North Union homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint on kitchen surfaces. If a contractor is disturbing painted surfaces, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required (verify at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov).

The PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires written contracts for home improvement work over $500 and includes a 3-day right of cancellation. Before demo, verify credentials and coverage step-by-step: (1) confirm HIC registration online, (2) request your contractor’s proof of liability insurance, (3) request proof of workers’ compensation coverage, and (4) confirm the electrician/plumber licenses for any electrical/plumbing scope. Do this before the first wall is opened—code issues discovered after demo are expensive to fix.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in North Union?

With 71.8% of North Union’s housing stock built before 1980, many Fayette County homeowners are working with older kitchens where the cabinet boxes may still be structurally sound but the look is dated and storage is inefficient. Cabinet refacing—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and applying veneer to visible surfaces while keeping the existing boxes—is a popular middle ground. In the market, refacing usually costs about 40–60% less than full replacement because you’re avoiding complete removal, floor/subfloor patches from a total cabinet pull, and the full hardware and installation labor that comes with new boxes.

Refacing is cost-effective only when the existing cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. If you’ve got sagging uppers, water-damaged base cabinets, or boxes that are out of level due to older floors, refacing can trap problems and shorten cabinet life. In North Union’s pre-1980 homes, it’s also common to find that the kitchen layout is “almost right”—the style is wrong, but the functional layout may already be set. That’s when refacing wins. If you need to change storage (pull-out trays, deeper pantry sections, corrected appliance clearances) or if the boxes are compromised, replacement becomes the better long-term value.

For a dollar example: if you’re choosing between refacing ($3,200–$9,000) and stock cabinet replacement ($9,000–$42,000), refacing makes the most sense when boxes are fine and you only want a refreshed door style. But if inspection reveals water damage or box instability, the “saved” dollars can become rework—because those issues typically surface during door installation and reveal deeper fixes needed to avoid misaligned doors.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in North Union

Choosing the right contractor in North Union comes down to verification, documentation, and clarity on scope. Start by checking licensing and credentials in the right places: confirm the contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for projects over $500 at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For electrical scope, confirm the PA electrician’s license through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing, request the plumber’s PA license verification as well. Even if a contractor manages the overall project, electricians and plumbers should be properly licensed for their portions of work.

Get 2–3 itemized written quotes—don’t accept a single lump-sum number without line items. Your quote should separate labor vs materials (demo, framing/drywall, electrical rough-in, plumbing rough-in, cabinets, countertops, flooring, trim, and finish painting), and identify what’s included for protection, haul-away, and cabinet installation adjustments. Read the scope for exclusions (what happens if subfloor leveling is needed, what’s the allowance for damaged drywall, and who handles permit fees if layout changes).

Warranty matters too: ask for the workmanship warranty length and what’s covered for materials (cabinets, countertops, flooring). Payment schedule should be conservative—never more than 10–15% upfront; use a holdback until the job is complete and cleanup is verified. Finally, require a timeline in writing, including arrival dates, expected cabinet/countertop ordering time, and the realistic completion window, especially during peak renovation seasons when lead times are longer for permit-heavy work.

Red flags in North Union include: vague “included/excluded” scopes that let costs expand after demo, refusal to provide itemized labor/materials, missing proof of workers’ compensation, a payment schedule asking for large upfront deposits, and contractors who skip lead-safe procedures on pre-1978 kitchens or can’t explain how permits/inspections are handled before wall openings.
