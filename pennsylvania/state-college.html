Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, compliance matters because kitchen renovations frequently involve mechanical changes—not just finishes. Contractors performing work over $500 must hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For any kitchen electrical work that installs or modifies circuits, a licensed PA electrician must do the work—verify credentials through the Department of Labor & Industry at dli.pa.gov. Plumbing work (sink and dishwasher connections, supply/valve changes, or relocation) must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are typically required in most municipalities when renovations include plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal/structural changes that affect building systems. Cosmetic work—paint, hardware swaps, and cabinet refacing where no plumbing/electrical is altered—often does not require a building permit, but you still need a contractor who documents the work properly and follows the UCC (Uniform Construction Code) process.

Lead paint is a major concern for older kitchens. In State College, many homes predate 1978, so lead-based paint may exist on surfaces disturbed during a gut. When contractors disturb lead paint, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required; you can verify certification at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring for gut remodels.

Finally, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires written contracts for work over $500 and includes a 3-day right of cancellation—request the details in writing. Step-by-step: confirm the contractor’s HIC registration online, then ask for a current certificate of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation. If the job includes electrical/plumbing modifications, request the electrician’s and plumber’s licenses and check the electrician/plumber standing in the state’s registries before work begins.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in State College?

With 65.9% of State College’s housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many Centre County homeowners are renovating kitchens that are structurally usable but visually dated—often from decades of wear around doors, hinges, and moisture-prone areas near sinks. That’s why cabinet refacing is popular: it typically replaces only the door and drawer fronts (and applies a new veneer), while keeping the existing cabinet boxes. In the right kitchen, it can cost about 40%–60% less than full replacement, and it’s faster—helpful if you’re coordinating a tight rental or school-year schedule.

Refacing is only a smart move when the cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. If the box is sagging, swollen, or out of level, refacing tends to magnify the problem—doors won’t align well and can fail prematurely. In older State College homes, it’s also common to find mismatched reveals or previous water issues near older shutoff valves and sink plumbing runs; in those situations, replacement can be the more reliable long-term fix.

Here’s a practical dollar example: say you have a functional cabinet layout and you want a refreshed look. Cabinet refacing often falls around $2,500–$8,000 for fronts and hardware, while stock cabinet replacement can run closer to $8,000–$35,000. If your boxes are solid, refacing can be the best value. If you’re dealing with major box damage or you want a different storage layout (like converting from standard drawers to soft-close deep drawers in key areas), replacement becomes justified because you’re paying to solve underlying structural and functional constraints—not just aesthetics.

Should I reface or replace my kitchen cabinets in State College?
