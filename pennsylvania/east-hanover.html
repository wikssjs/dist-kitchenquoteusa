Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovation contractors must be properly licensed and registered for the work they perform. If a project includes work over $500, a contractor generally needs Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For electrical changes, any circuit installation or modification must be done by a licensed PA electrician—confirm credentials at dli.pa.gov. For plumbing work (sink hookups, dishwasher connections, supply reroutes, or moving plumbing), plumbing must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required in most PA municipalities when you’re moving plumbing, adding new electrical circuits, or removing walls/altering load paths. Cosmetic work—painting, hardware updates, and typical cabinet refacing without disturbing walls—often does not require a building permit. However, “cosmetic” is not always cosmetic in older East Hanover homes: removing countertops can expose code issues, and minor electrical corrections may still trigger inspection requirements depending on what’s being changed.

Lead paint is critical for pre-1978 homes. If your kitchen surfaces may contain lead-based paint, contractors disturbing those surfaces must follow EPA Lead-Safe RRP rules and the firm’s staff typically needs EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification. Verify certification at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before a gut remodel in older East Hanover properties.

Finally, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires written contracts for work over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation. For hiring, verify HIC registration, obtain certificates of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation, and request their insurance documents before demolition begins.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in East Hanover?

With roughly 42% of East Hanover’s housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many kitchens start with solid cabinet boxes but worn finishes, dated door styles, or tired hardware. Cabinet refacing—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and adding veneer while keeping existing cabinet boxes—is often the best “style reset” when boxes are square, well-secured, and in acceptable interior condition. On the local market, refacing is commonly 40–60% less than full replacement because you’re avoiding the labor and materials associated with taking the entire system down and rebuilding from scratch.

Refacing typically makes less sense when the boxes have sagging, water damage at the sink, failed hinges/mounting rails, or when you want to change functional storage—like converting blind corners to better access layouts. In those cases, cabinet replacement becomes more cost-effective because you’re correcting underlying problems instead of covering them.

A practical East Hanover example: if your goal is modern flat-panel doors and refreshed hardware but your sink base is dry and straight, refacing can land around $2,500–$12,000, allowing your budget to go toward quartz or granite. If, however, the sink base shows swelling and the cabinet walls are out of level, refacing may look fine at install but can fail faster—at that point, replacing the cabinet run (often within $12,000–$45,000) is usually the more justified upgrade.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in East Hanover

Start with credentials. For HIC registration, confirm the contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For electrical work, ask who will pull permits and verify that their PA electrician is licensed through Pennsylvania’s Department of Labor & Industry tools at dli.pa.gov. For plumbing, verify the plumber’s PA license (or that they’ll manage work under a licensed plumber). Don’t rely on verbal assurances—use registry checks and written documentation.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes. Avoid one-line “lump sum” numbers. You want a breakdown showing labor and materials for cabinets, countertops (including templating and cutouts), flooring prep, electrical allowance, plumbing fixtures, ventilation work, and any demolition/disposal. Read the scope for inclusions and exclusions: what happens if an old drain line is corroded? Is patching drywall, floor leveling, and paint included? If the contractor is handling insurance-related documentation, confirm what paperwork is provided and how changes are logged.

On warranty, ask for workmanship coverage length and what it covers (especially cabinet installation, countertop sealing/setting, and flooring transitions). For payment, plan a schedule that never requires large upfront sums—limit deposits to about 10–15% and hold back until the punch list is complete. For timing, get an arrival estimate and a completion window in writing, including lead-time assumptions for cabinets and appliances.
