What a kitchen remodel costs in Mifflin County

Mifflin County homeowners face kitchen renovation costs that reflect not only material and labor rates, but the realities of an older, rural housing stock. With a median household income of $55,672 and a homeownership rate of roughly 66.4% (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), most area kitchens are in houses built before 1980—especially in Lewistown and older surrounding townships. These kitchens often need more than just new finishes: narrow galley layouts from the 1950s or undersized circuits mean layout changes and utility relocations are common, especially if accessibility or modern appliances are goals.

The framing for any Mifflin County remodel: blowing your kitchen budget usually happens by pricing one tier—like a cabinet swap—and then committing, project by project, to higher-scope changes like relocating the sink or opening a wall. Plan your scope honestly from the outset and don’t assume “remodel” means the same thing for every bid.

What moving the sink really costs

The moment you decide to move plumbing or major electrical in a Mifflin County kitchen, you're stepping over a cost cliff. Keeping everything in its current place puts you in the lower range—replacing cabinets, counters, and appliances for $28,000–$112,000. But move the sink, range, or any walls, and the price jumps—to the $60,500–$112,000 range—regardless of whether you're shifting things four feet or across the house.

Here's why: Relocating drain lines requires a constant slope, which is easy in a basement but can be a design headache over a finished ceiling or slab. Moving a sink into an island, for example, is an order of magnitude more labor, not just “moving some pipes.” Electrical work means checking if your panel has capacity for new circuits—not a given in pre-1980 homes. Demolishing or altering a wall? First, figure out if it’s load-bearing. That typically means involving an engineer and pulling a permit, both adding cost. Venting a relocated range hood requires a direct exterior duct—sometimes with core drilling brick or adding a new roof penetration, never a superficial matter.

If the current kitchen is tight or awkward, pause and look at your workflow. Often, layouts only feel unworkable because of dated cabinetry or lack of light, not plumbing locations. Many invest thousands moving a sink a short distance when a clever appliance selection or cabinet redesign would solve the bottleneck. In older Lewistown-area houses, keeping to the original plumbing and electrical layout is almost always the most cost-effective path—especially because the jump from fixture replacement to full-on utility relocation is so steep.

Permits and inspections for a Mifflin County kitchen

In Mifflin County, as in the rest of Pennsylvania, the trigger for kitchen permits is not cosmetic work. Painting, changing hardware, or even full cabinet refacing do not require a permit. What does? Any new or relocated plumbing lines, running new electrical circuits, opening or removing walls, or anything structural—those jobs require permits under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is enforced either by your municipality or, if they have opted out, by the PA Department of Labor & Industry.

Most kitchen electrical work—adding lighting circuits, moving outlets—will need to be inspected by a local inspector designated by your municipality. For plumbing, the same approach applies, unless you’re in a township where the state steps in to enforce the code. Mechanical work (like venting a new range hood through the roof) may also trigger review, especially when new penetrations are involved. Unpermitted electrical or structural work is the number-one reason kitchen sales get flagged at resale in Mifflin County: lenders and buyers will require evidence of final inspections.

Your contractor must have a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, issued by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for jobs over $500. Always verify this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Every crew member employed by that contractor must be covered by workers’ compensation insurance, and you can check this at the state’s WCAIS system. Skipping these steps creates risk not just during the job, but when you try to sell the home later on.

The biggest line item in your Mifflin County kitchen

Cabinetry drives your kitchen budget in Mifflin County more than any other single choice. Refacing—replacing doors, drawer fronts, and adding a fresh veneer over the boxes—can be excellent value, but only if your existing boxes are rock solid, square, and the layout suits you. Most 1950s–1970s kitchens in Lewistown and adjacent townships will not meet that mark; box construction in those years was often thinner, and old layouts usually need at least minor adjustments for modern use.

If you’re starting from scratch, stock cabinets are the most affordable, but only fit when your room’s dimensions are close to contemporary standards. Expect filler strips and limited finishes. Semi-custom cabinets adjust to odd dimensions and finishes, solving the problem of older, out-of-square walls—common to the pre-1980 homes making up nearly 78% of the local housing stock. Full custom fits anything and commands both time and money, usually reserved for unusual or high-end designs.

Do not fixate on price alone—pay attention to construction quality: plywood boxes outlast particleboard, dovetail drawers hold up better than stapled, and soft-close, full-extension hardware is now the reliable standard. Frameless cabinets offer more space but require near-perfect installation, while face-frame is more forgiving (and often found in existing homes here).

Cosmetic, replacement, gut, or layout change?

Kitchens in Mifflin County span a fivefold price band because “remodel” can mean anything from painting cabinets to moving every service in the room. Here are the four tiers you need to define up front.

  • Cosmetic refresh: Paint, new hardware, backsplash, maybe lighting upgrades—no cabinets or layout change. Few jobs in this county stay purely cosmetic in 50+ year-old houses.
  • Pull and replace: Your cabinets, counters, and appliances go away; new ones go into the same footprint with plumbing and electrical staying put. Most common starting point for Mifflin County owners looking to modernize in place.
  • Gut, same layout: Everything down to studs. Wiring and plumbing get replaced, but stay where they are. Necessary in houses where previous work means subpar insulation or electrical is two generations out of date.
  • Layout change: Anything structural: moving the sink, relocating the range, taking out a wall. This is the tier that triggers not just cost, but also permits, engineering, and schedule impacts.

The jump in price between “gut, same layout” and “layout change” is steep, not incremental. If you move a sink, you do not merely add a bit for extra pipe; you enter a different project class, pushing costs to $60,500–$112,000 territory. Decide which tier meets your goals before asking for quotes—a contractor cannot give a meaningful number without this clarity, and guesses in the dark waste everyone’s time.

Allowances, exclusions and change orders

Comparing kitchen quotes in Mifflin County gets tricky because no two are truly alike without digging into the fine print near the top and bottom of each estimate. The biggest source of confusion is allowances—dollar figures placeholders for items not yet chosen. For instance, one bid might set a tile allowance at a realistic figure (say, $1,300), while another plugs in $300. On paper, their totals are similar, but you’ll be hit with thousands in overages down the line if you don’t notice the difference.

Look for clear brand and product lines for cabinets, spelled-out materials and thickness for countertops, and details about electrical (such as which circuits are being added or made GFCI). The quote should spell out how surprises—like opening the wall and finding bad wiring—are handled. Change orders must be approved in writing before the extra work proceeds. Payment schedules should track milestones—like “after cabinet delivery,” not just dates. Finally, always get lien waivers from subs before releasing each payment so you are protected down the line.

Under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA), any job over $500 in Pennsylvania must have a written contract, and you have the right to cancel within three business days. Insist on that documentation upfront.

Why appliance selection comes first

In Mifflin County, getting cabinets built or ordered before you’ve picked your appliances is a recipe for headaches—or cabinets that just don’t fit. That’s because appliances dictate key dimensions. For instance, a counter-depth refrigerator fits nearly flush, but a standard-depth model will protrude—and the cabinet layout will have to be adjusted or it will look like an afterthought. The gap is similar for ranges: slide-in ranges require cabinetry on both sides to finish the look, while freestanding models are more forgiving.

Appliance fuel type matters. Induction or high-output electric ranges often require new or upgraded circuits—no small thing in older Lewistown homes. Gas means either relying on existing supply or running new lines, both of which aren’t always straightforward or even possible. Panel-ready dishwashers require the panel detail to be ordered with cabinets, and oversized or professional-grade fridges need custom panels and reinforcements.

Hood venting is commonly botched. A ducted hood that vents outside is always preferable for real cooking but it means planning for the vent run—sometimes through brick or up two stories. Recirculating hoods are a compromise and should only be used if venting is truly impossible.

With some appliance models, current lead times exceed even cabinets—so sequence your purchases accordingly, or risk a delay costing weeks or even a kitchen that misses the mark.

Where the money actually goes

A full kitchen remodel budget in Mifflin County typically breaks down with one clear winner: cabinetry, which eats up 25–35% of most projects. Labor and install comes next, particularly in older homes with hidden complications or if the layout is changing. Countertops and appliances follow—appliance budgets swing wildly depending on whether you opt for mainstream or premium brands. Flooring, lighting, and plumbing round out the rest.

Most homeowners overlook soft costs and contingency—permitting, design, delivery, disposal, and a reserve for what’s found inside old walls (water damage, faulty wiring). In this county, with so many pre-1980 homes, a 10–15% contingency is basic risk management.

Where is an extra thousand best spent? On sturdy cabinetry or improved lighting—either will last and change daily use. Worst spend? High-end appliances in a room with basic cabinets, or splurging on exotic flooring while skipping proper electrical upgrades.

Who inspects kitchen electrical work in Mifflin County? It’s crucial to check which applies before starting work, especially since electrical upgrades are a common trigger for permit and code compliance in pre-1980 homes. Failing to obtain the required inspection is one of the main reasons home sales get delayed at closing. If your project runs new circuits or upgrades wiring—something especially relevant in older Lewistown homes—the inspector will want to verify grounding, GFCI protection, and proper labeling. For example, a kitchen remodel with new appliance circuits in this area typically falls within the $28,000–$112,000 price range, most of which cannot be closed out without an official inspection.

How does Mifflin County's housing stock affect cabinet fit?
