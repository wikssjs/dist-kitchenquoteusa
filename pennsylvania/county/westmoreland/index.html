Kitchen renovation costs in Westmoreland County — 2026

In Westmoreland County, kitchen renovation costs reflect both the local housing stock and the expectations of mostly owner-occupied homes—over 98,574 households (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). With a typical median household income of $68,938, most homeowners are careful about budget, but projects here often cost more than expected for one reason: the era of construction. Most houses in the county were built between the 1960s and 1980s. This means kitchens frequently involve layout changes, electrical upgrades, or both—not just surface updates. If you’re living in one of these homes, be prepared for surprises behind the walls.

The biggest budget swings in Westmoreland County aren’t from kitchen size, but from scope. The cost difference between a “full gut remodel” and “cabinet replacement only” is enormous. The most common reason budgets are blown is that homeowners price out one tier, then ask for scope from the tier above—like moving the sink or knocking down a wall after starting with “just cabinets.” Know what you want before getting quotes.

Code requirements for Westmoreland County kitchens

In Westmoreland County, code and permit requirements for kitchen renovations depend on the scope of work and your municipality. No permit is generally needed for cosmetic changes—painting, switching out hardware, replacing countertops where plumbing stays put, or cabinet refacing. However, structural wall removal or moving plumbing lines, especially in places like Greensburg, almost always requires a building permit and a formal plan review. Electrical work—such as adding new circuits—is a typical permit trigger, as is any work on gas or ventilation (like installing a new range hood duct through an exterior wall).

Inspections don’t always come directly from your town office. In Greensburg and Hempfield, electrical inspections are commonly done by certified third-party agencies such as Code.sys. This surprises many homeowners, as you’ll coordinate inspections with an outside agency, not the municipality.

Contractors working on jobs over $500 must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General—always verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Employers must carry workers’ compensation insurance; you can check status through the state WCAIS system. Doing work without a required permit can result in fines, trouble at resale, and problems with home insurance—especially if an inspection was required by your municipality or a third-party agency. It’s rarely worth the risk to skip this step.

Defining your scope before you get quotes

The term “kitchen remodel” covers everything from repainting to gutting the room down to studs. Before calling any contractor in Westmoreland County, define which project type you’re after. Here are the clear scope tiers:

  • Cosmetic refresh: Paint, new hardware, switch some lighting or a backsplash—no cabinetry or layout change. It’s the fastest, least disruptive, and makes sense when the bones are good and walls are staying put.
  • Pull and replace: Remove and replace cabinets, countertops, and finishes, but keep plumbing and electrical in their original locations. This tier still avoids most permit costs if appliances and sink stay put and can be completed in weeks, not months.
  • Gut, same layout: Take everything down to the studs, update wiring and plumbing in place, but layout doesn’t change. This makes sense in Westmoreland’s 1960s–1980s houses where you need to update electrical for code, but the room flow still works.
  • Layout change: Move walls, the sink, or the range. Now you’re into permit territory, possible engineering, and a different contractor class. This is the cost cliff—moving a sink can double the complexity and price.

That jump from “gut, same layout” to “layout change” is huge. Moving services or partitions turns a manageable project into a complex one—both in cost and schedule. Decide on your tier before requesting bids. Without this decision, you’ll end up with numbers that don’t compare, and your budget simply will not hold. A contractor can’t meaningfully quote until you’re clear on where the big items are staying.

What moving the sink really costs

The most expensive changes in a Westmoreland County kitchen aren’t from square footage, but from moving plumbing and walls. If you keep the sink, dishwasher, and stove in the same spots, you stay in a certain price and permit tier. Move them—even by a few feet—and you take on not only plumbing rerouting (with its slope and vent requirements), but new electrical, possible slab or finished ceiling work, and in some houses, complicated structural decisions.

Drain lines need proper slope, so if the kitchen sits over a finished basement or a slab, routing can mean cutting concrete or opening multiple ceilings. Add in new electrical circuits, and your panel may need an upgrade to handle the load. If you want to remove a wall, you first have to know if it’s load-bearing, which requires structural review—and always triggers permitting (especially in Greensburg, where plan review is required for service changes).

Relocating a range hood means running ductwork to the exterior, which in older homes is rarely straightforward. Every one of these moves, even if it seems small, pulls the project up to the $62,000–$115,000 level—nearly double the basic gut remodel.

Ask yourself: is the current layout unworkable or just dated? In many Westmoreland County homes, a new finish and better cabinetry goes further, for much less, than a full layout swap. Moving a sink four feet often means spending more than it looks on paper—almost never proportional to the distance moved.

Choosing cabinetry

Cabinet selection drives both the look and the total cost of your kitchen. Here are the real-world choices for Westmoreland County homeowners:

  • Painting existing cabinets: If your boxes are sturdy, with solid wood doors and good hinges, a professional repaint is the lowest-cost dramatic change—but it won’t fix a poor layout or sagging hardware.
  • Refacing: New doors, drawer fronts, and veneer on existing frames. It works if your cabinet boxes are square, solid, and the kitchen’s layout suits you. Refacing is not a solution if you want to rearrange cabinets or add serious storage.
  • Stock cabinets: Cheapest for standard spaces. The downside? Westmoreland County’s 1960s–1980s housing rarely has dead-square corners or truly standard wall runs—you’ll need filler strips, and your finishes are limited.
  • Semi-custom and custom: Flexibility in sizing and style lets you work around non-standard rooms (common here), add tricky corner cabinets, or exactly match the design you want. Lead times can be long, and prices reflect the tailoring.

No matter the type, look for plywood (not particleboard) boxes, dovetail or doweled drawers (not just stapled), and soft-close hardware. Frameless cabinets give a bit more storage and a modern look, but either works if made well. Westmoreland County’s older homes often force the move to semi-custom simply because standard boxes rarely fit well after a few decades of shifting or settling walls and odd dimensions.

What a kitchen quote should specify

Kitchen quotes in Westmoreland County vary less from “what things cost” than from “how many things are specified.” Allowances are where apples-to-apples comparison breaks down: they’re placeholders for items you haven’t picked yet—tile, plumbing fixtures, even appliances. If one contractor plugs in $800 total for tile and another puts in $200, your bottom line is off by hundreds before you’ve chosen anything. Pay attention to these lines before comparing totals.

The quote should name cabinet brands, box construction, and hardware, not just “new cabinets.” Counters need to show material (quartz, granite, etc.), thickness, and edge profile. The way unforeseen conditions (like hidden water damage or illegal wiring) are handled must be written. Change orders—any changes after work starts—should require written approval, and the payment schedule should tie to work milestones (like “cabinets installed”), not just calendar dates. Lien waivers from trades protect you from later legal claims.

In Pennsylvania, any contract over $500 must be written under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, and you have three business days to cancel. Make sure all details are in writing before you sign.

How a Westmoreland County kitchen budget breaks down

Most homeowners are surprised by what eats up the kitchen budget. Cabinetry is almost always the single largest line (often 25%–35% of total cost), with labour close behind, especially in older homes where nothing is square. Countertops, appliances, flooring, lighting, and plumbing follow. But two overlooked areas really matter: an honest contingency for what’s likely behind the walls (especially in the 72.5% of homes built before 1980), and soft costs for permits, design, delivery, and debris—these add up quickly in Westmoreland County.

If you have an “extra” thousand to allocate, prioritize better drawers, cabinet hardware, and lighting before flooring or fixtures—these have the biggest daily impact. A splurge on appliances while saving on cabinets rarely pays off; a $6,700–$28,500 cabinet package makes more difference than a “pro” range with budget cabinetry surrounding it.

Quartz, granite, laminate or butcher block

Choosing a countertop is about much more than appearance. Quartz is an engineered stone, non-porous and stain-resistant, but it does not tolerate heat—setting a hot pan down can leave a permanent mark. Granite is natural, beautiful, and heat-resistant, but it requires periodic sealing to stay stain-free. Laminate has come a long way—durable, low-maintenance, and easy to install at a much lower price, it’s the right choice for many Westmoreland County kitchens, especially rentals or secondary homes. Don’t let anyone shame you out of it. Butcher block brings warmth and will show its wear—maintenance is needed, but sanding and oiling restore the surface.

Edge profiles, cutouts (for sinks/cooktops), and where seams land all affect price; a complicated template with miters and waterfall sides costs more to fabricate and install. A professional countertop job starts with a templating visit, not just a measurement—this ensures a tight fit, especially in houses where nothing is square.

Solid surface (like Corian) can be renewed if damaged but isn’t as popular here as quartz or granite. The honest answer: if you want something forgiving, low-maintenance, and budget-friendly, laminate is a good decision. For resale or daily heavy use, quartz or granite can make sense if the rest of the kitchen is at the same quality level.

For example, in Greensburg, any structural wall removal or changing of plumbing lines typically mandates a permit and a formal plan review. Ignoring this can cause issues when you eventually sell the home or try to file an insurance claim. While these requirements may seem like a hassle, the risks of skipping permits overshadow the cost—cutting corners can lead to fines, failed inspections, or expensive corrections. Always budget at least a few hundred dollars inside your total project price of $28,500–$115,000 for permit fees and inspections, particularly in the more tightly regulated municipalities.

How do I verify a kitchen contractor in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, any contractor working on jobs over $500 must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the state Office of Attorney General. To check, use the online registry at hic.attorneygeneral.gov—it’s public and takes minutes. You can look up by company, individual, or registration number. For Westmoreland County, this is not just paperwork: a current registration signals that the contractor is insured, legal, and subject to Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. Also verify active workers’ compensation coverage for any crew who will be on site—use the state WCAIS system. For extra protection, ask whether subcontractors are separately insured. Local references, verified insurance, and a written contract are critical safeguards.

Butcher block is warm but will show its wear over time. If resale is in mind, quartz or granite are safe bets, though solid surface materials (like Corian) have loyal fans due to repairability.
