Kitchen renovation costs in Delaware County — 2026

In Delaware County, kitchen renovations are shaped as much by local housing as by individual taste. With a median household income of $98,058 and 133,802 owner-occupied households (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), most homeowners weigh kitchen budgets carefully. The majority of homes here were built before 1980, with many dating to 1920–1960: that means original galley kitchens and non-standard walls, which can make simple upgrades tricky and layout changes more common.

The biggest pitfall? Pricing for a cabinet swap but choosing a full layout change halfway through. Always make sure the scope you’re pricing matches what you actually want to achieve.

Three ways to organise the project

Before picking cabinets or tiles, decide who is running your Delaware County kitchen remodel. Most homeowners choose one of three paths: design-build, general contractor, or owner-managed.

A design-build kitchen firm handles everything: design, materials, and installation, all under one contract. This brings the highest cost, but hands off the headaches — it’s the go-to for anyone who needs accountability and doesn’t want to juggle multiple trades. The general contractor model is the middle ground: the GC lines up and schedules trades, but you (sometimes with a designer) make the product and layout choices. This saves some cost but still requires decisions, approvals, and occasional on-site troubleshooting.

Owner-managed projects are cheapest up front but carry the most risk and hassle. You hire each licensed trade, order materials, schedule deliveries, and keep everyone on track. In Delaware County’s busy contractor market — especially in inner-ring suburbs like Upper Darby and Haverford — skilled crews are plentiful, but sequencing can still fall apart if trades are delayed.

If you work full-time and your project will need a permit (for new circuits, plumbing, or walls), managing it yourself is rarely worth the stress or risk. The money you save can evaporate the first week a plumber is double-booked or a township inspection holds things up. In tight labor markets like some western townships, delays are more common and good subs are busy.

Permits and inspections for a Delaware County kitchen

Permits are not one-size-fits-all in Delaware County. Cosmetic work — like painting, swapping hardware, cabinet refacing, and direct countertop replacement — typically does not require a permit in local townships. But the moment you start moving or adding electrical circuits, relocating plumbing, or touching structural walls, a permit comes into play. In Haverford Township, for example, new circuits or wall moves in a kitchen remodel mean a permit and plan review process with the building department.

Different municipalities within Delaware County run their own building departments, so check specific requirements before starting. Many townships will require you to submit plans if you’re making structural, plumbing, or electrical changes. Inspections are a key part of the process: in many areas, electrical inspections are not performed by township employees, but by township-approved third-party agencies. This can surprise homeowners used to direct municipal involvement and may add a few days to scheduling. Expect final sign-off before any kitchen is legally “finished.”

All contractors must be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for home improvement work over $500. Look them up at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. By law, every employer must provide workers’ compensation insurance — this is checked via WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Skipping permits or hiring an unregistered contractor can backfire: unpermitted work frequently delays or even blocks home sales in Delaware County when municipal records do not match what’s in the house.

Choosing cabinetry

Cabinets make up the single largest line item in most Delaware County kitchen budgets — and the biggest driver of both appearance and cost. Start with the right scope: painting, refacing, replacement, or custom.

Refacing means replacing doors, drawer fronts, and applying new veneer to your existing boxes. It’s a real budget-saver and can look excellent, but only if the boxes are sturdy and you’re happy with the existing kitchen layout. You can’t add storage, fix awkward corners, or move a fridge with refacing alone.

Stock cabinets are built in fixed sizes and most affordable, but Delaware County’s 1920–1960 housing stock means walls often aren’t square or standard — so stock cabinets often need filler strips or leave awkward gaps. Semi-custom cabinets fill the middle ground, allowing some resize for width or depth and broader finish choices. Custom cabinets are built to fit any situation and any finish, but lead times and prices both go up fast.

Regardless of type, check box materials: plywood is stronger and lasts longer than particleboard. Dovetail drawer construction beats stapled every time. Full-extension soft-close hardware is no longer a luxury; insist on it. Frameless cabinets give more space in small galley kitchens (common here), but face-frame is still standard and often more forgiving in older homes.

Cabinets are the critical path

In Delaware County kitchens, timelines are controlled by cabinet and countertop sequencing — not by how many trades are on site. The process starts with design and careful measurement, especially in older homes where nothing is square. The next step is ordering cabinets. Demolition comes after the cabinet order, not before: long lead times (especially for semi-custom or custom) mean you never want to be living without a kitchen while waiting on delivery.

Once demolition is finished, plumbers and electricians rough in the new layout, followed by township or third-party inspections as required. Only then are cabinets installed. Here’s the catch: countertops can only be templated — that is, precisely measured for fabrication — after the cabinets are in place and secured. Even with high contractor density in central and eastern Delaware County, expect a 1–2 week gap while the counters are templated and fabricated. This week of waiting is frustratingly invisible in most schedules but is always there, even with diligent planning.

Flooring, backsplash tile, and trim are installed last. In every case, ordering cabinets up front is the best way to beat delays. Waiting until after demolition just means more time eating in the basement. In older or non-standard kitchens, expect semi-custom lead times of 6–12 weeks — every day saved on the front end shaves a day off the total downtime.

Why appliance selection comes first

In every Delaware County kitchen renovation, appliance choices need to happen first — before any cabinet order is placed. That’s because your cabinets and countertop dimensions depend on the exact sizes of your fridge, range, dishwasher, and hood. If you pick appliances afterward, you risk costly do-overs or compromises that never look quite right.

Refrigerators come standard or counter-depth — counter-depth models integrate seamlessly with cabinetry, but they cost more and hold less. Ranges can be freestanding or slide-in; the latter needs a precisely measured cabinet gap. Gas versus induction or electric? Switching fuels can require dedicated circuits or upgraded gas lines, and induction nearly always means new wiring. Panel-ready dishwashers allow for full cabinet integration but must be ordered with the cabinet package.

For hoods, vented models (exhausted to the outside) remove smoke and odors far better than recirculating models; the latter are always a compromise and only suitable when venting is impractical in older Delaware County homes. Professional-grade appliances bring robust performance and distinctive looks, but for most, the extra spend is more about aesthetics or “chef’s kitchen” resale appeal. And remember: some appliance models have delivery waits as long or longer than semi-custom cabinets — plan ahead, and always lock appliance specs before placing a cabinet order.

Eight weeks without a kitchen

The reality of a Delaware County kitchen remodel is weeks without normal cooking or cleanup. Plan on setting up a temporary space: the fridge gets moved to the dining room or garage, while a microwave, coffee maker, and kettle live on a folding table. Most families end up using a powder room or laundry tub for washing dishes, even if it’s awkward. Stock up on paper plates and prepared foods — your food budget will spike, often costing more than the kitchen flooring.

Protect adjacent rooms by taping up plastic sheeting or zip walls; dust travels fast, especially in open layouts or older homes with less insulation. Expect dumpster placement (driveway or curb) and some material staging outdoors or in a garage — consider neighbors and HOA rules. Contractors will need the door propped open for loading and ventilation, so pets and children need to be kept well away. If demolition is extensive, especially in a small house, consider staying elsewhere for the first week. The mess, noise, and lack of plumbing get old quickly. Add the honest cost: eating out or takeout, for a family, can easily exceed the cost of a new kitchen floor over the 8-week stretch.

Renovating to live in versus renovating to sell

It’s true that a new kitchen helps a Delaware County home sell quicker. But it rarely returns its full cost, especially if you build to your own taste and to a price point higher than the neighborhood supports. Most mid-range kitchen projects in Delaware County recover a solid portion of their investment when selling, simply by improving day-to-day appeal and function. High-end or luxury renovations typically cost more than the next buyer is willing to pay, and the excess is not recouped, especially if the median household income in your area, like $98,058 here, is exceeded by a wide margin.

If you’re renovating to sell, the project should be scoped differently: pick neutral colors, mid-range fixtures and surfaces, and skip major layout reconfigurations. This reduces both your upfront spend and the risk of alienating buyers. If, on the other hand, you plan to live in your Delaware County home for a decade or more, it makes sense to invest in quality and layout changes that fit your lifestyle, regardless of resale math. Just know that what works for a Stonehurst duplex does not always pay off on closing day when aiming for Radnor Township resale prices.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Delaware County

Can I over-improve for my Delaware County neighbourhood? Home values and resale prices are very neighborhood-driven in Delaware County, especially in areas with a high concentration of homes built before 1980. Spending more than typical — such as putting a $123,000 kitchen into a modest home with lower comparables — rarely pays off at sale. Most buyers will not cover the excess, meaning you’ll enjoy the upgrade but shouldn’t count on recouping it. Align your kitchen budget with surrounding home values and focus on layout and finishes that are still in demand, even a decade from now. It’s smarter to invest up to the high end for kitchens in higher-value neighborhoods, but even then, restraint is wise.
How far ahead should I book a contractor in Delaware County? Delaware County’s contractor market can be competitive, especially in popular suburbs like Upper Darby and Haverford. For a full gut kitchen remodel in the $31,000–$123,000 range, the best contractors are often booked out three to six months, or longer for custom work. Lead times are driven by labor availability, particularly in western townships where good tradespeople have tight schedules. For cabinet refacing or swap projects, scheduling can be faster, but you should still expect a 4–8 week wait from final design approval to project kickoff. Start conversations with contractors well before you want the work to start, especially if your renovation involves permits or plan review.
How much more does moving the sink and range cost? In Delaware County, rerouting plumbing and electrical for a new sink or range layout typically adds several thousand dollars to your project. On average, a kitchen gut remodel with layout change ranges from $67,000–$123,000, while a same-layout gut job starts at $31,000. The additional cost covers new plumbing lines, rewiring for appliances, wall repairs, and sometimes a permit and extra inspections. This is common in smaller, older kitchens originally laid out for minimal appliances — moving major plumbing or electrical is rarely cheap, so it’s only worth it if the new layout solves real usability problems.
How much does a kitchen renovation cost in Delaware County? Most homeowners in Delaware County see kitchen remodels ranging from $31,000–$123,000 for a full gut, with typical projects landing near $67,000. Basic cabinet replacement can fall between $7,200–$31,000, while simple refacing typically costs less. The wide cost band comes from differences in materials, layout changes, and labor rates (which can be higher in certain towns). Always request itemized bids and clarify if appliance purchases and flooring are included, as those are big variables. Older homes here mean surprises are common — set aside a contingency fund.
Does replacing cabinets require a permit? Across Delaware County, straightforward cabinet replacement or refacing does not usually require a permit, as long as the kitchen layout stays the same and you are not moving plumbing, electrical, or structural elements. As soon as you add new electrical circuits or change walls — common in a full remodel in the $31,000–$123,000 range — most local building departments will want to review your plans and issue a permit. Always check with your township, as requirements vary, but for the majority of simple cabinet upgrades, you will not need to involve local officials.
Is lead paint a concern in Delaware County kitchen renovations? Yes, lead paint is a real concern in Delaware County, where most homes were built before 1978. If your kitchen remodel involves disturbing old painted surfaces — walls, trim, or windows — contractors are required by federal law to follow EPA Lead RRP protocols, using dust control and cleanup methods that protect your family and workers. A minor refacing project, such as $7,200–$20,500, may avoid major disturbance, but gut renovations almost always require lead-safe work practices. Always ask your contractor for their EPA certification and review how they’ll handle lead dust management.
How do I set up a temporary kitchen? During a Delaware County kitchen project, living without a kitchen for 6 to 10 weeks (full gut remodel) is standard. Most homeowners relocate the fridge to the dining room, set up a microwave and coffee maker on a folding table, and wash dishes in a powder room or laundry tub. Disposable plates and utensils help, though the cost for take-out and prepared food quickly adds up, often rivaling your flooring budget (in the $5,100–$20,500 range). Tape up plastic sheeting for dust control and keep pets away from work zones, as doors may be propped open daily. Plan for noise, mess, and some inconvenience—especially in older, compact homes.
Can I cancel a renovation contract in Pennsylvania? Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) gives you the right to cancel any contract over $500 within three business days of signing, with no penalty. This applies directly to kitchen renovations in Delaware County, whether you’re refacing cabinets for $7,200–$20,500 or gutting the whole room. Always ensure you receive a written contract that includes the cancellation terms, contractor registration info, and a clear project scope. Never let a contractor start work — or take a large deposit — before this period has elapsed. This right is statewide and cannot be waived.
What is an allowance in a renovation quote? An allowance is a placeholder dollar figure in your contract for an item not yet selected—such as countertops, tile, or lighting. For example, a kitchen remodel in Delaware County might include a countertop allowance of $9,200; spend more and you pay the difference, choose something less and receive a credit. Allowances help start work while you finalize selections but can cause surprises if the number is too low for your taste. Always clarify allowances with your contractor and update as items are chosen, ensuring the allowed amounts align with local market pricing.
Can I renovate a kitchen in stages? Yes, many Delaware County homeowners choose to renovate in stages—starting with countertop swap ($4,100–$16,500), then replacing appliances or flooring later. This can spread out costs and minimize disruption, but it often raises the total price, since trades have to mobilize multiple times. Staged work makes most sense for cosmetic upgrades or when you’re waiting for funds. The trade-off is that something is usually always “in progress,” so project fatigue is real. For any major scope change (walls, electrical, plumbing) it’s far more efficient to remodel all at once.
Is laminate countertop a bad choice? Laminate countertops are practical and cost-effective in Delaware County, especially in starter homes or rental units. While not as trendy as quartz, a new laminate top can look sharp and bring your kitchen into the present for a fraction of the price (vs. stone options up to $16,500). Laminate resists most staining and cleans easily, but it can be damaged by heat or hard impacts, and it doesn’t add significant value at resale. If you’re planning to stay five years or less, or need to control costs, laminate is a perfectly good choice for many households.
