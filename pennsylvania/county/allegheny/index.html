What a kitchen remodel costs in Allegheny County

Allegheny County homeowners plan their kitchen budgets around local home values—anchored by a median household income of $85,758 and a strong owner-occupied rate of 316,359 households (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). In neighborhoods across Pittsburgh and its suburbs, the prevalence of pre-1940 homes means many kitchens still carry old layouts—small, cut up, and starved for light or workspace. If you’re living in one of these houses, a gut renovation with layout changes is more common here than in newer developments, and it’s why kitchen projects locally can run the full spectrum of cost bands.

Most homeowners run into unexpected overruns when they plan for one level of work—like a simple cabinet swap—and wind up adding scope, such as moving plumbing or opening up walls, which bumps the project into a much higher price tier. Knowing where your project fits on the ladder below is the single clearest step you can take to get an accurate cost at the start.

Cabinets are the critical path

In Allegheny County, especially with continuous kitchen demand in Pittsburgh and its close-in suburbs, the biggest scheduling risk is lead times for cabinets and countertops. The real timeline starts at the design and measurement phase. Once the layout is set and materials chosen, cabinets must be ordered—often with an 8- to 12-week lead time, especially for semi-custom units, which are common here because of the non-standard wall dimensions in older homes. Don’t make the mistake of starting demolition before cabinets arrive or even before they’re ordered: you’ll be living in a construction zone and waiting on cabinets, with your kitchen unusable and the contractor unable to proceed.

Here’s how the chain looks: design and measure; place cabinet order; when your delivery date is certain, begin demolition; complete rough-ins for plumbing and electrical, then call for inspections if the scope requires them; only then can cabinets be set. As soon as cabinets are installed, the countertop company will come to template—because they cannot use a drawing, only actual cabinets set in place. This triggers what surprises most homeowners: a week or more of dead time before counters are fabricated and installed. Nobody works during this week, and you can’t skip it.

Urban and inner-suburban demand here means fabricator and installer schedules run tight, so a blown cabinet delivery date can quickly add weeks. Always get your cabinet lead times in writing and ask your contractor to schedule demolition accordingly. In this market, everything else—flooring, tile, final trim—waits on cabinets and counters to land first. If you want the shortest duration and least disruption, insist on ordering cabinets first, and never accept demolition as a way to “get started” while you wait.

Four kinds of kitchen project, four budgets

Not every “kitchen remodel” means the same thing—or anywhere near the same cost. In Allegheny County, you’ll see everything from basic paint-and-pull projects to wall-to-wall gut jobs in the same block. To avoid blowing your budget or wasting time with conflicting estimates, start by identifying which project tier you’re really planning:

  • Cosmetic refresh: Paint, new hardware, a backsplash, and lighting. No cabinet, counter, or layout changes. Quick and affordable, but no improvement in function.
  • Pull and replace: Swap cabinets and counters in the exact same layout. Plumbing and electrical stay in place. The fastest route to a dramatically improved kitchen without permits or engineering.
  • Gut, same layout: Strip to the studs, replace systems (wiring, plumbing), but everything goes back where it was. Covers musty old walls and surprise wiring, but doesn't alter traffic flow or workspace.
  • Layout change: Move walls, plumbing, or gas. This tier—most common in Pittsburgh's pre-1940 homes—triggers engineering, plan review, and permits. The cost jump between gutting and reconfiguring is steep because of code, inspections, and skilled labor.

The budget cliff is at tier four. Moving a sink or opening a wall is what changes both cost and process dramatically, turning a straightforward replacement into a project requiring permits, designer drawings, and sometimes multiple trades. Decide which scope you’re after before calling contractors, because no reputable pro can give you meaningful numbers unless you know (and share) your real goals. If you try to “see some prices first,” you’ll wind up disappointed or over budget later.

Permits and inspections for a Allegheny County kitchen

In Allegheny County, permit requirements come down to the scope and your municipality. Cosmetic kitchen work—painting, hardware swaps, cabinet refacing, and countertop replacement that leaves the fixtures in place—is usually permit-free in most boroughs. But the moment you plan to move plumbing, rewire circuits, pull a wall, or install a high-CFM hood, you’re likely in permit territory, especially within Pittsburgh city limits, where plan review by the Department of Permits, Licenses, and Inspections is routine for full remodels that alter layout or structure.

Inspections can be handled by municipal building departments or (in some suburbs) by a third-party agency for specialty inspections like electrical. In Pittsburgh itself, electrical permitting and inspection is handled directly under city permitting authority, while around the county, individual boroughs and townships manage their own process. This patchwork means checking with your municipal office rather than relying on a county-wide rule.

Your contractor must hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for any job over $500—verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If their business employs labor, they must carry workers’ compensation insurance (verify at WCAIS, wcais.pa.gov). The law also requires a written contract for work over $500 and grants you a three-day right to cancel after signing. Skipping permits where needed can block a future sale, as unpermitted work is often flagged during transfer, holding up the closing and sometimes forcing expensive retroactive inspections or remediation.

The biggest line item in your Allegheny County kitchen

Your cabinet decision—what kind, how many, what grade—will determine as much as a third of your total kitchen budget and most of your project’s lead time. In Allegheny County, especially in older Pittsburgh neighborhoods where wall dimensions rarely match modern standards, truly stock cabinets often don’t fit right, pushing many homeowners into the semi-custom band for both function and appearance, whether they planned it or not.

Refacing is the value play where the bones are good—you keep the boxes, replace doors and fronts, and apply veneer for a fresh look. This works great if your cabinets are sturdy, square, and still laid out the way you want. You won’t gain any new function, but you’ll save substantial money and time. Painting is even cheaper, but it won’t fix worn hardware or dated profiles.

Stock cabinets come in fixed increments and standardized layouts, which works for 1990s and newer homes but often leads to awkward filler strips in prewar Pittsburgh kitchens. Semi-custom gives more flexibility in sizing, finishes, and storage options and is the go-to when dealing with oddball room dimensions. Full custom fills any space but brings the highest price tag and lead time.

Construction quality matters at every price. Look for plywood boxes (not just particleboard), dovetail drawer joints, and full-extension, soft-close hardware. Face-frame cabinets are common here for traditional looks, while frameless styles offer a sleeker, more modern finish. Old rooms with out-of-square walls and floors—abundant in the city—generally demand semi-custom cabinets for fit and finish.

Allocating a kitchen budget

When Allegheny County homeowners see a kitchen allocation breakdown, it often upends their priorities—especially when they notice what lines really drive the cost. Cabinets are your single biggest category, soaking up 25–35% of the budget, because they’re the most labor- and material-intensive element and control the timeline. Labor and installation come next, with these numbers climbing fast if you’re changing the kitchen’s layout or tackling one of the area’s 73% pre-1980 homes (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), where demolition often uncovers hidden issues.

Stone countertops and appliances swing wildly depending on the level of finish. Budget builds tend to skimp on lighting, fixtures, and flooring, but in older homes, you can’t afford to neglect electrical and plumbing—the hidden systems that make everything else possible. Two categories that regularly trip up homeowners: contingency (for hidden wiring, water damage, or framing problems) and the “soft costs” of permits, design, delivery, and disposal. Both need real money behind them. Allocate an honest 10–15% to contingency, especially in a 1940s or older house, or a bigger share if you’re opening walls.

Where does an extra $1,000 do the most? Usually, in cabinetry upgrades or extra lighting. Where does it disappear with no visible benefit? Fancy appliances in a basic-stock kitchen. Don’t be talked into a $6,000 range unless your cabinets and layout justify it.

Circuits, GFCI and code compliance

In Allegheny County, the single biggest source of surprise expense mid-kitchen renovation—especially in pre-1980 homes—is electrical code compliance. Modern kitchens must have at least two dedicated 20-amp small-appliance circuits, GFCI protection at countertop outlets, code-compliant spacing (outlets no more than 4 feet apart), and dedicated circuits for dishwasher, disposal, and microwave. Electric ranges require a 240V circuit, absent from many older kitchens.

The reality in Pittsburgh’s older housing stock is bleak: shared kitchen and lighting circuits, knob-and-tube or two-wire ungrounded wiring, sometimes even aluminum branch circuits from mid-century updates, and main panels already full. Once you open the walls (as with a gut remodel), inspectors expect current code throughout the new work—it’s mandatory, not optional, under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code. This is particularly common in gut renovations to reconfigure small, compartmentalized Pittsburgh kitchens, where opening the walls reveals outdated wiring that must be replaced or reconfigured entirely to bring in dedicated circuits with enough amperage.

Allow $5,000–$15,000 for a full electrical upgrade in an older Allegheny County kitchen. This covers new circuitry, GFCIs, new breakers or service upgrades, and pairing everything off for final inspection—a not infrequent outcome in houses built before 1940 and those little “quickie” 1970s kitchen additions that still only have one or two circuits.

Practical survival during the work

Once the real work starts, daily life in an Allegheny County kitchen renovation gets messy fast. Plan a temporary kitchen—put the fridge somewhere easily accessed, use a folding table for a microwave, toaster, and kettle. If your laundry is on the same floor, a utility sink can double as a dishwashing station; otherwise, it’s bathroom sinks and a lot of paper plates. Expect your food budget to tick up: eating out (even “just for a few meals a week”) adds up during the 6-to-10-week span of a full gut project and is often a bigger line than the new flooring.

Dust control matters more than you think, especially in Pittsburgh’s older homes. Encourage your contractor to set up plastic sheeting with zippers or magnetic closures, and expect floors and vents to be sealed off outside the workspace. Ask where the dumpster sits (driveways go fast) and where the crew will stage materials—it’s easy to block access to other parts of the house for weeks by accident. If you have pets, be ready: doors stay open as crews move in and out, and sharp debris is on the ground most days.

Decide up front if you want to move out for the demolition and rough-in week. If you can stay with family or friends, you’ll get a break from dust and noise; otherwise, prepare for daily disruption. Be honest about your limits, and build a small buffer for takeout or groceries into your budget from the start.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Allegheny County

Do I need a permit for a kitchen renovation in Allegheny County?

Whether you need a permit depends heavily on your scope and where you live within Allegheny County. If your project is limited to painting, replacing appliances with same-type units, refacing cabinets, or swapping out countertops—without moving plumbing, electrical, or demolishing walls—most boroughs do not require a permit. However, in the City of Pittsburgh, almost any work that moves plumbing, electrical, or alters the layout (like knocking down a wall or moving a sink) will trigger a permit and often a formal plan review process. For full gut remodels that make these changes, expect to need a permit and to have the project inspected by the city department or designated inspection agency. Skipping permits can lead to trouble when you sell, as unpermitted work may be flagged during resale and result in costly remediation or delays.

What is a realistic contingency for a Allegheny County kitchen?

In Allegheny County—where 73.1% of homes covered are pre-1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024)—a realistic contingency line for a kitchen renovation sits at 10–15% of the total project cost. For a typical full gut kitchen costing $65,000, you want at least $6,500–$9,750 set aside for surprises behind the walls. These can include hidden water damage, old wiring, undersized framing, asbestos in old flooring, or uneven subfloors—issues especially common in Pittsburgh’s older housing stock. If you’re starting with a quick “pull and replace” and you know your home has already been updated, you might get away with less, but in any pre-1980 property in the region, skipping a contingency is asking for trouble. Most mid-project overruns are from things you can’t see during the estimate walk-through.

What happens if previous work was unpermitted?

If unpermitted kitchen work was done in your Allegheny County home—especially in cities like Pittsburgh with active inspection regimes—you may face issues at resale, refinance, or when pulling new permits. During a sale, home inspectors and attorneys often flag obvious work (like a finished kitchen in a prewar house) with no permit history. Remediation can mean paying for retroactive plan review, nuisance fines, partial demolition to expose and check hidden wiring or plumbing, or even being required to bring work up to current code. This can become expensive quickly, especially if your kitchen’s value tier was $30,000–$120,000, as inspectors expect work at that level to meet today’s standards. It’s best not to gamble: verify permits were pulled, and if you’re unsure, check with your local borough or the City of Pittsburgh office before beginning new work.

Does a kitchen renovation increase home value in Allegheny County?

In Allegheny County, where homeownership is high and much of the housing stock is pre-1980, a well-planned kitchen renovation typically improves resale value and marketability. Modern buyers in neighborhoods like Squirrel Hill and Mount Lebanon expect updated kitchens; in densely built Pittsburgh city zones, gut renovations are not just cosmetic—they solve real layout problems. While you may not recoup every dollar of a $65,000 gut, homes with fresh, code-compliant kitchens have a clear edge in both time-on-market and sale price. Just avoid “over-improving”: a six-figure kitchen with stock cabinets in a modest house is unlikely to yield a full return. Matching project scope to neighborhood standards is key for value.

What does a full gut kitchen remodel cost in Allegheny County?

The range for a full gut kitchen remodel in Allegheny County runs from $30,000–$120,000, with most typical projects tracking around $65,000. This price covers gutting everything to the studs, replacing all surfaces and finishes, updating plumbing and electrical to code, installing new cabinets, counters, appliances, flooring, and fixtures. The big swing depends on size, layout changes, cabinet grade, and local labor supply—which in Pittsburgh and nearby townships runs tighter due to steady demand. Moving structural walls or making major layout changes pushes you toward the high end of the band. Always budget for contingency, as older homes frequently require infrastructure upgrades once the walls are open.

How do I set up a temporary kitchen?

During a kitchen remodel in Allegheny County, daily living can be rough if you aren’t prepared. Set aside a space—often a laundry, basement, or dining room—where you can put your fridge, a folding table, a microwave, and an electric kettle or toaster. Use a utility or bathroom sink for washing; stock up on paper plates, cups, and utensils. Dust control is crucial, so keep temporary kitchens out of the main traffic flow if possible. Expect to spend more on groceries and takeout: over a 6–10 week gut renovation, your food costs may rival the low end of the flooring band, $5,000–$7,000. Plan for these expenses up front—they’re an unavoidable part of staying sane during a full kitchen redo.

Should I reface or replace my kitchen cabinets?

Refacing makes the most sense in Allegheny County homes when your cabinet boxes are sturdy, square, and laid out as you want them. If you’re happy with the overall kitchen flow and just want a visual update, refacing saves time and money—often running $7,000–$20,000—with minimal disruption. But in most Pittsburgh-area pre-1940 homes, kitchens are being gutted not just because of wear, but to reorganize cramped or poor layouts, and refacing can’t solve that. If you want to add storage, change the footprint, or deal with off-square rooms common in city houses, cabinet replacement—stock or, more often, semi-custom—delivers much more flexibility. The right answer depends on your goals and the condition of what’s already there.

Is laminate countertop a bad choice?

Laminate countertops in Allegheny County still make sense for kitchens on a strict budget, rental units, or when rapid turnaround is needed. They’re cost-effective—down at the $4,000–$6,000 end for countertop replacement only—and offer plenty of finishes. However, in neighborhoods like Squirrel Hill or Mount Lebanon, where mid- to high-end projects are common, most homeowners opt for stone or solid-surface materials for both durability and resale value. Laminate won’t boost your marketability the way quartz or granite will in a gut remodel, but it isn’t a “bad” choice—just a budget-driven one. If you’re staying in the house and watch your knives and heat, you’ll get years of use from a quality laminate top.

What are cabinet lead times in Allegheny County right now?

With strong ongoing demand in Allegheny County, especially in Pittsburgh and inner suburbs, cabinet lead times for semi-custom or custom models are typically running 8–12 weeks from order to delivery. Stock options can come faster—sometimes 2–4 weeks if in local inventory. Gut renovations in old homes usually require at least semi-custom for a good fit, so most projects locally see longer waits. Always confirm your cabinet order date and expected delivery window in writing before demolition. If your schedule is tight (for example, you’re planning a $7,000–$30,000 cabinet replacement with minimal downtime), prioritize suppliers that can meet your timeline and avoid companies known for backlogs.

Is lead paint a concern in Allegheny County kitchen renovations?

If your Allegheny County home was built before 1978—which is true for a significant share of housing in Pittsburgh and its oldest suburbs—lead paint is very likely present in older kitchen walls, window trim, and even cabinets. Under the federal EPA Lead RRP Rule, any contractor disturbing painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes must be certified and follow strict dust containment and cleanup procedures. Always verify your contractor’s compliance at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov, especially if kids or pregnant women are living in the house during the work. Failing to observe the rules isn’t just a legal liability—it can create long-term health risks. Addressing lead concerns may add to the cost, so for a full gut kitchen in a pre-1978 home, keep extra in your contingency line, possibly at the higher end of the $30,000–$120,000 range.

Should I hire a general contractor or a kitchen firm?

In Allegheny County, both options are common and the right choice depends on your project. A kitchen-specialty firm is often best for straightforward “pull and replace” or refacing projects—jobs like a $7,000–$20,000 door-and-veneer upgrade or one-to-one cabinet replacement. If you’re gutting the kitchen, moving walls, rerouting plumbing, or dealing with an old Pittsburgh house that needs active project management across multiple trades, a general contractor’s experience becomes vital. They’ll coordinate permits, plan review, and specialized subs for everything from structural framing to electrical upgrades, all while keeping the sequence on track. On higher-end or complicated jobs, a GC keeps you from getting overwhelmed and helps shore up gaps if, say, a tile or electrical subcontractor falls through. Either way, confirm HIC registration and insurance before signing a contract.
