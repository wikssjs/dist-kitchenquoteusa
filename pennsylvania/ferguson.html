Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovations often trigger permitting when you change anything “mechanical,” especially electrical circuits or plumbing locations. Pennsylvania also requires Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for many home improvement contracts over $500 (see hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work in particular, a licensed PA electrician must install or modify any circuits; you can verify credentials through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing, sink, dishwasher hookup, and any relocation of supply/drain lines must be performed by or supervised by a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are typically required in most Ferguson area municipalities when your project involves plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal/structural changes. Cosmetic work—like paint, hardware swaps, and cabinet refacing where you are not disturbing the electrical/plumbing—often does not require a permit.

Lead paint is another Pennsylvania requirement that matters in older Ferguson homes. If your home was built before 1978, lead-based paint may be present on surfaces disturbed during a gut remodel. EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required for contractors disturbing lead paint surfaces; verify certification before work begins (certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov).

To protect yourself, verify the PA contractor’s credentials and coverage step-by-step: (1) confirm HIC registration online, (2) request the electrician/plumber license numbers when they’re part of the scope, (3) obtain proof of general liability insurance, and (4) provide workers’ compensation proof (WC) before demolition. Then insist the contract is written for work over $500 under the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, including the 3-day right of cancellation.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Ferguson?

With about 36.9% of Ferguson’s housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many homeowners are staring at cabinets that are worn visually, not always structurally. Cabinet refacing—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and applying veneer to the existing box—can be an especially smart middle step when the cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. In Centre County’s market, refacing commonly costs about 40–60% less than full replacement because you’re saving on demolition, new box installation, and much of the labor tied to aligning a whole new cabinet system.

But refacing is not a fit for every Ferguson kitchen. If your boxes are swollen from prior moisture, warped at the base, sagging on one run, or out of square due to years of use, refacing tends to lock in problems—especially around doors that won’t close evenly. Also, refacing won’t meaningfully change storage function. If you want deeper drawers, different pull-out layouts, a new pantry configuration, or a major layout shift, replacement is usually the better value.

Here’s a concrete example: a homeowner choosing refacing at roughly $2,500–$8,000 can justify that approach when the kitchen is otherwise sound and only the face needs modernization. In contrast, if the project scope includes new countertops and flooring and your cabinet boxes are already coming off the wall for repairs, spending closer to a replacement range like $8,000–$35,000 can be justified because it reduces the risk of repeating cabinet issues later—particularly important in older PA homes where plumbing leaks or under-sink humidity have sometimes left long-term damage.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Ferguson

Choosing the right contractor in Ferguson usually comes down to verifying licensing, getting itemized pricing, and matching the contractor’s process to the realities of older Centre County homes. Start by verifying IICRC certification and PA licensing. For PA licensing, check the appropriate registries (including dli.pa.gov for electrical and licensing for plumbing roles as applicable). For IICRC, confirm the credential through the organization’s listing and ask the contractor to provide certificate details relevant to your scope. Then, request 2–3 itemized written quotes—ideally with a materials and labor breakdown (for cabinets, countertops, flooring, demolition, and electrical/plumbing line items), not a single lump-sum number.

When you read the scope, make sure you understand what is excluded: subfloor repairs, drywall patching, permit fees, disposal, plumbing reconnections, appliance hookup, and any code-required electrical changes. A good contractor will also document insurance claim support if needed (for example, if the kitchen renovation relates to water damage). Ask about warranties: workmanship warranty length (for installation), plus separate equipment/material warranties for cabinets, countertops, and appliances. For payment, never allow large upfront payments; a healthy schedule typically caps upfront at 10–15%, with holdback until key milestones (like countertops templated/installed and appliances tested).

Finally, demand a written timeline that includes an arrival estimate, sequencing, inspection lead times, and a completion window. In Ferguson kitchens with older wiring or plumbing, those inspection steps can shift the schedule by days, sometimes more—so you want that addressed early.

  • Verify contractor HIC registration for jobs over $500 (hic.attorneygeneral.gov).
  • Confirm the electrician’s license and the plumbing supervision/role for any circuit or plumbing changes.
  • Request and review certificate of insurance for general liability.
  • Request proof of workers’ compensation coverage.
  • Get 2–3 quotes that break labor vs. materials (not a lump sum).
  • Require a written scope specifying demolition, drywall patching, and haul-away.
  • Ask exactly who obtains permits if your project includes plumbing reroutes or new electrical circuits.
  • Confirm appliance hookup responsibility (gas vs. electric, dishwasher, range hood venting).
  • Ask how they handle cabinet delivery lead times and what happens if material ships late.
  • Verify countertop templating and install scheduling with a stated wait time (especially for quartz/granite).
  • Get warranty details in writing for workmanship and for each major product.
  • Confirm a payment schedule with holdback tied to milestones, not just “substantial completion.”

Concrete red flags I see in Ferguson kitchen projects include: (1) “one price” proposals with no line items for electrical/plumbing permits and no explanation of exclusions, (2) refusing to show insurance and workers’ compensation paperwork before starting demo, (3) offering countertops without a templating and re-check process (leading to fit issues), (4) suggesting you can skip permits even though they’re moving plumbing or adding circuits, and (5) asking for most of the money upfront with no milestone-based holdback.
