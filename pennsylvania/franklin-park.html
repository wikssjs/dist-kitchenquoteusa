Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovations that cross into plumbing or electrical work are regulated—and Franklin Park homeowners should plan for permits and inspections when walls are opened. Contractors doing home improvement work over $500 generally must be registered under the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) program (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For kitchen electrical work, a licensed PA electrician must install or modify any circuits—verify status through the licensing information at dli.pa.gov. Plumbing work (including sink and dishwasher hookup work, and especially relocation) must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits: in most Pennsylvania municipalities, you’ll typically need building permits and inspections when the work involves plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal/structural modifications. Cosmetic scope—like painting, hardware, or cabinet refacing without moving utilities—often does not require a permit.

Lead paint is another key consideration. If your Franklin Park home is pre-1978, lead-based paint may be present on kitchen surfaces. Federal EPA Lead-Safe RRP rules require an EPA Lead-Safe RRP–certified contractor for projects disturbing lead paint surfaces. Before hiring for a gut remodel in older homes, check the certified renovator registry at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Also remember the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act: contracts for home improvement work over $500 must be in writing and include a 3-day right of cancellation. When you request your quote, ask for certificates of insurance and workers’ compensation proof, and confirm the contractor’s credentials using the online registries before the first day on site.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Franklin Park?

With about 35.7% of Franklin Park’s housing built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many homeowners are facing kitchens that look dated but still have workable “box” construction underneath. Cabinet refacing—installing new door and drawer fronts and applying veneer while keeping the existing cabinet boxes—is a strong option when the boxes are square, firmly attached, and in acceptable interior condition. In Allegheny County, refacing is often a practical middle step because it refreshes the look without opening as much of the wall system, which can reduce how often we uncover hidden plumbing or electrical needs.

Refacing is typically 40–60% less than full replacement, but it only makes sense if your current layout is functional and your cabinet interiors are not sagging, water-damaged, or badly out of level. Replacement becomes the better value when you want new storage solutions (deeper drawers, tray pull-outs, corner solutions, or better dish storage) or when the existing boxes have been compromised.

Here’s a concrete example: if your current kitchen doors are worn, refacing may land in the $2,500–$8,000 range, while cabinet replacement for stock/semi-custom can run $8,000–$35,000. If the boxes are solid and you’re mainly after a new style, the price difference is justified. If you’re dealing with warped boxes near a sink supply area, replacement is usually the smarter path—even if the upfront cost is higher—because it prevents recurring alignment and door gap issues.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Franklin Park

Picking the right contractor in Franklin Park is about checking credentials before you sign, then matching the written scope to what you actually want done. First, verify IICRC certification where applicable and confirm Pennsylvania licensing and registrations. For Pennsylvania requirements, start with the relevant online licensing/registry resources: check the HIC registration status for home improvement work over $500 at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, and confirm electrical and plumbing credentials through the state’s licensing channels (verify electrician and plumber status directly rather than assuming). If a contractor mentions lead paint controls or expects to disturb surfaces in a pre-1978 kitchen, ask how they’ll meet EPA Lead-Safe RRP requirements and request documentation.

Then get 2–3 itemized, written quotes. A good quote breaks out labor and materials (cabinet install, demolition, electrical rough-in, plumbing changes, countertop template and install, flooring underlayment/leveling, and appliance hookup). Avoid “lump sum” only pricing—kitchen work often changes after demolition, but you should still see what’s included versus excluded.

Read the scope for exclusions (demo hauling, disposal, patching/skim coating, cabinet leveling, venting adjustments, electrical permit/inspection fees). Confirm warranty coverage: workmanship warranty length and separate manufacturer warranties for cabinets, countertops, and appliances. Payment schedules should be conservative—never more than 10–15% upfront, and hold back until completion or until punch list items are resolved. Finally, request a written arrival estimate and completion window tied to the schedule you’re given.

  • Ask who will pull permits and whether inspection fees are included.
  • Confirm the electrician and plumber are licensed for the specific circuit and hookup work.
  • Request the exact scope for appliance hookup (waterline connections, gas hookup responsibilities, and electrical outlet requirements).
  • Verify insurance: certificate of insurance naming you as additional insured where required.
  • Verify workers’ compensation coverage (not just “we have insurance”).
  • Check materials lead times for your chosen cabinets and countertops (quartz templating timing matters).
  • Insist on a written change order process for uncovered issues (not verbal approvals).
  • Ask how they handle uneven subfloors (leveling/underlayment responsibilities).
  • Confirm what disposal includes: dumpsters, hauling, and where debris is removed.
  • Get a countertop measure/template date and who is responsible for re-template if changes occur.
  • Make sure warranty terms are in writing and specify what’s covered for labor vs parts.
  • Require the start date, work hours, and jobsite protection plan (floor protection, dust control).

Red flags to watch in Franklin Park: (1) no written contract or unclear scope, (2) asking for large upfront payments (well beyond 10–15%) before demo or rough-in, (3) vague “we’ll handle permits” without naming who pulls them, (4) refusing to provide proof of insurance and workers’ compensation, and (5) quoting without acknowledging older-housing realities like potential plumbing/electrical bring-up-to-code once walls are opened.
