Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, the rules that affect your kitchen remodel usually fall into three buckets: contractor credentials, licensed trades for certain work, and building permits for changes that go beyond “finish-only” updates. First, for many kitchen remodel projects over $500, your contractor should be properly registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the HIC registry at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For electrical work, a licensed Pennsylvania electrician must install or modify any circuits; you can verify credentials through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing, sink and dishwasher hookups, supply/drain relocation, and any reroutes must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required when plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal are involved. Cosmetic work—paint, hardware changes, and cabinet refacing—typically does not require a permit because it generally doesn’t change the building systems. However, if you’re planning to move a wall, relocate the sink, add a new island circuit, or alter venting, assume you’ll need permits and inspections before walls go back up.

One more important factor for Dunbar: many homes were built before 1978, and lead-based paint can be present on kitchen surfaces. If a contractor disturbs lead paint, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required. Verify certification at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring for gut remodels in older Dunbar homes.

Step-by-step verification: (1) confirm the contractor’s HIC registration online; (2) request a current certificate of insurance and check limits; (3) ask for proof of workers’ compensation coverage; (4) require written, itemized scope in a signed contract and make sure the contract is compliant with the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, which includes a 3-day right of cancellation for covered work over $500.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Dunbar?

With 67% of Dunbar’s housing stock built before 1980, many Fayette County homeowners are working with kitchens that are structurally serviceable but visually dated. That’s exactly why cabinet refacing is such a common decision here: you replace door and drawer fronts, apply veneer to visible surfaces, and update hardware while keeping the existing cabinet boxes. In most situations, refacing costs about 40–60% less than full replacement because you avoid full demolition, new box installation, and much of the patch-and-finish work that comes with replacement.

Refacing is only cost-effective when the existing cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. In older Dunbar kitchens, the “gotcha” can be doors that don’t close evenly due to box sag, water-related damage near sinks, or walls that are out of plumb—issues that may require carpentry correction anyway. If the boxes are compromised, replacement may end up being the better long-term value even if it’s more disruptive upfront.

Here’s a concrete way to judge the difference. If your goal is to modernize the look without changing storage, refacing in the $3,200–$9,000 band can be justified versus replacement if the boxes are still sound. But if you want different drawer layouts for cookware, need new organizers, or have mismatched or water-damaged base cabinets, replacement typically moves toward the $9,000–$42,000 range (or higher for semi-custom), and you’ll avoid paying twice to “fix around” problems.

For a lot of Dunbar homeowners, the practical approach is: inspect first. If the boxes are structurally good and the layout works, refacing can deliver a fast refresh. If the cabinets are failing or you’re changing the kitchen’s function, plan for replacement and the knock-on effects on countertops, flooring, and potentially electrical/plumbing access.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Dunbar

Choosing the right contractor in Dunbar is about confirming credentials, getting transparent pricing, and protecting yourself contractually. Start with licensing and certification. For general contractor credentials, verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov when the project qualifies (often over $500).

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes, not lump-sum only. You want a breakdown that distinguishes labor versus materials (cabinet line item, countertop fabrication and install, electrical/plumbing rough-in, flooring prep, demolition, drywall, and finish painting). Warranty and payment matter. Ask for a clear workmanship warranty period and whether cabinets/countertops include manufacturer warranties (and what proof of purchase is needed). Payment schedules should be conservative; avoid large deposits and never pay more than 10–15% upfront. Use a holdback that’s released after punch-list completion. Also request the timeline in writing—arrival dates, estimated start, and completion window—so you’re not stuck waiting on cabinet lead times or permit inspections.
