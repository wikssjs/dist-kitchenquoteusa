Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovations often involve licensing and permits, particularly when you change plumbing or electrical systems. Contractors performing “home improvement” work over $500 generally need a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (see hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work, any new circuits or circuit modifications must be installed or modified by a licensed Pennsylvania electrician—verify credentials through dli.pa.gov. Plumbing work (such as new sink/dishwasher hookups, or relocating drains and supply lines) must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required in most PA municipalities when you do any of the following: reroute plumbing (moving sink/dishwasher locations), add new electrical circuits, modify the electrical service/panel, or remove/open walls for structural changes. Cosmetic work—painting, new hardware, and cabinet refacing where no walls or utilities are moved—typically does not require a permit.

Because many Aston kitchens are older, lead safety is also critical. In pre-1978 homes, lead-based paint may exist on interior surfaces. If your contractor disturbs lead paint, the EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required for those doing the work; verify using certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Step-by-step credential checks for Aston homeowners: (1) Confirm the company’s HIC registration for work over $500. (2) Ask for the electrician/plumber’s license details and verify via PA registries. (3) Require a Certificate of Insurance showing general liability limits and ensure the certificate lists the correct jobsite address. (4) Request proof of workers’ compensation coverage. (5) Get the written contract before work starts and make sure any cancellation rights are documented for projects over $500 under the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (include the 3-day right of cancellation in writing).

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Aston?

With 68.9% of Aston’s housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many Delaware County homeowners are still dealing with 30–50-year-old cabinet boxes. In many cases, the boxes remain solid—square, well-secured, and structurally usable—while the door style and finishes look dated. That’s where cabinet refacing is often the best value. Refacing replaces only the door and drawer fronts (and adds a veneer finish), keeping the existing cabinet box structure. For most kitchens, refacing can cost about 40–60% less than full replacement, but it only works when the cabinet boxes are in good interior condition.

Refacing is cost-effective when you can reuse your current openings, hinges, and cabinet dimensions without forcing new hardware or disguising gaps. If your boxes are out of square, show water damage, sagging shelves, or warped rails from past moisture, you’ll likely pay for refacing—and still need replacement later. Replacement becomes the better decision when you want to change storage (tall pull-outs, pantry systems, upgraded drawer interiors), adjust cabinet layout for a modern workflow, or correct installation issues that refacing can’t fix.

Concrete example: if your kitchen is a typical size and you’re deciding between refacing and full replacement at comparable aesthetic quality, refacing might land around $2,500–$8,000, while stock cabinet replacement commonly sits in the $8,000–$45,000 band. That price difference is justified when your boxes have problems or you’re upgrading storage and needing a fresh layout. If your boxes are sound and you just want a modern door style, refacing usually wins on cost and disruption.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Aston

Choosing the right contractor in Aston is mostly about verifying credentials and then confirming the quote matches your scope. For PA licensing and compliance, first confirm the contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for projects over $500 (check hic.attorneygeneral.gov). Next, verify any electrical work with a licensed PA electrician through the PA Department of Labor & Industry resources at dli.pa.gov, and verify plumbing through the same approach. If the contractor claims certifications (like lead safety capabilities for renovations in older homes), ask for documentation.

Then, get 2–3 itemized written quotes. “Itemized” should mean labor and materials broken out (demo/disposal, rough carpentry, cabinet install, countertop fabrication/setting, tile/floor prep, electrical/plumbing allowances, and appliance hookup). Avoid lump sums that don’t explain what’s included—especially in Delaware County where older plumbing and undersized electrical can reveal extra work once openings are created.

Warranty matters, too. Ask for the workmanship warranty length and whether the coverage applies to cabinet installation, countertop setting, flooring transitions, and plumbing/electrical connections. For payment scheduling, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until key milestones (especially final inspections, caulking/grouting, and punch-list completion). Finally, require a written arrival estimate and completion window, including any lead-time expectations for cabinets and countertops—reputable firms in the area often book 2–4 months out for full guts.
