Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovation contractors are expected to be properly registered and licensed for the type of work you’re authorizing. If the project is over $500, contractors performing home improvement work must hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work that involves creating or modifying circuits, a licensed PA electrician must perform the work—confirm credentials through dli.pa.gov. Plumbing work—such as sink and dishwasher hookup or relocation—must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required in most PA municipalities when you’re changing the kitchen’s systems or structure. In the Chartiers area, kitchen renovations that involve plumbing rerouting, adding new electrical circuits, or removing walls typically trigger building permit and inspection requirements. Cosmetic work such as paint, new hardware, or cabinet refacing often does not require a permit. For lead paint concerns, homes built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint on kitchen surfaces; contractors disturbing those surfaces generally need EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification. You can verify certifications using certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Before work begins, follow a simple step-by-step verification: (1) check the contractor’s HIC registration online, (2) request a COI (certificate of insurance) naming you as an additional insured, (3) ask for proof of workers’ compensation coverage, and (4) confirm the licensed electrician/plumber details in writing for any electrical or plumbing scope.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Chartiers?

With Chartiers’s housing stock skewing older—about 63.0% of homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024)—many homeowners have cabinets with boxes that are still square and sturdy, but doors and finishes that look dated. Cabinet refacing replaces only the door and drawer fronts and applies veneer to the existing box surfaces. In many Washington County kitchens, refacing can cost 40–60% less than full replacement because you’re not paying to remove and rebuild the full cabinet base and wall system.

Refacing is usually the right move when the existing cabinet boxes are in acceptable interior condition: the frames are straight, the face is not swollen from moisture, and the storage layout already works for you. However, in older kitchens, we sometimes find hinge issues, uneven reveals, or past water exposure that makes refacing look “off” once doors are rehung. When the box is out of square or the interior layout no longer fits your routine, replacement becomes justified—especially if you want better drawers, pull-outs, a pantry solution, or a layout that better supports ventilation and countertop clearance.

Here’s a practical dollar example we see in Chartiers: if your budget is tight and you’re targeting a refreshed look without changing the storage layout, a cabinet refacing at $2,000–$8,000 can deliver a dramatic style update. If you instead need wider drawers, a different sink base size, or you’re converting awkward corners, replacement at $8,000–$40,000 may cost more, but it’s often the option that prevents you from paying twice later when you discover the box can’t support the new hardware and layout.

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes. Don’t accept a vague lump sum—insist on a breakdown separating labor and materials (demo, framing/repairs, cabinets, countertops, flooring, electrical/plumbing labor, and appliance hookup). For warranties, look for both workmanship warranty length and separate manufacturer/installer coverage on equipment and materials.

For payment scheduling, avoid paying more than 10–15% upfront. Hold back a portion until critical punch-list items are complete.
