What a kitchen remodel costs in Concord

A 1955 galley often needs walls opened to create the plan most people want today, while a newer tract home from the late 1990s may call only for cabinet and counter updates.

The most common budgeting mistake in Concord is to price the “pull and replace” scope and then start specifying layout changes mid-project, busting the budget with costs for plumbing, permits, and structural work. Defining your scope before you get quotes

A “kitchen remodel” in Concord can mean anything from a $1,500 facelift to a six-figure gut job. Defining your scope before calling contractors is the best way to get real numbers. Every serious project falls into one of four tiers:

  • Cosmetic refresh: New paint, hardware, perhaps lighting or backsplash—no changes to layout, cabinets, or the basic structure. Quick and affordable, with little chance for surprises.
  • Pull and replace: Old cabinets and counters come out; new ones go in the exact same spots. Plumbing, wiring, and layout stay put. You get a new look with minimal disturbance.
  • Gut, same layout: Everything down to the studs—the walls stay the same, but you get modern insulation, electrical, and plumbing right where they were. Cost jumps, but so does long-term reliability.
  • Layout change: Walls moved, plumbing and wiring relocated, possibly new windows or structural changes. This is where the project requires permits, design work, and a much larger spend.

The cliff comes between “gut, same layout” and “layout change.” Moving a sink or taking out a wall means engineering, permits, and specialized trades—costs outrun “pull and replace” jobs by a wide margin. Concord homeowners, especially in older 1950s kitchens, often face this choice head-on: keep the galley plan or spend big for an open layout.

Decide your tier up front. No contractor in Delaware County can give you a meaningful price without knowing if you just want new cabinets or are set on restructuring your space. Otherwise, you’ll get numbers that collapse when you try specifying finishes or moving layout elements.

Why the contingency exists

In Concord, 31.9% of homes were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). Much of Delaware County’s housing stock is a wave of 1920–1960 construction. These homes conceal decades-old surprises behind plaster and cabinetry, complicating even well-planned kitchen remodels.

  • Out-of-plumb walls and out-of-level floors are extremely common. Shimming can resolve small differences, but serious correction adds $1,500–$4,000 for structural carpentry.
  • Electrical issues: Undersized or ungrounded wiring in old kitchens requires replacement to meet today’s code—figure $2,000–$6,000 if running new lines or circuits.
  • Pipes: Galvanized supply lines or cast iron drains will need updating—budget $2,500–$8,000 for rerouting during a full gut job.
  • Uninsulated exterior walls often come up in Concord’s older housing. Installing new insulation during a gut is $800–$2,500 extra, but is worth doing while walls are open.
  • Previous unpermitted work can surface—potentially leading to unplanned costs for corrections or inspections.
  • Lead paint or asbestos flooring: For homes built before 1978, federal lead rules apply—testing must happen before any disturbance, and a certified renovator must handle any remediation.

If your home is 1980s or newer, the issues shift: expect more builder-grade cabinets and counters reaching their lifespan together, fewer electrical circuits than modern appliances need, and base cabinets or boxes of particleboard that have sagged.

Practical survival during the work

Living through a kitchen renovation in Concord means planning for disruption. You’ll need a temporary kitchen space. Most people set up the fridge in the dining room, place a microwave and kettle on a folding table, and use paper plates to cut down on dishwashing. For weeks when plumbing is offline, a laundry or bathroom sink becomes your lifeline for rinsing dishes and prepping food.

Dust is the enemy. Expect plastic sheeting and zip walls to keep construction zones separate—but there will always be fine dust that migrates, especially in open-plan homes. Plan extra cleaning and cover or move valuables from adjacent rooms.

The crew’s dumpster takes over a section of driveway or curb, while tools and materials often occupy a porch, garage, or dining room. Be ready for doors standing open as trades come and go, which matters for pets—secure them away from the work.

Some Concord homeowners choose to move out for the demolition week, but most stay. The real hidden cost is food: eating out or buying takeout for two months easily overtakes the new flooring budget (often $5,600–$22,500). That’s not a padded line item—plan for the increase before work begins.

Choosing a countertop for a Concord kitchen

Most Concord homeowners choose between quartz, granite, laminate, butcher block, and solid surface for kitchen counters. Each handles daily life differently.

  • Quartz is engineered and non-porous, shrugging off stains and never needs sealing. But it’s more heat-sensitive than people think—a hot pan can mark it permanently. Seams are nearly invisible but price jumps with complex edges or extra cutouts.
  • Granite is natural, tough, and heat-resistant—but does need sealing every year or two to avoid stains. Each slab is unique, and seams may be visible. Chips can sometimes be filled, but heavy damage means replacement.
  • Laminate options today are far improved and stand up to daily use in many Concord kitchens. They’re stain resistant, easy to clean, and the least expensive to install. Heat and sharp knives still damage them, but in a rental or lower-budget setting, laminate is a smart call—not a last resort.
  • Butcher block brings warmth and character but asks for regular oiling and attention to spills. It’ll show every mark and nick, but can be sanded and renewed in the right hands. Not for those seeking low-maintenance.
  • Solid surface (like Corian) is repairable and nonporous. Scratches can be buffed out, but it’s less heat-tolerant than stone. Joints are nearly invisible when well installed.

Fabrication drives price: templating means the installer visits to make a custom pattern for your space—no two kitchens have the same fit. Edge styles (bullnose, bevel, ogee) and cutouts for sinks or cooktops add to the cost, as do longer seams or unusual layouts. In Concord, the cost range for countertop replacement is $4,500–$18,000, mostly set by material and complexity.

The biggest line item in your Concord kitchen

Cabinets dictate the largest share of a kitchen budget, and in Concord, the decision isn’t just style—it’s what your walls will even allow. Refacing is smart when boxes are sturdy, square, and laid out as you want. You’ll get new doors and a fresh veneer for far less than a full rip-out. It can’t solve bad layouts or add storage, though, and old particleboard won’t hold screws as well over time.

Stock cabinets appeal because they’re ready-made, usually at the best price—if your kitchen is close to standard size. But most Concord homes, especially those from the 1920–1960 boom, have non-standard wall runs or odd corners. Semi-custom becomes the default in these situations—slightly higher price, more flexibility in size and finish, and fewer filler strips between cabinets and walls. Full custom is only needed for unique, high-end layouts or where nothing else fits.

Construction quality matters beyond price: plywood boxes are more durable than particleboard, dovetail drawers beat stapled construction, and full-extension soft-close hardware feels better and lasts. Frameless cabinets give a modern look and maximize storage but can be trickier to install in uneven walls—something Delaware County’s older homes throw at every job. Concord homeowners often land in semi-custom territory due to their home’s quirks, regardless of original intent.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

In Concord and throughout Delaware County, permits line up with the scope of the project. Cosmetic work—painting walls, swapping hardware, refacing cabinets, or direct countertop swaps—rarely requires a permit. This covers most facelift projects, and is why many Concord homeowners start small to avoid the paperwork and cost.

A building permit is almost always required when your kitchen project involves new electrical circuits, moving plumbing, or removing or altering walls. If you’re adding recessed lighting, relocating the sink, or opening up a wall to the dining room, you’ll be working under a permit. Townships in Delaware County require plan review for projects with structural, plumbing, or electrical changes.

Many municipalities in the county use third-party inspection agencies for electrical work. It’s not unusual in Concord for your township inspector to handle the general inspection, but a separate private agency comes for electrical—something many don’t anticipate. The township ultimately signs off, but these parallel inspections can affect schedules.

Contractors working on any kitchen over $500 must be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General under the Home Improvement Contractor registry, which you can check at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. All employers must carry workers’ compensation; coverage is verifiable at the state WCAIS system.

Unpermitted work can be a big problem at resale in Delaware County. If changes aren’t documented, you could be forced to redo or even tear out improvements. Always ask your contractor to pull the permit if required.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Concord

Should I choose plywood or particleboard cabinet boxes?

Plywood cabinet boxes are more rigid, resist water damage better, and hold screws more securely than particleboard—advantages that matter in many Concord kitchens, especially those built in the 1950s and 60s where walls are rarely square. Particleboard is found in many stock and some semi-custom cabinet lines. It’s less expensive and usually performs fine in lower-traffic kitchens, but it won’t hold up if exposed to a leak or constant moisture. In Delaware County’s older housing, settling and irregular walls can reveal the limits of particleboard. The extra up-front for plywood boxes—typically found in semi-custom or custom cabinets priced from $22,500–$34,000—often pays off in longevity and fewer headaches during installation. If you plan to stay in your Concord home for more than five years, plywood is usually the safer investment.

How do I verify a kitchen contractor in Pennsylvania?

In Pennsylvania, any contractor bidding or performing work over $500 must be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General as a Home Improvement Contractor. Always verify their registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov—never take a verbal assurance. Additionally, all employers must carry workers’ compensation insurance, which you can confirm through the WCAIS system. These are not just bureaucratic hurdles—they protect you from liability if a worker is injured on your job. For a Concord kitchen remodel, expect contractors handling a full gut or cabinet replacement (projects starting at $7,900–$34,000) to provide this paperwork up front. Never sign a contract or hand over a deposit before checking these credentials; most local pros expect the question and will have documents ready.

What is a realistic contingency for a Concord kitchen?

For a kitchen in Concord, especially in homes built before 1980, a 15–20% contingency is sensible. The older housing stock (31.9% pre-1980) means demolition often uncovers out-of-square walls, outdated wiring, or poor prior repairs—each can add thousands to a typical gut remodel. On a project budgeted at $73,500, setting aside another $11,000–$15,000 gives you room to address surprises without halting the job. Even in newer homes, setting aside 10% for minor curveballs is smart. The main reason: once cabinets or walls are out, there’s little choice but to proceed and fix what is found. If your kitchen is 1990s or newer and a straightforward pull-and-replace, you might get by with less. For anyone opening walls in Concord, erring on the higher end prevents bad surprises.

Should I hire a general contractor or a kitchen firm?

In Concord, a dedicated kitchen firm is usually the right fit for most full-scale renovations, especially if your scope includes cabinets, counters, and coordinating multiple trades. General contractors are ideal if your project also touches other parts of the home—say, opening up the kitchen to the living area or adding an extension. Kitchen firms often streamline design and sourcing; their subs handle cabinets, counters, electrical, and plumbing in rhythm. Both should be registered as Home Improvement Contractors for jobs over $500, and experienced with Delaware County’s permit process. For a galley kitchen layout change—one that touches plumbing and walls—a kitchen firm or a GC with strong local subs is critical. If you’re updating cabinets only ($7,900–$34,000), a kitchen-focused contractor is probably more cost-effective and faster.

What is the cheapest way to update a Concord kitchen?

If you’re watching the budget, cosmetic updates give the best return for the dollar in Concord. Painting cabinets, changing hardware, new light fixtures, and a fresh backsplash make a tired kitchen feel new for a fraction of the cost. A pro cabinet paint job typically lands between $1,200–$3,500, compared to replacement or refacing. These updates require little to no permitting, minimize disruption, and preserve the home’s character—ideal for Concord’s smaller galley or bungalow layouts. If counters are damaged, a new laminate top is effective and affordable ($4,500–$18,000), and is perfectly appropriate in rentals or modest homes. Save major investments—gutting, layout changes, or moving plumbing—for plans you’ll enjoy for a decade or longer.

How much do new kitchen cabinets cost in Concord?

Cabinet budgets in Concord run broad. Stock options, delivered fast and installed in standard kitchen layouts, start at $7,900 and may reach $34,000 if your space is larger or you upgrade finishes. Semi-custom, which most homeowners in older homes end up selecting, run from $22,500–$34,000. These give some sizing and finish flexibility, which is invaluable in houses with wonky walls and tight galley spaces—common in Delaware County’s mid-century homes. Full custom or high-end lines also top out in the $34,000 range, mainly when you want unique features or have an unusual footprint. If your boxes are sound, refacing can bring a whole new look for $7,900–$22,500.

Should I renovate before selling or let the buyer do it?

If your Concord kitchen is truly worn, dated, or non-functional, you may get more interest—if not always higher offers—by updating with a cosmetic refresh or mid-tier pull-and-replace. Full gut jobs (up to $135,500) are rarely repaid on resale and should only be considered if your home will then lead the market. Simple upgrades—new paint, counters ($4,500–$18,000), or updated lighting—help buyers picture themselves in the home and don’t require permits or lengthy timelines. Many local buyers expect to remodel to their taste, especially in older homes. If everything works but styles are out-of-date, more than half the time, leaving it for the next owner is the smarter play, especially in a stable market like Concord.

What should I expect in a pre-1980 Concord kitchen?

Kitchens in Concord built before 1980—just under a third of homes—often have out-of-plumb walls, under-sized cabinets by today’s standards, minimal circuits, and original plumbing that may be past its prime. Expect to uncover ungrounded wiring, cast iron or galvanized piping, or poorly insulated exterior walls once renovation begins. Federal rules on lead paint apply to homes built before 1978; you’ll need certified testing and, if required, specialized abatement before work can move forward. Often, these homes are ripe for semi-custom or custom cabinets to match odd dimensions, costing $22,500–$34,000. Always budget extra (15–20%) for unforeseen repairs—the odds of running into a hidden problem are much higher in these properties.

Why is there a gap after cabinets are installed?

In Concord’s housing stock—especially older kitchens—walls are rarely straight or square. Cabinet installers use shims to level runs, but small gaps between cabinets and walls or floors usually persist. Typically, these are covered with scribe molding, caulk, or filler strips—but the size sometimes surprises homeowners. Especially with stock cabinets ($7,900–$34,000), it’s common for installers to leave a purposeful gap rather than cut cabinets or force a bad fit. In newer construction, these gaps shrink, but in a 1950s galley with settling or past repairs, some tolerance is necessary to keep doors and drawers working properly. Proper trim and touch-up paint will make finished edges look intentional. It’s normal and not a sign of poor workmanship—just a reality in Concord’s varied housing stock.
