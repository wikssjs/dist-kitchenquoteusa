Allowances, exclusions and change orders

Comparing kitchen estimates can be confusing, especially in Spring where many projects hit surprises inside walls from 1970s builds. The main mechanism that causes misleading comparisons between bids is the “allowance.” An allowance is a placeholder dollar value for an item (like backsplash tile or cabinet hardware) that hasn’t been picked. If one bid has a generous tile allowance and another uses just $150 for the same line, you’ll see a big change later when reality catches up. Always check each allowance line—not just the bottom line—when comparing quotes.

Make sure your contract specifies cabinet brand and model, along with the countertop material, thickness, and whether seams or special edges are included. A contract should explain what happens if sagging floors or odd wiring turn up: is there a written change order, and do you have to sign off before the contractor proceeds? Payments should be by project milestones, not just fixed dates, and each pay period should come with a lien waiver from subcontractors to protect your title.

Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) says any contract over $500 must be in writing, and you get three business days to cancel with no penalty. A contractor who offers to waive your insurance deductible, or who won’t provide a valid written agreement, isn’t following the rules.

Why the electrical line grew

Many homeowners in Spring are surprised by how much of their kitchen budget goes to electrical upgrades. The Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), enforced by the Centre Region Code Administration and other local offices, expects any renovated kitchen to meet current IRC-based standards. That means separate 20-amp circuits for countertop appliances, dedicated runs for the dishwasher, disposal, and microwave, modern 240V service if you install an electric range, GFCI protection at all countertop outlets, and tight outlet spacing. All of these go beyond what was required in the 1960s–1980s.

Around 48.7% of homes here were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), which means the typical kitchen often has only one or two outdated, ungrounded circuits—or aluminium branch wiring. It’s very common to open walls expecting “just a pull-and-replace,” only to find a shared, obsolete kitchen circuit and a service panel with no spare spaces. Once new work begins, inspectors will look for the whole project to meet the current code, whether or not it was originally quoted, and that upgrade is never optional.

If your home has a small-galley kitchen left over from the original subdivision build-out, expect electrical to be a major wildcard, especially if more outlets or powered appliances are part of the plan. Bringing kitchen wiring up to current standards in Spring typically costs $4,000–$16,000 depending on whether you need a new subpanel or just run new circuits from the existing one.

Why the contingency exists

Roughly half of all homes in Spring were built before 1980, making surprises during kitchen renovation common (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). Once cabinets and walls come out, crews often find out-of-plumb studs and sloping floors, especially in small-galley kitchens from the original subdivision era. Fixing these surfaces for new, level cabinet installation can require significant shimming and carpentry, adding several days and $1,000–$4,000 to your contingency budget.

Many older Spring kitchens feature ungrounded or undersized electrical wiring, galvanized supply pipes, or cast iron drains, all of which must be updated to meet today’s code. Swapping galvanized pipe for PEX or copper can run from $1,500–$6,500, while a full electrical rewire will run $4,000–$16,000. Insulation is another issue—many pre-1978 exteriors lack wall insulation entirely, which is often discovered only after opening up.

Previous DIY or unpermitted work is also common, sometimes complicating demolition and requiring more extensive repair. Any home built before 1978 falls under the federal EPA Lead RRP Rule, meaning all painted surfaces must be tested for lead before disturbance. Likewise, old sheet flooring can hide asbestos, which requires special removal processes.

If you’re in a newer part of Centre County’s housing stock—late-1980s or later—builder-grade shortcuts are another pain point: particleboard boxes falling apart, minimal circuit counts, or laminate counters reaching failure all at once. In both eras, the contingency is there because many issues simply aren’t visible until demo.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

In State College and surrounding Centre County municipalities, whether or not you need a building permit depends on what you’re changing. Cosmetic work like painting, swapping hardware, refacing cabinets, or replacing a countertop in the same footprint usually doesn’t trigger a permit. However, any changes to electrical or plumbing systems—such as relocating or adding new circuits, or shifting the sink—almost always require a building permit in State College.

If you’re planning to move walls, adjust the layout, or upgrade mechanical systems (such as installing a new range hood duct that wasn’t there), expect plan review from the Centre Region Code Administration in State College or from the local code office or third-party enforcement firms in surrounding townships. Electrical inspections are usually performed by certified third-party agencies contracted by local code offices, not by the town itself. This often catches homeowners by surprise, especially during resale, when missing documentation is discovered.

Only hire a contractor registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registry for any job over $500. Contractors must also have active workers’ compensation insurance (verifiable through the state’s WCAIS system) for all employees. Unpermitted work can delay or outright block your sale later, especially in the State College–Bellefonte area, so get paperwork in order before demolition begins.

Defining your scope before you get quotes

Making sense of kitchen remodel prices in Spring starts with defining your project’s scope before you call any contractor. “Kitchen remodel” covers a vast range—from a $4,900 paint-and-tile update to a $118,500 overhaul with new layout, structure, and full finish upgrades. Sorting out which of the four major project levels fits your goals will keep both your expectations and contractor conversations realistic.

Cosmetic refresh: Paint, new hardware, tile backsplash, and lighting updates—no cabinet or layout changes. Great for quick looks and tight budgets, since much of the work is DIY-friendly and doesn’t need permits.

Pull and replace: New cabinets and countertops in the exact same layout. Plumbing and electrical stay put; floors and finishes are updated. This is by far the most common scope in late-70s to early-80s homes around Spring, and the price can be sharply lower than a gut remodel.

Gut, same layout: Everything to the studs for new surfaces and mechanicals, but all services (sink, range) return to their original spots. This triggers code upgrades—think GFCI and more circuits—but lets you avoid the cost and complexity of moving plumbing or structure.

Layout change: Major move—sinks, ranges, or walls. This is where the cost cliff hits: new engineering, plan review, plumbing, and permits drive up both price and timeline. Only choose this tier if your existing kitchen simply cannot function or if walls, plumbing, or structure must be moved for future use.

The step from tier three to four is so steep it can double your budget. Decide your general direction before contractor calls—otherwise, no quote you receive will be meaningful, and you risk bidding one job and running another.

The biggest line item in your Spring kitchen

Your choice of cabinets will decide half your kitchen budget. In Spring, the main options are painting, refacing, stock, semi-custom, and full custom cabinets. Refacing is far cheaper and makes sense if your boxes are sound, square, and your layout works—but it can’t give you more storage or change traffic patterns. It’s a good call in many homes from the State College boom era that have sturdy boxes but dated doors.

Stock cabinets are most affordable but come in fixed sizes—perfect for standard galley kitchens but less ideal when walls aren’t perfectly plumb (as is common locally), requiring extra filler strips. Semi-custom lets you tweak dimensions and pick from multiple finishes, at a higher price but less frustration with uneven walls. Custom cabinets fit anything and can maximize every inch in awkward spaces, but you’ll pay the highest price with the longest wait. In Spring’s older subdivided neighborhoods with non-standard wall runs, many homeowners are surprised to find they need semi-custom for a good result, regardless of what they first budgeted for.

Quality is more than price: look for plywood (not particleboard) boxes, dovetail drawers, full-extension and soft-close hardware, and pay attention to construction style—frameless for more space, face-frame for traditional looks. Saving on hardware but skimping on box quality rarely pays for itself in a kitchen you want to last 20+ years.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Spring

How much more does moving the sink and range cost? Moving the sink and range in a Spring kitchen remodel pushes the project from a standard pull-and-replace across a significant cost divide. When these services are moved—even a few feet—you add new plumbing runs, electrical circuits, additional demolition, and often require a structural or engineering review. In Spring, this generally shifts your project from a typical $29,500–$118,500 gut remodel into the higher part of the layout change bracket, easily adding $10,000–$35,000 depending on the complexity and duration. Plan review by the Centre Region Code Administration is likely to be required, and inspections will verify compliance. The main danger is underestimating both the time and expense added, so always scope out kitchen changes before committing to a layout move.
Can I over-improve for my Spring neighbourhood? It's easy to over-improve a kitchen in Spring, especially in neighborhoods built during the 1960–1985 subdivision wave. The median household income here is $77,205 and the homeownership rate is high, but many houses are modestly sized. Investing in a full gut remodel at the high end of $118,500 can make less sense if surrounding homes are still sporting original 1970s cabinets and basic finishes. Appraisers and future buyers are not likely to assign full value to a luxury kitchen in an otherwise mid-market block. Aim for upgrades that match both the style and price point of local comparables. High-quality replacements at the middle of the price band ($64,500) often deliver the best long-term value while keeping resale prospects strong.
Should I choose plywood or particleboard cabinet boxes? In Spring, plywood cabinet boxes stand up better over time, especially given the area's older, less-square construction. Plywood resists sagging and moisture in local kitchens that often see heavy seasonal swings in humidity. Particleboard is less expensive and can be serviceable for tight budgets, but it's more prone to long-term damage—especially on the floors and under sinks where leaks happen. For most Spring kitchen remodels, investing in plywood cabinets (costing from $6,900–$29,500 depending on style and features) is a durable choice. If you’re only planning a short stay or updating a rental, particleboard may suffice, but for a primary residence refacing or replacement, plywood is a safer bet in the State College–Bellefonte area.
How do I verify a kitchen contractor in Pennsylvania? You should always check a kitchen contractor’s registration before signing any contract in Spring or anywhere in Pennsylvania. For jobs over $500, they must be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General’s Home Improvement Contractor registry, which you can search at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. You should also verify active workers’ compensation insurance via WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov) for any company with employees. Ask for both numbers and check them yourself, since unregistered contractors can void your warranty and expose you to liability. This is especially important on larger kitchen projects, such as a cabinet replacement at $6,900–$29,500 or more, since the stakes are so much higher if something goes wrong.
Do older Spring homes need electrical upgrades for a kitchen? Almost every pre-1980 home in Spring needs at least partial electrical upgrades during a kitchen renovation. Many kitchens from the State College area’s big 1960–1985 buildout have only one or two ungrounded circuits, sometimes even with aluminium branch wiring or a full panel lacking space. The Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code now requires multiple dedicated circuits, plus GFCI protection and receptacle spacing. Once walls are open for new cabinets or appliances, local inspectors will expect you to meet today’s standards, driving up both the timeline and cost. Expect electrical work to land between $4,000–$16,000 for most upgrades. Never skip this step; it’s a code and safety matter, not just a nice-to-have.
What does a quartz countertop cost in Spring? Quartz countertops remain popular in Spring kitchens because they offer durability and an easy-to-care-for surface. Fabrication and installation typically fall in the $4,000–$16,000 range for most Centre County kitchens. The final price depends on the size of your kitchen, the specific brand and color you choose, and whether you select premium edges or cutouts for things like undermount sinks. If your project involves moving plumbing or replacing cabinets at the same time, that can push the bundled price higher. Always ask if the quote includes template, seam work, and reconnecting the sink—unitemized quotes can hide add-ons. Spring’s labor market occasionally factors in higher lead times, but this is the right band for local jobs.
Can I live in the house during a kitchen renovation? Most Spring homeowners stay in their homes during a kitchen renovation, but it does come with real disruptions. For a cabinet replacement or countertop install (projects in the $6,900–$16,000 range), the kitchen will be out of commission for at least one to three weeks. Full gut remodels or layout changes can extend this to several months. Plan for microwave and refrigerator parking in another room and expect some dust throughout the house, despite the best efforts at plastic sheeting or air scrubbers. If you have a second kitchen or can set up a basic one elsewhere, the process is manageable. If not, or if allergies or kids are a concern, budgeting for temporary housing makes sense—especially during major gut jobs common in Spring’s mid-century stock.
Why is there a gap after cabinets are installed? Gaps are common after cabinet installation in Spring kitchens, especially with original subdivision-era walls that are rarely straight or plumb. Installers routinely find out-of-level floors or bowed walls, and must use shims and fillers to achieve a level run for your cabinets and countertops. These intentional reveals are then hidden with scribe molding or side panels. If you’ve paid towards the higher end for semi-custom ($20,000–$29,500) or full custom cabinets, gaps are minimized by made-to-fit boxes, but even top-tier products can’t compensate for significant framing irregularities. Don’t panic if you see temporary spaces during installation; reputable contractors will finish these gaps before sign-off. If unaddressed, push for a solution before your final payment.
Should I reface or replace my kitchen cabinets? In Spring, refacing makes sense if your cabinet boxes are solid, square, and the existing layout works for you. It costs less—typically $6,900–$20,000—and keeps disruption minimal because most of the structure stays put. Refacing replaces doors and visible surfaces, so it’s ideal when the underlying storage is sufficient but the look is outdated. But if you want to reconfigure storage, add drawers or pullouts, or correct crooked runs left from the original 1970s build, full replacement becomes the better investment. Replacement also lets you address underlying issues like damaged boxes or out-of-square rooms, which are common in Spring’s older homes. Think about how much you want to change—the more the function, the more sense replacement makes.
