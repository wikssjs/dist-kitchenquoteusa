Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen remodels commonly require more paperwork than homeowners expect—especially in older Lansdowne homes. First, if your project involves electrical work, it must be performed by a licensed Pennsylvania electrician. Any new circuits, circuit modifications, or panel-related work for kitchen receptacles and appliances should be handled by a pro; verify licensing through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing (sink hookup, dishwasher connection, or any relocation), a PA-licensed plumber must perform the work or supervise it.

Pennsylvania also requires contractors to hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for work over $500. You can confirm this through hic.attorneygeneral.gov. In most municipalities, kitchen renovations involving plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal typically require building permits and inspections. By contrast, strictly cosmetic work—such as painting, new hardware, or cabinet refacing without changing utilities—typically does not require a permit.

Lead paint is another key compliance area. Lansdowne homes built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint on kitchen surfaces. If your project will disturb painted surfaces (common during a gut remodel, cabinet removal, or patching), contractors should have EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification. Verify credentials at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before signing.

Finally, under the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, contracts for work over $500 must be in writing and include a 3-day right of cancellation. Ask for the contract and a certificate of insurance plus proof of workers’ compensation coverage before work starts.

  1. Check HIC registration: confirm the contractor is active and matching the business address.
  2. Check electrical/plumbing licenses: ensure the correct licensed trade is named for each scope.
  3. Request a current certificate of insurance: general liability should be current and appropriate.
  4. Request workers’ compensation proof: PA requires coverage for employees.
  5. Confirm lead-safe certification: ask specifically if the job will disturb pre-1978 paint.
  6. Ask what permits are required: get a written statement of what will be pulled and by whom.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Lansdowne?

In Lansdowne, the decision between cabinet refacing and cabinet replacement is unusually common because about 91.7% of the local housing stock was built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). Many of these kitchens have cabinets with solid box frames but dated door styles, worn finishes, and mismatched hardware. Refacing—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and applying veneer over the existing boxes—often costs about 40–60% less than full replacement, which is why homeowners near Delaware County’s busier corridors often choose it when they want a noticeable update without a full tear-out.

That said, refacing is only a smart financial move when the existing cabinet boxes are structurally sound. In older Lansdowne homes, we frequently see sagging faces, water-damaged particleboard near sink bases, or boxes that are out of square from earlier repairs. If boxes are compromised, refacing can lock you into repeated problems and still require patching and rework—erasing the savings.

Here’s a concrete budgeting example: say you’re considering new doors plus veneer with an estimated $2,500–$8,000 refacing-style budget versus stock cabinet replacement at $8,000–$45,000. If your boxes are square and dry and the layout fits your needs, refacing can be the right call because you keep the “hard part” (the boxes) and spend on the visible surfaces. But if a sink base has swelling or the corner cabinets won’t close properly, the extra $ you avoid up front can reappear later in repairs—so replacement becomes the more reliable long-term choice.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Lansdowne

Choosing a contractor in Lansdowne should be methodical, not just based on a friendly pitch. Start with licensing and professional credentials. For PA electrical/plumbing work, confirm the licensed trade through the official state registry at dli.pa.gov. For the contractor itself, verify Home Improvement Contractor registration on hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If the project will disturb painted surfaces in pre-1978 areas, ask whether the contractor holds EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification and confirm via certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Then request 2–3 written, itemized quotes—not a single lump sum. You want a labor/material breakdown that clearly separates demo, carpentry, electrical, plumbing, drywall/patching, flooring installation, and cabinet/countertop fabrication & set. Read the scope line-by-line: clarify what’s included for disposal, patching, backsplash (if any), appliance hookups, and whether permits are included or billed separately. Also ask about insurance and documentation—make sure they can provide a current certificate of insurance and proof of workers’ compensation.

Warranty matters in kitchens that see daily water and heat. Ask for workmanship warranty length (how long they stand behind install quality), and separate warranties for cabinets, countertops, and appliances. Payment should be controlled: never pay more than about 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until key milestones are complete. Finally, insist on a written arrival estimate and completion window, including lead-time expectations for cabinets and countertops—reputable Delaware County firms often book 2–4 months out for full gut remodels.

What are the signs I need a full kitchen gut remodel?

A full gut remodel is usually the right call when the kitchen’s problems aren’t just cosmetic. Signs include repeated moisture damage near the sink base, sagging or out-of-square cabinet framing that won’t hold new doors properly, major electrical issues (flickering, unsafe outlets, or the need for new dedicated circuits for modern appliances), or plumbing that requires rerouting rather than simple replacement of fixtures. In older Lansdowne homes, you might also discover that the venting setup can’t support a new range hood, or that subfloor repairs are needed before you can install new cabinets and flooring. If you’re considering layout changes—like moving the sink or range—expect a permit-heavy project and a higher likelihood of encountering structural surprises once walls open up. Those situations commonly align with full gut budgeting (starting in the $45,000–$150,000 range for same-layout work and higher when layout changes require moving plumbing/electrical).
