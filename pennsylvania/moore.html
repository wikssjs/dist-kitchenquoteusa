General contractor, kitchen firm, or manage it yourself?

How you approach a kitchen renovation in Moore shapes your cost and stress as much as the material choices themselves. For most homeowners, the straightforward route is hiring a dedicated kitchen firm—what’s called “design-build.” You sign one contract, get design, cabinetry, and installation all from the same place, with the highest price but the simplest process. You’ll have the least effort and a single company accountable for the result.

Going through a general contractor is the middle path. Here, you (or a kitchen designer you hire) decide on the cabinets and finishes, then the general contractor manages the trades: demolition, carpentry, plumbing, electrical, floors, and more. It’s often less expensive than a design-build firm, but you need to stay involved on decisions and timing. Expect a fair amount of your evenings spent choosing, approving, and chasing options if you go this route.

The third way—owner-managed—means you directly hire each trade and run the scheduling yourself. This is the cheapest option on paper, but by far the most demanding. It’s not for anyone who works full-time or will need permits (triggered easily by electrical or structural work in Moore). Northampton County doesn’t have as many dedicated remodeling firms as nearby Allentown, making it harder to pull subcontractors together for a small, one-off job. If you aren’t ready to coordinate twelve shipments and six contractors, managing it yourself in this market can drag on for months and cost more in the end than hiring help.

Allocating a kitchen budget

Most Moore kitchen renovations start with a wish list, but they need a budget breakdown before making choices. Cabinetry is almost always the largest expense—often a third or more of a gut renovation—because it sets the look and function and drives much of the lead time. Labor is next, especially if you’re moving walls or reconfiguring plumbing and electrical. Countertops, appliances, flooring, lighting, and plumbing fixtures follow, and each one can swing depending on material and brand.

Two areas that get skipped in early estimates: contingency for hidden conditions (very real in Moore’s older homes) and soft costs like permits, design, delivery, and dumpster fees. You need both, especially in any kitchen built before 1980.
Put extra funds where they show: upgrading from vinyl to hardwood or from stock to semi-custom cabinets is worth it. Spending big on a professional-grade range but leaving outdated, low-end cabinets around it rarely pays back.

Planning appliances before cabinets

One of the most common mistakes in a Moore kitchen renovation is picking cabinets first and appliances second. Cabinets are measured and built around the specific appliances you select, especially for refrigerators, dishwashers, and slide-in ranges. If you order cabinets before finalizing appliance models, you are almost guaranteed to face expensive change orders or end up with a fridge sticking out or a range that leaves a gap.

For example, standard-depth refrigerators extend well past counters, while counter-depth models sit nearly flush but cost more and offer less storage. Slide-in ranges require cabinetry built just so, while freestanding ranges are more forgiving. Choosing gas versus induction dictates both the electrical service (older Moore homes often need a panel upgrade for induction) and venting. Panel-ready dishwashers and refrigerators need exact cabinet openings and hinge clearances.

Range hoods raise another issue: Ducting to the exterior is always preferred for air quality and moisture control, especially with Pennsylvania’s winter climate. Recirculating hoods are a compromise you’ll notice.

Professional-grade appliances may offer build quality, but in many homes, you’re paying a premium mostly for looks and a name. Always check lead times—some refrigerators and ranges now take longer to arrive than cabinets do, so order both at the same time to avoid delays that can push back your entire project.

When a Moore kitchen needs walls moved

In Moore, whether you keep your kitchen layout or move walls is the cost cliff that drives the entire project. As long as plumbing, appliances, and electrical stay in the same spots, you’re in the basic cost band. But as soon as you move a sink, stove, or wall—even if it’s just a few feet—you trigger a jump. This isn’t a matter of a few hundred dollars; moving the layout typically puts you in the $67,000–$124,000 range, even for an average-size space.

Why so much? Relocating a drain line requires proper slope and, in homes with a concrete slab or finished lower level, getting that slope can mean opening ceilings or floors you didn’t plan to touch. Electrical is similar: new circuits to the range or oven may mean you need a new panel or at least a subpanel. Removing a wall to “open up” the kitchen almost always leads to the big question—is it load-bearing? If it is, you’ll need an engineer’s report and a municipal permit, which you should budget for both in money and in waiting time. Venting a new hood to the outside sometimes means cutting through brick or stone, which is not a simple or cheap job here.

Take a hard look at whether your current layout is truly unworkable or just dated. In Moore’s older housing stock, a mid-century galley kitchen might be cramped, but moving everything rarely pays for itself unless the whole space plan is wrong. Many owners spend five figures just to shift a sink four feet—think it through before ordering plans.

Permits and inspections for a Moore kitchen

In Moore, and throughout Northampton County, you generally don’t need a permit for purely cosmetic changes—paint, cabinet refacing, new hardware, or even swapping out a countertop if you’re not moving the plumbing. However, as soon as you plan to add or move electrical circuits, shift plumbing, or alter a structural wall, you are in permit territory. For example, any kitchen renovation involving structural changes or new electrical circuits in Bethlehem requires a building permit, and plan review is required for projects that alter structural walls or add new electrical circuits.

For projects requiring a permit, inspections are serious business. In Bethlehem, for instance, municipal inspectors—not outside agencies—handle electrical inspections; this can mean a different standard and possible delays if you need to call for re-inspection. Skipping permits can bite you at resale, as any unpermitted work can come up in a buyer’s due diligence or create issues with insurance claims down the road.

The Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration is required for any project over $500, and you should verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. All contractors with employees must carry workers’ compensation insurance, which you can confirm on the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). And plumbers and electricians are licensed municipally, so check with your township’s code office for additional requirements. Don’t let anyone talk you into skipping the permit; it’s rarely worth the risk in the Moore market.

The real timeline for a Moore kitchen

In Moore, most homeowners start demolition before fully understanding the sequence and timing of a kitchen renovation—a main source of frustration. Here’s how it really plays out: Your timeline starts with design and field measurement. You have to select cabinets and appliances before anything gets ordered. Cabinet lead times can stretch 8–14 weeks in Northampton County, sometimes longer for specific finishes or semi-custom options, and local supply is a bit tighter than in Allentown due to fewer dedicated remodeling firms. Don’t rip out your kitchen until your new cabinets are in the warehouse or on the truck.

Once demolition begins, expect 1–2 weeks to get to the “rough-in” stage, where plumbing, wiring, and any layout changes take place before inspections. After passed inspection, cabinets go in—usually a week for install, plus more for fine trimming in older, out-of-square homes. Countertops cannot even be templated until the cabinets are fully set and leveled. Fabricators come out, make a digital template, then go back to the shop for custom cutting. This gap is at least one week, but often 10–14 days, and is rarely discussed up front. Only after counters are installed can final plumbing, backsplashes, flooring, and trim-out begin.

If everything goes right and nothing is moved, a Moore kitchen can be completed in 6–10 weeks wall to wall. Add layout changes or run into a supply snag, and schedules shift fast. Plan to live with a temporary kitchen for longer than you think—and make all key material orders before the first cabinet comes off the wall.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Moore

Can I over-improve for my Moore neighbourhood? In Moore, where most homes are owner-occupied and the median income sits at $89,668 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), it’s possible to over-improve—especially if you install ultra-custom cabinets or commercial appliances in a home that’s otherwise mid-market. The higher-end kitchen remodels can easily hit $124,000, but most resale values won’t support this unless your house is already among the most valuable in the area. If you plan to stay ten years, do what you’ll enjoy—but market-wise, a kitchen with tasteful, durable finishes in the $31,000–$67,000 range typically returns the best value without overspending.
What is the difference between stock and semi-custom cabinets? Stock cabinets come in standard sizes and finishes that fit most kitchens and are ordered from a catalog, which makes them the most budget-friendly and fastest to install option in Moore. Semi-custom cabinets, on the other hand, start as a base cabinet but allow modifications for width, height, finish, and some design features. They cost more but are a solution for older Moore homes with uneven walls or non-standard dimensions—common in houses built before 1980. You might pay as little as $7,200 for all new stock cabinets or up to $31,000 for a full semi-custom package.
What is a realistic contingency for a Moore kitchen? Older kitchens in Moore built before 1980 can hide surprises—bad subfloors, old galvanized plumbing, or out-of-date wiring behind the walls. A realistic and prudent contingency is 10–15% of your total renovation budget. For a mid-tier kitchen at $67,000, that’s $6,700–$10,000. In most cases, you will spend most or all of this if you’re opening up walls. If you don’t budget for it, unexpected issues can force tough choices on the fly or even stall the project until you come up with more cash.
What is an allowance in a renovation quote? An allowance is a placeholder amount in your contract for an item you haven’t chosen yet—like appliances, tile, or even cabinet hardware. For example, your Moore kitchen bid might include a $5,000 appliance allowance. If you select appliances costing less, you pocket the difference; if you go over, you pay the extra. In older area homes, allowances often aren’t enough—market rates for finishes can easily outpace what was set months earlier. Always ask the contractor what price level the allowance is based on, not just the dollar amount, to avoid budget shock.
Does replacing cabinets require a permit? In much of Northampton County, including Easton, you typically do not need a permit to replace your kitchen cabinets if you are not changing the layout or moving utilities. This means removing your existing cabinets and installing new ones in the same footprint won’t trigger inspections or paperwork. However, if your Moore project adds electrical circuits, shifts plumbing, or changes structural walls, you will need a permit—especially in Bethlehem. A simple cabinet replacement lands in the $7,200–$31,000 range without permit fees, but any code-triggering work should be discussed with your township office first.
Are Moore kitchens typically small galley layouts? Many homes in Moore and the surrounding Northampton County area do feature galley kitchens, especially in the older housing stock built before 1940. These layouts are long and narrow with two parallel counters, which can limit work and storage space. Remodels here often focus on either reconfiguring the space or removing a wall to “open up” the kitchen. Such changes can move a project from a basic cabinet swap at $7,200 into a full gut remodel at $31,000–$124,000, depending on how much is altered.
Do I need to change my kitchen layout? Not necessarily. If your kitchen layout in Moore works for your family, leave it—updating cabinets, appliances, and finishes will cost much less (starting at $31,000 for a full gut with no layout changes). Only consider moving walls, plumbing, or major appliances if access, storage, or flow are genuinely unworkable. Remember, even minor layout tweaks push you into a higher cost and permit bracket—and in this market, that can quickly add $36,000 or more. Evaluate carefully before making structural shifts.
How much does a kitchen renovation cost in Moore? The cost in Moore covers a wide band: from $31,000 for a modest full renovation (cabinets, counters, appliances, flooring) in the same layout, up to $124,000 if you use high-end materials and change the kitchen’s footprint. Most families spend around $67,000 for a comprehensive renovation. Simpler updates like cabinet replacement can run as low as $7,200. Your final price depends mostly on cabinet quality and whether plumbing or electrical moves are needed; square footage is much less important in this market than what’s behind the walls.
Why is there a gap after cabinets are installed? In every Moore kitchen, there’s a mandatory pause once cabinets are installed and before the new countertops go in. Countertop fabricators must physically template your space—they can’t cut the stone or solid surface from a drawing. Templating only happens after the cabinets are fully in place and level. This gap, usually at least a week and sometimes longer, is required for accuracy. Homeowners are often surprised because nobody mentions this phase. During this time, your kitchen may be unusable, and it’s an unavoidable part of the process—factored into any full remodel budget, from $31,000–$124,000.
