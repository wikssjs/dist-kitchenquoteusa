Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovation contractors must be properly licensed for the type of work you’re doing. If your project includes work over $500, the contractor should hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work, any circuit installation or modification in your kitchen must be done by a licensed Pennsylvania electrician—verify at dli.pa.gov. Plumbing changes—such as relocating a sink, adding a dishwasher hookup, or changing supply/valve locations—must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are typically required in most PA municipalities when you’re doing anything beyond a surface cosmetic upgrade. In Oakmont, that usually means: moving plumbing lines, adding new electrical circuits, installing new hood/venting where ductwork is altered, or removing walls/structural elements. Cosmetic work such as paint, hardware changes, and cabinet refacing generally doesn’t require a building permit.

Lead paint is another Pennsylvania reality for older homes. In Oakmont, many kitchens are in the pre-1978 category, so lead-based paint may exist on surfaces disturbed during a gut remodel. If your contractor will disturb lead paint, they should be EPA Lead-Safe RRP certified. You can verify this using the EPA certified renovator search at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before work begins.

For peace of mind, request a written contract for work over $500 under the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, including the 3-day right of cancellation. Then verify credentials step-by-step: check HIC and trade licenses online, ask for current certificates of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation coverage, and ensure the certificate lists the correct policy holder and coverage dates. If any document is missing or outdated, pause the process until it’s resolved.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Oakmont?

With 70.8% of Oakmont’s housing stock built before 1980, many Allegheny County kitchens have cabinet boxes that are still structurally sound, even if the style is dated. Cabinet refacing—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and applying new veneer—can cost 40% to 60% less than full replacement, and it’s a smart option when your existing layout is functional and your boxes are solid. In practice, that means you can often keep the footprint and focus on the visible improvements: new finishes, updated hardware, and a cleaner countertop look. For many pre-1980 kitchens, that “box stability” is the key difference between a worthwhile project and a frustrating one.

Refacing is most cost-effective when the existing boxes are square, firmly attached, and free of significant sagging, water damage, or widespread delamination. If the boxes are out of level, warped from moisture, or the interior storage is inefficient (blind-corner issues you can’t live with, missing organizers, or broken rails), replacement usually ends up being the better long-term value—especially if you’re also upgrading flooring transitions and countertop edges.

Here’s a concrete way to judge it. If you can reface at roughly $2,500–$8,000 and keep the same countertop footprint, the savings can be justified as long as the box frames remain true and the sink run and appliance spaces don’t need relocation. But if your kitchen needs a layout change, modern circuit planning, and new storage configurations, you may be better off budgeting replacement (often $8,000–$35,000 for stock or moving higher for semi-custom) so the cabinet system matches the new plan.
