Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovation contractors must hold the required Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for work over $500. You can verify the registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If your project includes any kitchen electrical work—anything involving new or modified circuits, receptacles, lighting wiring, or panel changes—a licensed PA electrician must perform the work. Confirm licensing at dli.pa.gov (Pennsylvania Department of Labor & Industry).

For plumbing, any work involving sink supply lines, dishwasher hookups, relocation of fixtures, or water/waste line changes must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber. In most PA municipalities (townships and boroughs in Montgomery County), permits and inspections are commonly required when you reroute plumbing, add new electrical circuits, or remove/move walls that affect structure. Cosmetic changes—like paint, hardware swaps, and cabinet refacing that does not require wall opening or mechanical changes—typically do not require permits.

Lead paint rules matter in older New Hanover homes. Many houses built before 1978 may have lead-based paint, and when a contractor disturbs lead-painted surfaces during a gut remodel, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required. Before hiring, you can check certification via certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Under the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, contracts over $500 should be in writing and include a 3-day right of cancellation. To verify credentials step-by-step, request: (1) HIC registration documentation, (2) proof of electrician/plumber licensure, (3) certificate of insurance (general liability) and (4) workers’ compensation proof. Then match names and policy numbers to the certificate—don’t rely only on a verbal assurance.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in New Hanover?

With about 32.2% of New Hanover homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many homeowners are dealing with aging cabinetry where the boxes are still structurally usable, but the door style, finish, and hardware are outdated. Cabinet refacing—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and applying veneer to the existing boxes—can be a smart middle ground. It typically costs 40–60% less than a full replacement when the cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition.

Refacing is often best for Montco kitchens where you want a quicker refresh without opening as many walls. But refacing has limits: it can’t correct warped boxes, rot from prior leaks, or repeated repairs from earlier moisture issues. If your current cabinets have soft bottoms, sagging doors from water damage, or mismatched gaps that never fully closed, full replacement may cost more upfront but can prevent recurring problems.

To ground the decision in real pricing: if your cabinets are sound and you’re aiming for a new look, refacing commonly lands in the $3,500–$12,000 band. If you instead need to replace boxes due to damage, standard stock replacement may start in the $12,000–$70,000 band, with semi-custom/custom options pushing higher. In older New Hanover homes, the “justification” for replacement is usually hidden condition—like a cabinet base that has been exposed to sink leaks for years—rather than aesthetics alone.

Both approaches can pair with new countertops (often $3,500–$16,000) and updated flooring, but the right cabinet path depends on what your contractor finds once drawers and toe-kicks are removed. A careful assessment before ordering materials can save you from spending refacing dollars on a situation that requires replacement.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in New Hanover

Choosing the right contractor in New Hanover comes down to credential verification, clear written scopes, and accountability on schedule and warranty. Start with licensing and certifications: in Pennsylvania, confirm the contractor’s HIC registration for eligible home improvement work at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For the work themselves, verify the right licensed trades—especially electricians and plumbers—using the Pennsylvania Department of Labor & Industry resources at dli.pa.gov.

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes that break out labor and materials by line item (for example: demo, rough carpentry, cabinet install, countertop fabrication/templating, flooring subfloor work, electrical and plumbing allowances). Avoid one-line “lump sum” estimates unless the scope is extremely detailed and attached.

Read the scope for exclusions: ask what is included for subfloor leveling, patching and trim, countertop sink cutout, disposal/removal, and GFCI/dedicated circuit work if you’re replacing appliances. If the contractor expects you to coordinate permits, that should be stated plainly. Also ask how warranty handling works if cabinets arrive damaged or if a countertop needs rework due to sink plumbing alignment.

Payment structure matters in Montco renovations: never pay more than 10–15% upfront for deposits. Hold back a portion until key milestones are complete, especially after cabinets are set and before final finish payments. Require a written arrival estimate and completion window so your schedule is realistic, particularly because cabinet delivery and scheduling for quality crews can stretch several weeks for larger remodels and older homes.

  • Require a milestone-based payment schedule with a retainage/holdback until completion.
  • Get a detailed start date, expected duration, and daily work expectations (including dust control).

Red flags to watch for in New Hanover include: refusing to provide itemized labor/material pricing; offering only a verbal permit plan; minimizing electrical/plumbing requirements (“we’ll just reroute without permits”); asking for large upfront payments (well beyond 10–15%); and providing vague warranties (no written workmanship duration or unclear responsibility for delays tied to materials).
