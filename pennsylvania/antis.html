Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovations aren’t just “construction”—they’re regulated. For projects over $500, a contractor performing home improvement work typically needs a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical changes in your Antis kitchen, any work that installs or modifies circuits must be performed by a licensed Pennsylvania electrician—verify licensing through dli.pa.gov. For plumbing (new sink locations, dishwasher hookups, or relocating supply/drain lines), plumbing work must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits are commonly required in most PA municipalities when renovations involve plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal/structural changes. Cosmetic work usually doesn’t require a permit—things like painting, hardware swaps, and cabinet refacing generally fall in that category because they don’t alter structural systems, electrical circuit design, or plumbing routing.

Lead paint is critical for older Antis homes. If your kitchen has surfaces with lead-based paint risk (pre-1978 homes may), contractors disturbing those surfaces must use lead-safe practices. EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required for contractors performing activities that disturb lead paint (verify before hiring at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov).

Finally, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires written contracts for home improvement work over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation. When you’re screening contractors, confirm (1) HIC registration, (2) proof of liability insurance, and (3) workers’ compensation coverage—ask for certificate of insurance and written workers’ comp proof before work starts.

Bottom line for Antis: if your boxes are sound and you’re primarily updating the face of the kitchen, refacing can deliver a noticeable visual upgrade for much less. How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Antis

Next, get 2–3 written quotes that are itemized by labor and materials rather than a single lump sum. Use a holdback until key milestones are complete (for example, cabinet set/level and countertop set, and final punch list completion). Finally, ask for a written arrival estimate and a completion window based on lead times for cabinets and slabs—especially in spring and summer when scheduling can tighten.
