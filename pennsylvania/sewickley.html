Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovation contractors must be properly licensed and registered for the work they perform. If a project involves Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) work over $500, contractors are generally expected to hold HIC registration (check hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work—anything involving adding, modifying, or relocating circuits used in the kitchen—a licensed PA electrician must perform the installation (verify credentials via dli.pa.gov). Plumbing work (sink and dishwasher hookups, new drain lines, or relocating plumbing) must be performed by, or supervised by, a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits vary by municipality, but generally: plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, and wall removal typically require building permits and inspections in most PA localities. Cosmetic work—paint, hardware, cabinet refacing—usually does not require a permit as long as no walls, plumbing, or wiring are being altered.

Because many Sewickley homes were built before 1978, lead-based paint may exist on kitchen surfaces. If a contractor disturbs lead paint, Pennsylvania projects typically require EPA Lead-Safe RRP (Renovation, Repair and Painting) certification. Before hiring for a gut remodel, verify certification at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Finally, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract for home improvement work over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation—ask for the paperwork in writing. Before signing, confirm the contractor provides a certificate of insurance for general liability and proof of workers’ compensation, and verify licensing in the online registries before work begins.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Sewickley?

With about 72% of Sewickley’s housing stock built before 1980, many kitchens are functionally usable but visually dated, and the cabinet boxes are often the last parts homeowners want to demolish. In that situation, refacing—replacing door and drawer fronts and applying new veneer over the existing cabinet boxes—can deliver a refreshed look at a lower cost than full replacement. For many Westmoreland projects, refacing lands roughly 40–60% less than replacement, because it avoids removing everything, reduces disruption to plumbing and flooring transitions, and shortens the cabinet install schedule.

That said, refacing is only a smart value when the existing boxes are structurally sound and fairly square. If the box frames are warped, the interior faces are delaminating, or the cabinets have been patched poorly over the years, you may pay for refacing now and still need replacement later. Replacement becomes the better choice when you want new storage layouts—bigger pantry capacity, roll-out organizers, special pullouts, or a more modern toe-kick and upper cabinet configuration—or when your kitchen has non-standard measurements that call for semi-custom or custom sizing.

Concrete example: if your current cabinets are stable and you’re aiming for a style change, cabinet refacing at $2,500–$8,000 can be the right budget move. But if you discover uneven box openings from decades of renovations and you want new full-height storage with corrected dimensions, cabinet replacement (often $8,000–$35,000 for stock/semi-custom) is usually justified because you eliminate the recurring “filler piece” and alignment compromises that refacing can’t fix.

What are the signs I need a full kitchen gut remodel?

A gut remodel is usually the right call when the kitchen’s “bones” are limiting performance, not just aesthetics. Signs include recurring outlet or breaker issues (especially if the kitchen lacks modern GFCI/ARC protection), poor ventilation that can’t be fixed without major ducting changes, or plumbing that’s showing age symptoms like slow drains or frequent shutoff failures. Another signal is when the cabinet boxes are not square or are showing water damage, delamination, or structural bowing—refacing can’t correct those problems. If your countertop surfaces and flooring are failing due to subfloor issues, or the layout is unsafe or cramped for modern appliance needs, a full gut may cost less than patchwork over multiple phases. In Sewickley and Westmoreland County, it’s also common to go gut when you want a new lighting plan plus countertop and flooring replacement together, because that coordination reduces duplicate labor. If you’re seeing multiple system red flags at once, you may be nearing the full remodel price band (often $35,000–$95,000 for same-layout projects) rather than a countertop-only or flooring-only approach.
