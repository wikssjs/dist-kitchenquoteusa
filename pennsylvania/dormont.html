Cosmetic, replacement, gut, or layout change?

Most confusion and sticker shock happens because “kitchen remodel” means different things to different people. There are four main scope levels to know before starting — and staying clear on these tiers is the only way to avoid a budget blowout:

  • Cosmetic refresh: Paint, new cabinet hardware, lighting swaps, backsplash, and maybe a faucet change. Cabinets, appliances, and major fixtures stay in place. The fastest and cheapest way to update, best for homes with a functional but dated kitchen.
  • Pull and replace (“replacement”): All new cabinets and countertops, but nothing moves. Layout, plumbing, and electrical stay put. This delivers the most impact for the least risk in most Dormont kitchens — especially if your kitchen already has a decent working triangle.
  • Gut, same layout: Everything down to the studs, services modernized but kept in place. Here you’re addressing issues behind the walls common in Dormont’s pre-1980 homes, such as knob-and-tube wiring and aging plumbing.
  • Layout change: Walls moved or removed, sink or appliances relocated, structural or mechanical work. This is where codes, permits, and engineer drawings come into play, and the budget jumps sharply.

The biggest cost jump is from “gut, same layout” to “layout change.” Moving a kitchen sink or knocking out a wall in Dormont means more trades (plumber, electrician, sometimes engineer), municipal permits, and sometimes plan review. If you want “open concept” or to shift fixture locations, be clear about that from the start — don’t ask for a basic quote and then unveil a wall removal or an island with a sink at design time. A qualified contractor can’t give you a meaningful number until you lock down the scope.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

In Dormont and elsewhere in Allegheny County, code enforcement is local. For most boroughs, repainting, hardware changes, or cabinet refacing do not require a permit. Even many cabinet replacements fly under the permit radar as long as you follow the original layout and aren’t touching the plumbing or electrical.

The line changes quickly, though: if you move a wall, reroute plumbing, or add new electrical circuits, a permit is typically required. In Pittsburgh, and many surrounding boroughs, major kitchen jobs involving moving services or structures require a building permit and, for layout changes, plan review from the Department of Permits, Licenses, and Inspections. Mechanical work such as a new range hood can also trigger the need for a permit, depending on venting and code specifics.

Electrical inspections are handled by the city in Pittsburgh, but many suburbs—including Dormont—use approved third-party agencies, which surprises a lot of homeowners at final inspection. Contractors working on your home, if the contract exceeds $500, must carry Home Improvement Contractor registration (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov) and provide workers’ compensation (check via WCAIS). Avoid any contractor who cannot show both.

If you skip the permit where it is required, the problem usually surfaces at resale—a missing permit or lack of inspection can stall a closing. Always check with the borough code office before starting.

Allowances, exclusions and change orders

If you’ve seen two kitchen quotes for the same space with totals thousands apart, the differences usually come down to how allowances are handled. An allowance is a placeholder dollar amount for something not yet selected—like “backsplash tile: $1,000 allowance.” Unsurprisingly, bids with unrealistically low allowances look cheap up front, but leave you to fund the difference later.

The only way to compare bids is side by side on their allowance lines, not their grand totals. Look for fully itemized allowances—flooring, tile, pulls, lighting—with realistic amounts. The same applies to cabinets: the contract should specify the line, construction, and finish, not just “quality cabinets.” Countertop details should include material, thickness, seam location, and edge profile. Quotes should outline how surprises—say, a bad subfloor—are priced and approved, and require written change orders before work continues.

A trustworthy payment schedule will be tied to milestones (like completion of demolition or cabinet install)—not load most payments at the start. Finally, ask for lien waivers from subcontractors with each major payment to avoid legal headaches if a contractor fails to pay trades. Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a detailed written contract on work over $500, and gives you three business days to cancel after signing.

The real timeline for a Dormont kitchen

Many Dormont homeowners dramatically underestimate how long a kitchen renovation actually takes, especially with the finish-trade labor market in Allegheny County being consistently tight. The main limiting factors? Cabinets and countertops. Here’s the real flow:

  • Step 1: Design & measurement. This takes time, especially if you want a good visual plan and accurate quotes.
  • Step 2: Order cabinets. Lead times can be 6–12 weeks for semi-custom lines. Do not start demolition before ordering — you’ll be living without a kitchen much longer.
  • Step 3: Demolition and rough-in. Once materials are close, remove old cabinets and open walls for electrical and plumbing. Inspection may be required if you’re moving services or changing layout.
  • Step 4: Cabinets installed. Only then can counters be templated — not before, as every Pittsburgh-area fabricator will insist. Expect a 1–2 week gap while your counters are fabricated.
  • Step 5: Counters, plumbing, flooring, and finals. Sink, appliances, and any backsplash or trim finish out the job.

In reality, a full gut remodel with layout changes in Dormont takes 10–16 weeks, mostly due to cabinets and sequencing the trades. Jumping the gun and tearing out the kitchen before ordering materials is the number-one reason a job drags on. If you want the shortest possible downtime, lock your cabinet choices and order before ripping anything out.

Three ways to organise the project

In Dormont, you have three choices for shepherding your kitchen project from drawings to a working kitchen, and each comes with trade-offs on cost, speed, and stress:

  • Design-build kitchen company: All-in-one outfit designs, supplies, and installs. This is the smoothest route if you want a single point of contact and are willing to pay for project management. Cost is highest, but mistakes and scheduling risks are lowest.
  • General contractor: You (or your designer) select the cabinets and finishes, and a GC coordinates all trades. Most Dormont kitchens are done this way. Costs are typically lower than design-build, but you’ll be involved in decisions and sourcing. You get professional scheduling, but may still need to make quick decisions when surprises happen.
  • Owner-managed: You hire and oversee each trade and sequence their work. This has the lowest dollar cost, but the highest in time, logistics, and stress. If you work full-time or don’t have flexible daytime availability, it’s risky—especially with trades in Allegheny County staying busy and delays snowballing easily. If permits are involved, you’re also responsible for scheduling inspections.

Unless you have prior experience running construction projects, avoid self-managing anything that triggers permits or touches wiring and plumbing. With a tight local labor market and high demand near Pittsburgh, good subcontractors can be booked out weeks or months—one missed hand-off and your kitchen can sit idle for far too long.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Dormont

Do I need to change my kitchen layout? Many Dormont kitchens were built before open-concept designs were popular, so walls and small footprints are common in pre-1980 homes. You don’t automatically need to change the layout — a “pull and replace” with new cabinets and counters (in the same footprint) can work well if the flow is reasonable and appliances are accessible. However, if your kitchen feels cramped or you’re constantly dodging doors and drawers, layout changes can add real function. Just know, costs rise sharply for layout changes: what might be $29,500–$117,500 for a gut in the same layout can climb well above $63,500 if you start moving plumbing or walls. If resale is your goal, most buyers value function and an open feel, but you don’t get those by default from just replacing finishes.
Can I over-improve for my Dormont neighbourhood? Absolutely — and it happens. Dormont has a median household income of $73,798 and a mix of home values. It’s possible to “over-renovate” by installing high-end custom cabinetry and luxury appliances where homes rarely sell at high numbers. If the average kitchen renovation locally is in the $29,500–$63,500 range, spending at the top of the market (or above) only makes sense if you plan to live there long term or have very specific needs. For investment properties or soon-to-sell homes, stick with durable, appealing materials and spend where quality shows — cabinets and lighting — to keep costs in line with typical neighborhood expectations.
What flooring works best in a kitchen? In Dormont’s climate, with its regular freeze-thaw cycles and older homes, resilient flooring holds up best. Sheet vinyl, luxury vinyl plank (LVP), and ceramic tile are all common in Allegheny County. Avoid hardwood in kitchens if you have frequent spills or a high-traffic household — movement and moisture in area clay soils can be unkind to it. For a typical Dormont kitchen, top-tier LVP or mid-range tile can be installed for $4,900–$19,500. The best choice balances durability, cleanability, and a style that matches the age of your house.
What is an allowance in a renovation quote? An allowance is a placeholder in your written contract for finishes or fixtures that haven’t been chosen at signing, often for items like backsplash tile or lighting. If the allowance is too low, you’ll pay the difference between the placeholder and the real item cost. For instance, a countertop quoted as “$6,000 allowance” in a kitchen with otherwise high-end finishes is suspicious — most Dormont kitchens see counter costs between $3,900–$15,500. If you want to know your true budget, compare allowance lines between quotes, not just the totals, and look for realistic, itemized amounts.
What is a realistic contingency for a Dormont kitchen? With 95% of Dormont homes built before 1980, unknowns behind the walls are common — old wiring, hidden water damage, misrouted pipes. A smart contingency (set-aside for surprises) is 10–15% of your kitchen budget, or roughly $2,950–$17,625 on a full gut. Skimping here turns a surprise into a project-stopping problem. Anything less than 10% and you’re gambling with your timeline.
What are cabinet lead times in Dormont right now? Cabinet lead times in Dormont and the broader Allegheny County area vary with demand, but for 2026 most stock and semi-custom lines run 6–12 weeks from order to delivery. Custom cabinetry can stretch even longer. Given the tight finish-trade labor market in the Pittsburgh core, delays anywhere in the supply chain can mean you’re living with a makeshift kitchen longer than hoped. For a mid-range replacement job, expect a timeline of 1–3 weeks for installation, but only after cabinets arrive.
Do older Dormont homes need electrical upgrades for a kitchen? Most do. Dormont’s overwhelmingly pre-1980 homes often have outdated or insufficient kitchen circuits — especially if the wiring hasn’t been touched in decades. Modern appliances, GFCI-protected outlets, and code-required dedicated circuits all push the threshold for professional upgrades. Budget for new circuits in any “gut” or “layout change” remodel, especially if you add high-draw appliances or outlets. This cost is rolled into a full remodel, typically at $29,500–$117,500, but can drive smaller jobs up if not anticipated.
How much does a kitchen renovation cost in Dormont? In 2026, Dormont kitchen remodels range widely: $29,500–$117,500 for a full gut remodel (same layout), with a typical job at $63,500. Just replacing cabinets costs $6,800–$29,500, and new counters alone run $3,900–$15,500. The biggest cost swings happen if you move the layout or run into major issues in your pre-1980 walls or subfloor. Always budget for contingency.
Can I cancel a renovation contract in Pennsylvania? Yes, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) gives you a three business-day right of cancellation after signing any written home improvement contract over $500. This applies to any kitchen project you might take on in Dormont — so don’t let anyone pressure you to finalize a deal on the spot. All reputable contractors in Allegheny County should be clear about this protection (and provide the contract in writing). Beyond those three days, cancellation may incur penalties or forfeiture of deposits, so read all paperwork carefully before signing.
