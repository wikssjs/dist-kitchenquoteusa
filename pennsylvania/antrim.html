Three ways to organise the project

How you structure a kitchen renovation in Antrim has a bigger impact on your stress (and sometimes your budget) than any single product choice. Most homeowners here use one of three routes: a design-build kitchen company, a general contractor, or self-managing the project by hiring trades individually.

A design-build firm handles every aspect: design, products, and trades work, all under a single contract. This is the most expensive and least hassle — you get one accountable party, but you pay for that privilege. It makes the most sense if your time is tight or you don’t want to manage the process day by day. Using a general contractor costs a bit less — you’ll handle more decisions and own communication between your designer (if you use one) and the trades, but the GC lines up the workflow and solves the problems as they arise. This approach fits best if you have time to stay engaged but don’t want to juggle schedules and contracts.

Managing the project yourself — hiring each trade, ordering materials, chasing the electrician — is the budget option but not for the faint-hearted. In Franklin County, where labor is tight and sub availability can add weeks at a time to schedules, even skilled DIYers can see a simple kitchen drag on for months. Unless you’re retired or can devote real time and attention, owner-managed projects often lose money in coordination failures and costly delays, especially when permits or inspections are triggered by layout changes or moved utilities.

Allowances, exclusions and change orders

Comparing kitchen quotes in Antrim is less straightforward than homeowners expect, mainly because of allowances. An allowance is a placeholder amount for an item you haven’t picked yet—like tile, faucets, or even cabinets—and the real total can swing thousands depending on how realistic these figures are. Two bids may look the same on the bottom line, but if one allows $1,200 for countertops and another $5,000, their real-world total isn’t close. Always compare allowance lines one by one before trusting the grand total.

Cabinet brands and lines should be named on your bid—not just “premium cabinets.” Make sure the spec calls out countertop material, thickness, edge type, and seam details. Read how your quote addresses surprises: old plumbing buried in the wall, unlevel floors, or water damage from a past dishwasher leak need a defined process for pricing and written change orders—never just verbal approvals. Payments should be tied to real milestones, not calendar dates, with reasonable deposits. Lien waivers protect you from subs claiming unpaid work.

Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a written contract for work over $500 and gives you three business days to cancel after signing. Don’t sign a proposal or hand over a deposit until you see all this in writing.

Defining your scope before you get quotes

“Kitchen remodel” means something different to every homeowner in Antrim, but your definition determines who will bid, price bands, and even whether you need a permit. There are four clear tiers:

  • Cosmetic refresh: new paint, lighting, hardware, maybe a fresh backsplash — no cabinet or layout changes.
  • Pull and replace: new cabinets, countertops, and often flooring, keeping the exact footprint; plumbing and electrical stay put.
  • Gut remodel, same layout: everything down to the studs and subfloor — all surfaces and fixtures new, current plumbing and electrical updated in the same locations.
  • Layout change: moving or removing walls, relocating the sink or range, or expanding kitchen boundaries. This is a full rework involving permits, engineering, and a more specialized team.

The steepest price jump in Antrim hits the moment you cross from “gut, same layout” to “layout change,” because moving plumbing or gas lines triggers a different class of permit, likely a plan review, and requires coordination between more skilled trades. This is the cliff that turns a $30,500–$66,000 job into $66,000–$122,000.

If you only need new finishes or you’re happy with the kitchen shape, keep it to cosmetic or pull-and-replace. Decide your tier before you call anyone out—an honest contractor can’t price a moving target, and sticking to one scope level from day one is how you avoid sticker shock and project creep.

The biggest line item in your Antrim kitchen

Cabinets are the largest single cost in almost every Antrim kitchen, and your choice here locks in both look and price. In Franklin County, most homes are post‑1960, meaning boxes are often decent and the layout works, making cabinet replacement or refacing common for mid‑life upgrades. Here’s where your options break down:

Refacing gives new doors, drawer fronts, and veneer to your existing boxes. It’s a real option — and can be excellent value — if your boxes are sturdy and you don’t want layout changes or more storage.

Painting old cabinets is cheapest, but does nothing for durability or function. Stock cabinets are the big-box basics: affordable, but you’ll see filler strips if your kitchen isn’t built in standard measurements. Semi-custom cabinets let you adjust width, depth, and finish — which matters here because builders in the '60s, '70s, and '80s rarely built perfectly plumb walls. Full custom suits homes with odd corners or a high-end wish list, but comes with the highest price and longest wait.

Quality boils down to box material (plywood beats particleboard), drawer construction (dovetails outrun staples), and modern hardware—look for full-extension, soft-close features. Antrim’s “mid-century but not historic” homes make semi-custom the best blend of value and fit for most.

Code requirements for Antrim kitchens

In Antrim and the rest of Franklin County, kitchen work that is purely cosmetic—such as painting cabinets, swapping out hardware, or even refacing—typically doesn’t require a building permit. You can also usually replace countertops in place without one. However, if your renovation involves new or relocated electrical circuits, changing plumbing locations, removing or building out walls, or adding new mechanical venting, you will almost certainly trigger a permit requirement, especially within borough limits where plan review is common for remodels involving system changes.

In this part of Pennsylvania, each township runs its own code enforcement, so it’s smart to check with your office if you are unsure what triggers a permit. Electrical inspections in many towns—including those around Antrim—are handled by third-party agencies such as Approved Code Services or Commonwealth Code Inspection Service, not by the township directly. This sometimes comes as a surprise, so confirm your project’s inspection process early on.

Your contractor must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for jobs over $500, and all employers are required to carry workers’ compensation, which you can verify via WCAIS.

If you skip the permit on work that actually requires it, you risk long delays and extra cost at resale—uninspected renovations mean deals fall through or you pay for retroactive approval or even removal. Get it right up front.

Planning appliances before cabinets

In a kitchen renovation, cabinets literally wrap around your appliances. If you select appliances after the cabinets are ordered, it’s easy to end up with a fridge that sticks out or a range that does not fit the slot. In Antrim, make all appliance choices first or at least nail down the model dimensions before designing cabinets — especially for counter-depth refrigerators (which sit flush and look built-in, but cost more) versus standard-depth (which protrude), and slide-in versus freestanding ranges (which affect countertop cuts and cabinet heights).

If you plan for gas, induction, or electric, know that induction and today’s high-powered electric ranges may need a bigger circuit than what’s in most post‑1960 Franklin County homes. Panel-ready dishwashers or refrigerators can be an upgrade, but require careful cabinet sizing; always confirm actual model specs up front.

Proper range hood venting, ideally ducted to the outside, is where many owners pay the price for a hasty plan—recirculating hoods are legal but less effective. If you dream of pro-grade appliances, be aware that most of the cost premium is design and feel rather than real-world performance, except for serious cooks—don’t overspend there unless the kitchen demands it. Right now, certain appliance models have double the lead time of cabinets, so order early.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Antrim

Does a kitchen renovation increase home value in Antrim? A kitchen renovation can increase your home value in Antrim, especially when the work matches the quality level and layout expectations of buyers looking for post-1960 homes. Since most homes here are owner-occupied and larger, mid-life updates along US‑11 and I‑81 are common selling points. However, a full gut remodel in the typical range of $30,500–$122,000 won’t always dollar-for-dollar translate to value at resale. The best return tends to come from “pull and replace” projects that update surfaces and cabinetry but retain the existing, functional layout. Overspending on luxury finishes or rearranging the entire layout makes sense mostly if you plan to stay long-term or demand specific features, not if you’re renovating to sell within a couple years.
What is a realistic contingency for a Antrim kitchen? In Antrim, setting aside a contingency of 10–15% of your total contract price is smart, particularly if your home is among the 52.1% of local homes built before 1980. Unforeseen issues like old wiring, uneven subfloors, or minor structural fixes crop up more often than you’d expect. For a gut remodel budgeted at $66,000, this means a contingency of about $6,600–$9,900. If you’re only doing surface updates (refacing or new counters), you may be able to get by with 5–10%. Don’t let a contractor talk you into skipping contingency; surprises are what stalled projects in Franklin County look like.
Can I renovate a kitchen in stages? Yes, you can renovate a kitchen in stages in Antrim, and many homeowners do—especially given travel surcharges for small jobs in Franklin County. Common phased approaches are flooring first (if it’s running under cabinets), then appliances and lighting, and finally cabinets and counters. However, you need to plan every phase before starting, or you'll risk paying extra for rework. For example, installing new flooring around old cabinets can mean double labor fees later. Cabinet refacing at $7,100–$20,500 can be an intermediate step if your boxes are solid, buying time before a full remodel.
Are Antrim kitchens typically small galley layouts? Not in most cases. While homes built before 1960 in Franklin County sometimes have smaller, galley-style kitchens, the bulk of Antrim’s housing arose in the later decades, producing more open, suburban layouts. That said, some kitchen footprints here aren’t as wide open as in newly built homes, so remodels frequently involve working around a 1980s-1990s “box” or L-shaped plan. Major wall moves—costing $66,000–$122,000—are less common except when homeowners want to dramatically open up spaces to match modern trends.
What is the difference between stock and semi-custom cabinets? Stock cabinets are mass-produced and come in set sizes, which can mean using fillers to make them fit your Antrim kitchen. They work well if your walls are straight and you’re on a tight budget—stock replacement in Franklin County runs $7,100–$30,500. Semi-custom lets you adjust some dimensions and pick from more finishes, which is important here because post‑1960 homes often have walls a bit out-of-square. The upgrade makes for a more fitted look and efficient storage, but costs more and has a longer lead time.
Who inspects kitchen electrical work in Antrim? In and around Antrim, electrical inspections are often handled by third-party inspection agencies such as Approved Code Services or Commonwealth Code Inspection Service, rather than the township’s own staff. This is the case across much of Franklin County and can catch homeowners off-guard during scheduling. If your kitchen remodel involves new or moved electrical circuits—which is nearly always the case once you hit the $66,000 gut-and-layout-change mark—you’ll want to confirm who inspects early to avoid permit or scheduling hiccups at the end of the job.
How much more does moving the sink and range cost? In Antrim, moving the sink or cooktop is what tips a “pull and replace” or “gut, same layout” job ($30,500–$66,000) into full layout-change territory ($66,000–$122,000). Moving plumbing or gas lines doesn’t just mean new pipes—it triggers permit and inspection requirements and may force upgrades to underlying systems. If the budget is tight, keeping core services in place saves tens of thousands and keeps the job in a faster, simpler permit class.
What are cabinet lead times in Antrim right now? Cabinet lead times in Antrim have partly stabilized, but for semi-custom or custom work, expect at least 6–12 weeks from order to delivery—longer if you choose painted finishes or unusual sizes common in post‑1960 housing stock. Stock cabinets are sometimes available in two weeks (if locally warehoused), but that’s the exception, not the rule. For a typical project—budgeting $20,500 for semi-custom—expect to order before demo starts and confirm all appliance specs for proper fit. Do not rush selections or pick something inferior for speed; a delay now is always cheaper than a replacement later.
Should I hire a general contractor or a kitchen firm? In Antrim and across Franklin County, the best fit comes down to your expectations and involvement. A kitchen design-build firm bundles everything—design, materials, and trades—into one contract, costs more (though not always by much), and reduces homeowner hassle, especially helpful when labor availability is as thin as it is here. If you want full say over design and don’t mind keeping tabs on trades, a general contractor will save some money and work well for straightforward scopes (think $30,500–$66,000 gut-and-replace jobs). Attempting an owner-managed project is only smart if you’re retired or work flexible hours—delays from sub scheduling can easily wipe out your savings here.
