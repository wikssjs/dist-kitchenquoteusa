Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, the renovation rules matter because kitchen projects frequently touch both electrical and plumbing. For work over $500, contractors performing home improvements must be properly registered under the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) program (verify via hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For any kitchen electrical work—such as adding a circuit, modifying outlets, or connecting new lighting—a licensed PA electrician must install or modify circuits (confirm licensing through dli.pa.gov). Plumbing work, including sink and dishwasher hookups and any relocation, must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits and inspections also come into play depending on scope. In most PA municipalities, kitchen renovations that involve plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal typically require building permits and inspections. Cosmetic work—like paint, new hardware, or cabinet refacing—typically does not require a permit, but it still must be done to code where it intersects electrical fixtures or venting.

Lead paint is a major planning issue in Washington for homes built before 1978. If your kitchen surfaces are disturbed during a gut remodel, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required for contractors disturbing lead-containing materials. Before hiring, verify certification status using the EPA’s certified renovator search (certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov).

Finally, the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires written contracts for home improvement work over $500, including a 3-day right of cancellation—ask for it in writing.

Step-by-step verification: (1) check HIC registration online; (2) request certificates of insurance showing general liability and workers’ compensation; (3) confirm the electrician and plumber are properly licensed and insured; (4) review the contract scope and include permit responsibility; (5) ensure proof of coverage is provided before work begins.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Washington?

With 66.6% of Washington’s housing stock built before 1980, many Westmoreland County homeowners have kitchens that are structurally workable but visually dated. Cabinet refacing—replacing door and drawer fronts and applying veneer while keeping the existing cabinet boxes—is a popular middle option because it can cost roughly 40–60% less than replacement when the boxes are still straight and solid. That’s often the sweet spot in Washington when you want a cleaner look, modern hardware, and a refreshed finish without tearing out everything behind the doors.

Refacing is only cost-effective when the existing boxes are square, secure, and in acceptable interior condition. Older kitchens may have boxes that are out of level, have water damage, or don’t sit true against uneven walls. If your remodel goal includes wider drawers, a different drawer stack configuration, or fixing a cramped layout, replacement is usually the better long-term move because new boxes can be designed around today’s dimensions and cabinet planning.

Here’s a concrete example: if your budget targets a refresh and your layout is already functional, refacing at $2,500–$8,000 may be justified to achieve a modern door style without paying for full removal and rebuild. But if you’re also planning a full kitchen gut within the $35,000–$95,000 band (new countertops, flooring, and electrical/lighting upgrades), cabinet replacement can be a more efficient use of your disruption—because you’re already coordinating trades and inspections.

In older Washington homes, I also look closely at how the uppers and lowers are attached to framing. If fasteners are corroded or the box condition is marginal, refacing can “look good at first” but fail sooner—making replacement the safer investment.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Washington

Choosing the right kitchen renovation contractor in Washington comes down to verification, clarity, and risk control. Start by confirming the contractor’s credentials. For licensing, verify the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration online (hic.attorneygeneral.gov) if your job scope meets the threshold for registration. For trade work, verify a PA-licensed electrician via the DLI licensing search (dli.pa.gov) and ensure plumbing is performed by a PA-licensed plumber. If the project could involve lead paint disturbance in older homes, ask whether they follow EPA Lead-Safe RRP requirements and confirm any relevant certification details.

Then request 2–3 itemized written quotes. You want labor and materials broken out—especially for cabinets, countertops, electrical fixtures, plumbing parts, and flooring underlayment/prep—rather than a single lump sum. Read the scope line-by-line for exclusions (for example, “electrical permitting fees,” “damages due to concealed conditions,” cabinet installation tolerances, and how the contractor handles out-of-square walls). Also confirm whether insurance claim documentation is supported if you’re renovating after a covered loss.

Warranty should be in writing: workmanship warranty length and what’s covered for equipment and materials (cabinets, countertops, flooring, and appliances). Payment schedule matters—never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and use a holdback tied to punch-list completion. Finally, get an arrival estimate and a completion window in writing so you can coordinate with appliance delivery and inspections.

  • Ask for proof of HIC registration and review it before the contract is signed.
  • Verify the electrician’s PA license and require the license number on the paperwork.
  • Confirm plumbing supervision by a PA-licensed plumber.
  • Request certificates of insurance: general liability and workers’ compensation.
  • Get quotes that list labor vs. materials for cabinets, counters, flooring, and electrical/plumbing.
  • Make sure the scope states who pulls permits (and who pays permit fees).
  • Confirm whether demolition includes floor/trim removal and how debris hauling is handled.
  • Ask how concealed conditions are handled (water damage, knob-and-tube remnants, old venting).
  • Require a finish plan for cabinets and countertops (timelines for templating and ordering).
  • Verify lead paint procedures if the home is pre-1978 and surfaces will be disturbed.
  • Review warranty terms and get them in writing before scheduling.
  • Lock a payment schedule with a holdback until final cleanup and punch-list completion.

Red flags I see too often in Washington-area kitchen projects include: contractors who won’t provide itemized quotes, a contract with vague scope (“includes all work”), refusal to provide proof of insurance/workers’ compensation, promises of unusually fast cabinet timelines without mentioning lead times, and no clear explanation of permits when you’re moving plumbing or adding new circuits.

What are the signs I need a full kitchen gut remodel?
