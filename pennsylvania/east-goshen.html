Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, a legitimate kitchen renovation contractor must hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for jobs over $500 (check hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For electrical work, a licensed PA electrician must install or modify circuits—verify credentials through dli.pa.gov. Plumbing changes—moving a sink, adding a dishwasher hookup, or relocating shutoff valves—must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

Permits depend on scope. In many PA municipalities, kitchen renovations that involve plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal typically require building permits and inspections. Cosmetic work—like paint, hardware swaps, and cabinet refacing where no systems are altered—often does not require a permit. For pre-1978 East Goshen homes, be mindful of lead paint: if a contractor will disturb lead-painted surfaces, EPA Lead-Safe RRP certification is required for contractors performing that work. You can confirm certification status at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring for any gut remodel that may disturb older kitchen finishes.

Under the PA Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, you should receive a written contract for all home improvement work over $500, including your 3-day right of cancellation. As you screen bids, verify insurance and coverage step-by-step: confirm their HIC registration, request a current certificate of insurance naming you as additionally insured if applicable, and ask for proof of workers’ compensation coverage (not just general liability). For each, have the documents in writing before work begins.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in East Goshen?

With about 43.0% of East Goshen’s housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many kitchens are “old but not broken.” The cabinet boxes are often structurally sound—still level, still attached well—but the doors, drawer fronts, and finish feel dated. That’s why cabinet refacing is a common middle ground: it replaces only door and drawer fronts (and veneer), keeping the existing box structure. In practice, refacing usually costs 40–60% less than full replacement because demolition is minimal and new cabinet frames aren’t installed.

Refacing only makes sense when the existing boxes are in acceptable interior condition. If you find water damage, warped sides, failing drawer guides, or boxes that are out of square, replacement may be the safer route. East Goshen homes also often have older countertop substrates and under-sink plumbing conditions; if we’re already rebuilding around leaks, the “keep the box” assumption can break.

For a concrete budget example: if you currently have an older kitchen and you want a fresh look with quartz—your cabinet choice may be the difference between staying around $3,500–$18,000 for refacing versus moving into $18,000–$65,000 for stock replacement. If the boxes are straight, solid, and functionally fine, that refacing savings can be justified because your money is better spent on countertops and upgraded electrical. If the boxes show repeated moisture intrusion near the sink or dishwasher, replacing becomes cost-effective because you avoid redoing the same labor later.
