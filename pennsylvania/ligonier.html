Why the contingency exists

Ligonier’s kitchens are mostly in homes built before 1980 (71.4% per U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), and that means opening up the walls is a gamble. The typical surprises—and what they cost to address—include:

  • Out-of-plumb walls and uneven floors: Shimming cabinets or self-leveling floors often means several hundred to several thousand dollars depending on severity.
  • Old wiring: 1960s–1970s houses often have undersized, ungrounded, or minimal circuits. Upgrading to modern code for a kitchen can run from a few hundred up to a few thousand dollars, especially if adding AFCI/GFCI protection or new circuits.
  • Galvanized supply lines or cast iron drains: If you find these, replacement as part of the remodel is almost always a smart move, with costs scaling by kitchen size and accessibility, commonly $1,000–$3,500 extra.
  • No insulation on exterior walls: Retrofits cost $400–$1,000, but not doing it means a cold kitchen.
  • Previous unpermitted work: Fixing hidden shortcuts can mean anything from patching to full replacement, and is hard to price before demolition.
  • Lead paint and asbestos flooring (pre-1978): Federal EPA Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rules apply. Professional testing is required before disturbance, and remediation can add $500–$2,500 depending on extent.

Newer homes in the area aren’t immune. Builder-grade kitchens from the 1980s onward often skimped with particleboard cabinets or minimal electrical service. These reach end-of-life at the same time, and that can push you into a more involved renovation even if the layout is modern enough.

Code requirements for Ligonier kitchens

In Ligonier and the rest of Westmoreland County, kitchen projects fall under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), based on the IRC and enforced at the municipal level. Standard cosmetic upgrades—like painting, hardware, refacing, or swapping in a new countertop that matches the old one—generally do not need a permit. But if your project moves or adds electrical circuits, relocates plumbing, or alters any walls or structure, you can expect to need a permit. In Greensburg, specifically, moving plumbing lines or removing a structural wall during a kitchen remodel will trigger a building permit and likely a formal plan review. In Hempfield Township, replacing cabinets—if you’re not moving appliances—typically does not.

Third-party agencies such as Code.sys are commonly used for electrical inspections in both Greensburg and Hempfield, so don’t count on a municipal inspector every time. Inspections must be scheduled before the walls are closed up, and final electrical sign-off is a separate step.

Anyone signing a contract over $500 must be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) and must carry workers’ compensation insurance if they have employees, which you can verify in WCAIS.

Unpermitted work catches up to sellers at resale time. Appraisers and buyers do look for permits—and lack of one can mean delay or price reduction.

Four kinds of kitchen project, four budgets

In Ligonier, the vague phrase “kitchen remodel” covers a price range from finishing touches to full rebuild. Break it into four tiers so you know what you’re asking:

  • Cosmetic refresh: new paint, cabinet hardware, lighting, maybe a backsplash—no cabinets or layout changes. Fast, inexpensive, and no permit triggered.
  • Pull and replace: new cabinets and counters, same layout and footprint. Plumbing and electrical stay put. Minimal disruption, often no permit needed, unless you upgrade electrical or make structural changes. This is where most “cabinet replacement” numbers ($6,700–$28,500) land.
  • Gut, same layout: everything down to the studs, with updated plumbing, electrical, insulation, but keeping everything in place. Full replacement but not reimagined—the costs are higher ($28,500–$115,000) because of the mechanicals and finishes.
  • Layout change: moving the sink, opening walls, rearranging floor plan. This is where the budget jumps—permits, engineering, possibly relocation of services—rarely under $62,000 and going up from there.

The steepest cost jump is from gutting in place to changing the layout. The crossover is when you move plumbing or electrical: that’s a bigger crew, more inspections, and different timelines. Decide your project tier before calling contractors—no pro can give you a meaningful number without knowing which category you mean, and “just a few changes” puts you in a new budget bracket.

Renovating to live in versus renovating to sell

A new kitchen helps a home sell faster in Ligonier, but it’s not a magic investment. The reality is, if your goal is to move within a few years, a big spend is not coming back dollar for dollar. Data shows mid-range renovations recoup a far greater share of their cost at sale than top-shelf ones, especially in Ligonier, where the median household income is $68,125 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). Over-improving—adding features or spending well above the typical range ($62,000 and above)—rarely pays off, unless you’re planning to stay and enjoy it for a decade.

If you’re renovating to sell, aim for updated but neutral finishes, mid-range cabinetry and counters, and avoid moving the layout. The goal is to remove objections for buyers, not to build your dream. If you’re renovating to live in, the calculation changes—spend on what matters to you: storage, lighting, a layout that works for your cooking, even if it’s not a sure resale winner. Don’t let resale math stop you from enjoying your home, but do know where the market lines are in Ligonier’s price bands.

Planning appliances before cabinets

Avoid the sequencing trap—appliance dimensions dictate cabinet layout, not the other way around. If you pick appliances after cabinet plans are set, you’re at the mercy of what fits, not what you want. For example, standard refrigerators are deeper than counter-depth, which means the wrong choice can leave you with a fridge sticking out past the new cabinetry. Ranges come as freestanding or slide-in; slide-ins need different counter treatments. Gas and induction cooktops require not just a gas or electrical line, but also enough amps; you may need to plan for a dedicated 40-amp circuit if you want induction. Panel-ready dishwashers need custom cabinet panels sized correctly, and cannot simply be swapped in later.

Range hood vents should ideally duct to the outside; recirculating hoods fit more situations but won’t actually clear out smoke or humidity after a cooking marathon. Upgrading to a ducted hood requires planning for wall or roof penetration before cabinet installation, not after.

Professional-grade appliances do give better temperature control and heavier hardware, but above all you are paying for looks and brand. Unless cooking is a hobby, most families are well served by mid-range lines.

Finally, double-check lead times: Some high-end appliances are backordered months, longer than it takes to fabricate cabinets, and scheduling around them can be the hardest part.

Allowances, exclusions and change orders

Comparing kitchen remodel bids is more complicated than lining up bottom lines, because much of the total gets carried as an "allowance." An allowance is a placeholder—a rough dollar figure for an item you haven’t selected yet (like tile, lighting, or appliances). One contractor might plug in $8,000 for cabinets, another $15,000, and it’s easy to mistake lower allowances for real savings. Always compare the allowance lines first: If a “tile” row reads $500 in one bid and $1,500 in another, you know most of the difference is just what’s set aside, not actual cost cutting.

A strong quote will call out the cabinet brand and construction in detail; slab descriptions should list countertop material (quartz, granite), thickness, finished edge profiles, and how seams are handled. Good contracts also spell out what happens with surprises in the walls: Are you billed time and materials, a set rate, or must you approve a written change order first? Payments should be linked to clear milestones and come with lien waivers from subcontractors so you’re protected against later claims. Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a written contract on jobs over $500, and gives you a three-day window to cancel with no penalty.
