Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, kitchen renovations are regulated in ways that matter for both cost and scheduling. If your project involves new electrical work—new circuits, modifications, or panel-related changes—a licensed PA electrician must perform the work. To verify contractor credentials, you can check licensing through the Pennsylvania Department of Labor and Industry (DLI) at dli.pa.gov. For plumbing (sink hookups, dishwasher connections, and any relocation of plumbing), plumbing work must be performed by or under the supervision of a PA-licensed plumber.

For general contracting, Pennsylvania requires Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for qualifying home improvement work over $500. Verify HIC status using the Attorney General’s HIC resources at hic.attorneygeneral.gov.

Permits and inspections are commonly required when your kitchen project includes plumbing rerouting, new electrical circuits, or wall removal/structural work. Cosmetic work—such as paint, hardware, or cabinet refacing—typically does not require permits. However, always confirm based on your exact scope and what your municipality enforces.

Lead safety is a major factor in Swarthmore’s older housing. Many homes built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint in kitchen areas. Contractors disturbing lead paint surfaces are required to be EPA-certified under the Lead-Safe RRP program. You can verify certification at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov before hiring for gut remodels.

Finally, under the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, contracts for home improvement work over $500 must be in writing and include a 3-day right of cancellation. Ask for the contract in writing and keep it with your paperwork.

Step-by-step verification for Swarthmore homeowners: (1) confirm the contractor’s HIC registration; (2) obtain the electrician’s and plumber’s PA license details for the specific trades working in your kitchen; (3) request certificates of insurance—general liability and workers’ compensation (WC)—and verify coverage dates and project name/addresses where applicable; and (4) verify Lead-Safe RRP certification when there’s any likelihood of disturbing painted surfaces in pre-1978 cabinets, trim, or walls.

Cabinet refacing vs. replacement — what makes sense in Swarthmore?

With 96.9% of Swarthmore’s housing stock built before 1980, many kitchens are 30–50 years old. That’s a common scenario in Delaware County: the cabinet boxes are often structurally solid, but the door style, finish, and hardware look dated, and the interior storage doesn’t match how today’s families work. Cabinet refacing is the middle ground—replacing only the door and drawer fronts and adding veneer while keeping the original boxes. In many cases, it costs 40–60% less than full replacement and can deliver a noticeable visual upgrade without gutting everything.

Refacing is cost-effective only when the existing cabinet boxes are square, solid, and in acceptable interior condition. If boxes are sagging, warped from moisture, out of level, or damaged by past leaks, refacing won’t “fix” the underlying structure—those issues show up as misaligned doors and poor closure. Replacement, on the other hand, lets you reset dimensions, use new layout planning, and correct foundation or subfloor inconsistencies that older homes sometimes have.

Here’s a specific money example. If your kitchen needs new counters and refreshed cabinets but you’re keeping the same layout, cabinet refacing at $2,500–$8,000 may be a smart move—especially when you’re trying to stay closer to the lower end of a broader renovation. But if you’re already budgeting for a full gut in the $45,000–$150,000 range and you know your boxes are compromised, spending toward replacement (often $8,000–$45,000 for stock/semi-custom) can prevent repeated adjustment and future door problems. In older Swarthmore kitchens, we typically use a quick level-and-sound assessment during demolition prep to decide whether refacing is truly worth it.

How to choose a kitchen renovation contractor in Swarthmore

Choosing the right contractor in Swarthmore means verifying credentials and reading the contract like a homeowner. Start with licensing: confirm the company’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for qualifying work over $500 using hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For electrical work, verify the specific electrician’s PA license at dli.pa.gov. For plumbing, ask for proof of the PA-licensed plumber supervising or performing the work.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes. Avoid “lump sum” estimates that don’t clearly separate labor, demolition, carpentry, electrical, plumbing, and material selections. A credible quote will show what’s included (and what isn’t), including countertop install details, appliance hookups, and flooring underlayment/prep work.

Read scope carefully for exclusions such as permit fees, disposal/dumpster costs, wall patching/painting, and any allowance items (like laminate vs quartz). Ask whether the quote includes documentation for insurance claims, if that’s relevant. Confirm warranty terms: workmanship warranty length, plus equipment/material warranties for cabinets, countertops, and appliances. For payment schedule, never agree to pay more than 10–15% upfront; use a milestone-based schedule and hold back a portion until punch list items are complete. Finally, insist on a written arrival estimate and completion window, not just “start date and hope.” Lead times for cabinets can affect your timeline, so ask what happens if materials arrive late.
