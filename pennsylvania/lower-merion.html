Allowances, exclusions and change orders

Allowances are the single biggest reason kitchen remodel quotes in Lower Merion can’t be compared by total price. An allowance is a placeholder—a budget plug for an item not yet chosen, like backsplash tile or light fixtures. If one bid carries a tile allowance at $3,000 and another at $400, what looks like a competitive quote can jump thousands once final selections are made. Always compare allowance lines before you ever look at bottom-line numbers.

The details matter in a kitchen bid. Cabinets should be specified by brand and series—never just “premium cabinets.” Countertops need the material, thickness, edges, and seam layout. Allowances let the project start before final choices, but vague allowances mean trouble. If you hit an unforeseen condition—say, water-damaged subfloor—the contract should spell out how extra work and costs are approved. Change orders (additions to scope or materials) should always require your written approval before work proceeds. A reputable contractor ties payment to milestones (like cabinet delivery or countertop install) instead of fixed dates, and should provide lien waivers from major subcontractors as you pay. Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a written contract for projects over $500 and gives you three business days to cancel after signing.

The real timeline for a Lower Merion kitchen

Many Lower Merion renovations run weeks behind because of timeline expectations set early, not because of slow trades. Here’s the ground truth: the longest lead time in most kitchens is cabinetry. After design and measurements, you select, order, and wait—sometimes 8 to 16 weeks, as this area’s contractor and supplier market in Greater Philadelphia is busy year-round. Ordering after demolition is a rookie mistake that leaves your kitchen out of commission longer than necessary.

The build sequence isn’t arbitrary. First comes design, then site measure, then cabinet ordering. Once cabinets are committed, demolition of the old space happens. Afterward, trades do electrical and plumbing rough-in (which has to pass inspection before anything covers it), then install drywall. Cabinets go in next. Only after cabinets are anchored can the countertop fabricator measure (template) for stone or quartz—there’s no shortcut, no matter what anyone promises. Expect a natural “dead spot” of a week or more while countertops are cut and prepped. Flooring, backsplash tile, and trims finish out the job.

If you live in one of Lower Merion’s classic postwar houses, the sequencing gets even more crucial because of potential surprises inside the walls. Planning around these bottlenecks—especially cabinets and counters—means less purgatory without a functioning kitchen.

Why the electrical line grew

For homes in Lower Merion—especially those built before 1980, which account for 81.7% of the township’s stock (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024)—kitchen rewiring is often the most expensive surprise. Current Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC) expects every kitchen remodel to bring electrical up to present safety and capacity standards, with multiple dedicated small appliance circuits, GFCI protection at all countertop outlets, dedicated circuits for dishwasher, disposal, and microwave, plus 240-volt service for an electric range.

In a typical mid-century Abington or Upper Dublin tract house, you’re likely to find a single ungrounded circuit doing duty for all outlets and lights, maybe aluminum branch wiring, and a main panel already full. Once walls are open, code compliance is not optional — inspectors and reputable contractors have no leeway to “grandfather” unsafe wiring. Even late-1990s layouts often lack enough circuits. At minimum, you should budget $4,900–$19,500 for core kitchen electrical, depending on whether new circuits, a subpanel, or full rewiring are required. Skimping here is unsafe, and shortcuts will haunt you at resale and with insurance.

Practical survival during the work

Living through a kitchen renovation in Lower Merion takes planning and flexibility, especially when the project stretches past a month. Set up a temporary kitchen before demolition — most people move the fridge to a dining room or hallway, assign a folding table for a microwave and coffee maker, and use a bathroom or laundry sink for dishwashing. Paper plates and microwavable meals become the default, and your food bill will go up. It’s realistic to spend as much eating out as you will on new flooring — plan for an extra $6,100–$24,500 over an eight-week project if you can’t cook at home.

Dust control is non-negotiable. Request plastic sheeting and zipper walls to keep renovation mess from migrating beyond the kitchen. Think about where the contractor’s dumpster and material deliveries will stage — in Lower Merion neighborhoods, tight driveways mean planning with neighbors. Pets need a safe, isolated space as workers come and go with the door open. If your project involves major demolition, consider moving out for that week; noise, dust, and the lack of running water make it nearly unlivable.

What requires a permit in a kitchen renovation

In Lower Merion Township, moving a wall or changing the kitchen layout—to relocate plumbing or electric—demands a building permit and plan review. Cosmetic jobs like repainting, installing a new countertop in place, refacing cabinets, or swapping out appliances without layout changes typically do not trigger permits. However, adding new circuits, relocating plumbing, structural wall removal, or significant vent and hood work will require official approvals.

Inspections for permitted work will involve municipal code officials and, for electrical, either the Lower Merion code office or (in many nearby townships) an approved third-party inspection agency. This surprises many homeowners used to direct municipal inspectors in Norristown.

Contractor registration is mandatory for work valued above $500—verify through the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. All contractors with employees must also carry workers’ compensation insurance, verifiable via WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Unpermitted work is frequently flagged during resale, and discovery can mean fixing or removing upgrades after you’ve already paid for them.

What's behind the walls in a Lower Merion kitchen

With 81.7% of Lower Merion homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), most kitchen remodels here aren’t just about pretty finishes—they’re about dealing with old bones. Once demolition starts, it’s common to uncover out-of-plumb walls and sloping floors, which mean shimming cabinets for days and extra labor (expect $1,200–$4,000 for proper leveling). Wiring often turns out to be original—ungrounded, undersized, or patched together from previous unpermitted work. Full rewiring can add another $4,900–$19,500. Plumbing surprises mean galvanized pipes due for replacement, cast iron drains corroded at the joints, or supply lines too narrow for new fixtures.

Exterior walls rarely have insulation. If your kitchen is on the north side, adding insulation during renovation is a worthwhile upgrade for modest extra cost. You may also find asbestos flooring or lead paint if the house predates 1978. For these, the EPA Lead RRP Rule applies—any disturbance requires contractors certified in lead-safe practices. Testing always comes first; safe remediation is required.

If your home is among the newer tract stock, expect fewer hazardous materials but more builder-grade shortcuts: particleboard cabinet boxes sagging after decades of service, minimal circuit counts, and laminate counters of the same era reaching end-of-life together.

Frequently asked questions — kitchen renovation in Lower Merion

Is lead paint a concern in Lower Merion kitchen renovations? Lead paint is a common issue for most of Lower Merion’s housing stock, with more than 80% built before 1980. Any kitchen built prior to 1978 is subject to the federal EPA Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule, which means certified contractors must test, contain, and safely remove or encapsulate lead-painted surfaces before work begins. If you’re opening up walls or disturbing painted surfaces in an older home, factor in both the cost and the time required for lead-safe work. This can add days to the schedule and anywhere from $400–$2,000 depending on the amount of lead paint that must be handled. Even for refacing or minor plumbing, always ask your contractor if they follow RRP. It’s not just about passing inspection—it’s about the health of your household, especially if there are kids present.
Is induction worth it over gas? In Lower Merion, switching to induction is becoming more common with kitchen remodels, particularly as many of the post-1990s homes already have the 240-volt electrical capacity necessary for these ranges. Induction cooktops are fast, safe, and easy to clean, and don’t require combustion inside your home—an advantage in a market prone to humid summers and occasional air quality restrictions. With current energy prices, induction cooking can actually be more efficient than gas. However, it may require upgrading your electrical panel, which can easily add $1,600–$4,900 to your project. Ultimately, induction is worth considering for its performance and air quality, but if you love to cook with a wok or already have a new gas line, the benefit is less clear. Make your choice based on how you cook and your willingness to invest in possible panel upgrades.
Is a cosmetic refresh enough or do I need a gut remodel? In Lower Merion, whether you go for a cosmetic refresh—paint, hardware, counters—or a full gut remodel depends on your goals and the age of your home’s infrastructure. Many houses here still have the original 1950s–70s layouts, cabinets, and two-prong electrical. If your cabinets are sturdy and the layout works, a cosmetic update costing $8,500–$36,500 can yield a big visual impact without tearing the kitchen apart. If you’re facing persistent leaks, failing wiring, or a dysfunctional footprint, a gut job becomes the smarter choice—especially for homes built before energy-efficient standards. Gut remodels (starting at $36,500) let you address plumbing, wiring, and insulation all at once. Ask yourself: is the kitchen just tired, or is it failing behind the walls?
How much more does moving the sink and range cost? Moving plumbing or gas lines for the sink or range in a Lower Merion kitchen can quickly shift your project from a finish job into a full remodel. Not only do you open up the floors and walls—triggering permit and inspection requirements—but you also run new supply, waste, and vent lines. Expect to add $7,000–$16,500 to your job for shifting the sink or range, especially if your home still has galvanized or cast iron pipes that must be brought up to standard. Each move often means rerouting electrical as well, so the budget impact nearly always stretches into five digits.
What adds the most resale value in a kitchen? In Lower Merion, where home values and owner-occupancy are both high, projects that combine updated cabinetry, energy-efficient appliances, and attractive solid-surface countertops resonate the most with buyers. Full gut remodels can pay off, but you rarely get every dollar back unless the previous kitchen was really dysfunctional. The sweet spot is a thoughtful modernization—think semi-custom cabinets, stone or quartz counters, improved lighting—for $36,500–$79,000. Over-improving with ultra-high-end finishes can outprice the neighborhood. Focus on functional layout, durable materials, and visual impact. When in doubt, ask a realtor what buyers remark on first when they tour homes in your area. Usually, it’s cabinetry and counters.
Why is there a gap after cabinets are installed? The weeklong gap after your cabinets are installed—and before your countertops go in—is a common surprise in Lower Merion kitchen renovations. Countertop fabricators cannot do final measurements (“templating”) for quartz or stone until the cabinets are fully installed, leveled, and anchored. Once they measure, you’re looking at 7–14 days for cutting, fabrication, and delivery during busy periods—a real factor in the Greater Philadelphia market. That dead time is not poor scheduling; it’s a reality of the trade. No reputable shop will cut stone or high-end surfaces from a drawing. For a typical job, this hidden week is more than just a minor inconvenience—it turns your kitchen from “almost done” to “still waiting.” It’s something to plan for, and it’s included in all realistic project timelines and budgets, whether you’re investing $8,500 or north of $79,000.
How much do new kitchen cabinets cost in Lower Merion? For a Lower Merion home, replacing kitchen cabinets with stock to semi-custom selections will generally cost $8,500–$36,500, with most typical projects landing around $24,500. These numbers reflect labor, tear-out, and proper installation—not just the boxes themselves. The cost is higher in this area than in rural Pennsylvania because of labor demand and the size/complexity of existing kitchens. Fully custom options will push the high end (or exceed it), while competitive material-only online cabinet sources may let you approach the lower band, but you’ll still pay for professional carpentry. Be cautious about going too cheap; poorly installed or low-grade cabinets are the fastest way to lose value in a resale-driven market.
What is a change order and how should it work? A change order is a written adjustment to your original contract, required whenever the scope of work, finish selection, or site condition changes in the middle of a project. In Lower Merion, reputable contractors will not proceed with changes—whether it’s adding a pantry or upgrading to a premium stone countertop—unless you have signed a detailed, priced change order. This protects you from surprise invoices and ensures each extra is agreed to, not “assumed.” Pricing is always based on the current market and should include both material and labor costs; for example, adding a backsplash tile with a material upcharge may run you another $2,000–$6,000 depending on the tile selected and wall area. Never accept a “verbal OK”—always require the change to be put in writing, with a new timeline and payment milestone if appropriate.
Do I need a permit for a kitchen renovation in Lower Merion? In Lower Merion, any kitchen renovation that changes walls, plumbing, electrical circuits, or the basic footprint absolutely requires a building permit and plan review. Swapping appliances or doing a basic cabinet refacing does not. Home sale transactions in this township are more likely to flag unpermitted work, especially for kitchen remodels, and buyers may ask for full demolition or costly corrections before closing. Contractors for work over $500 must be registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General, and all must provide evidence of workers’ compensation if they have employees. For full transparency and resale value, insist on permitted work—never rely on “nobody will notice,” because in Lower Merion, they often do.
